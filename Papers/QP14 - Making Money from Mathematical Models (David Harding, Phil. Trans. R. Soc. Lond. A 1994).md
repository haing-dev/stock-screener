# Making Money from Mathematical Models (David Harding, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. Lond. A 1994)

**Authors:** David Harding (Adam, Harding and Lueck Ltd)

**Summary:** Question: do mathematical models in finance have enough empirical grounding, and how should one test them. Method: philosophical essay on scientific method, Adam Smith and efficient market theory (OCR of 1994 Phil. Trans. R. Soc. paper, no data). Conclusion: models must be falsifiable and tested by trading against the market; speculation based on empirical observation is the productive path.

## Argument map

**Question:** What is the scientific status of mathematical models in finance (especially efficient market theory), and how should their usefulness be judged?

**Thesis:** Financial models should be judged like natural-science models by the falsifiable predictions they make, and the most honest test is trading profit against the market; by that standard efficient market theory has limited scientific utility, and empirical speculation is the intellectual front line. ### Forward-looking inputs (reader synthesis)
(reader) No specific inputs. The method says any signal should be a falsifiable prediction against market-implied expectations, validated on data after the model was frozen; signals grounded in stable human behaviour should last longer than those grounded in transient opinions.

### Reader inferences (not stated by the authors)
- (reader) The "judge models developed some time ago without prior knowledge" principle is the case for a frozen-rules holdout test.
- (reader) The Burke quote suggests behavioural (human-nature) anomalies such as trend are more durable than opinion-driven ones.
- (reader) Profit as test needs a significance standard to separate skill from luck, which the paper does not provide.

## Claims

### C02 (page 2)
- Paper's aim: examine whether mathematical models in finance have enough empirical grounding to support useful inference, and warn that models lacking such grounding spread erroneous conclusions; suggests how to enrich empirical grounding. _(mechanism, premise)_ #mech/research_method
  > of the sufficiency of the empirical grounding of such models to the task of drawing useful inferences about such a reality; and of the danger of unwittingly propagating erroneous conclusions about this reality in the absence of such a grounding.
  ^QP14-C02-1
- A model can never be the reality, only a simulation of it; even a model matching all known observations cannot be called true because the universe of observations keeps expanding. _(mechanism, premise)_ #mech/research_method
  > the theory cannot be the reality ; it can at best only be a near perfect simulation of that reality.
  ^QP14-C02-2
- Models must make empirically falsifiable predictions testable without bias; a theory's utility is the quality of its predictions and their accord with observation. _(mechanism, premise)_ #mech/research_method
  > such a model must be capable of producing empirically falsifiable predictions and must thus be capable of being subjected to an unbiased test of its value within its own conceptual framework.
  exact_rule: accept a model only if it yields falsifiable predictions tested against empirical observation; judge by predictive accuracy
  ^QP14-C02-3
- A model is judged by the usefulness and accuracy of its predictions, not by whether it is "true". _(mechanism, premise, Opus only)_ #mech/research_method
  > A theory’s utility is then determined by the quality of the predictions it makes and the accuracy with which these accord with empirical observation.
  ^QP14-C02-4b

### C03 (page 3)
- Mathematical elegance is an aesthetic criterion and does not imply a model is correct; the best model is not necessarily the elegant one. _(mechanism, premise)_ #mech/research_method
  > it would not be logically correct to infer that mathematics is reality, that the elegant solution is necessarily the best.
  conditions: Directed at mathematicians working in finance
  ^QP14-C03-1
- Models should not be treated as 'true for all practical purposes': Newtonian mechanics looked sufficient until a tiny discrepancy with observation opened the way to Einstein, so small unexplained deviations matter. _(mechanism, premise)_ #mech/research_method
  > The idea that models are nearly true or are true for all practical purposes must be rejected as forcefully as the idea that they are true.
  conditions: Illustrated by Newton-Einstein example (a tiny inconsistency had enormous consequences)
  ^QP14-C03-2
- When a model appears to fit, the explanation for why it looks true should be investigated rather than accepted. _(mechanism, qualifier)_ #mech/research_method
  > If they look true we must look for the explanation.
  ^QP14-C03-3
- Edmund Burke (1759 letter to Adam Smith, cited): theories based on unchanging human nature last, whereas theories based on changing opinions are forgotten. _(citation, premise)_ #mech/behavioral
  > A theory like yours, founded on the nature of man, which is always the same, will last, when those that are founded upon his opinions, which are always changing, will and must be forgotten.
  conditions: Cited as support that durable models rest on human nature
  ^QP14-C03-4
- Empirical testing gives an independent way to arbitrate between views, replacing sterile conflict with constructive argument. _(mechanism, premise)_ #mech/research_method
  > Natural science provides an independent method of arbitration between views
  ^QP14-C03-5
- Tiny discrepancies between a near-true theory and observations can have enormous consequences (Newton to Einstein). _(mechanism, evidence, Opus only)_ #mech/research_method
  > the tiniest chink in the accordance of the theory’s prediction with reality was sufficient to open the way for the einsteinian revolution.

### C05 (page 5)
- Ultimate test of a model's utility is profit from exploiting differences between its predictions and common opinion; older models are easier to judge because social systems evolve slowly. _(mechanism, mechanism_step)_ #mech/research_method
  > Had Smith lived through the subsequent centuries there would have been many occasions on which he would have been able to profit handsomely by exploiting the difference between his theories’ predictions and the common opinion
  ^QP14-C05-1
- Profit from trading a model's predictions against the market is the most honest test of its scientific utility; beating the market is thus an empirical test, not mere greed. _(mechanism, premise)_ #mech/research_method
  > In economics and the study of markets there can be no more incorruptible measure of utility than profit accrued by arbitraging the predictions of a model against the common view (the market).
  exact_rule: validate models by trading profit relative to market consensus
  ^QP14-C05-2
- Successful speculators are detached, unemotional observers; speculating is the empirical scientist's way of testing theories. _(mechanism, premise)_ #mech/behavioral
  > And a study of speculation will show that most successful speculators can be well described as ‘observers’.

- The theory's strength is the insight that consistent profit is hard, but that is not unique to it among alternatives. _(mechanism, qualifier)_ #mech/research_method

  ^QP14-C05-7
- Models are best judged on data that arrived after they were built (no prior knowledge), i.e. genuine out-of-sample tests over long periods. _(construction, design_choice, Opus only)_ #mech/research_method
