### 51. Chris Davis — Stewardship, patience and value investing (Insightful Investor, May 2026)

*Chairman and portfolio manager of Davis Advisors, an employee-owned firm founded in 1969 with \~$30B AUM (Mar 2026); Berkshire Hathaway director. The firm has beaten the Russell 1000 Value "in all periods" and the S&P 500 for \~3–4 years.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y4j55eMPdQo)

**Takeaway:** For a long-term owner the business's reinvestment return matters more than the entry multiple: "your return over time is going to approach the return on equity of the business, no matter what your starting price is." Davis buys aligned owner-operators at \~14–15x earnings, assumes no multiple expansion, and plays AI through enablers and users rather than hyped winners.

- ROE convergence: buy at 80%, or even half, of book a business reinvesting at a 4–5% ROE, and your return converges on 4–5%. That cheap stock is a value trap unless a catalyst arrives (liquidation, a private equity bid, new management).
- Pay 4x book (20x earnings, a 5% yield) for a business reinvesting everything at a 20% ROE. Yield on cost goes 5 → 6 → 7.2 → 8.5 → \~10%, converging on 20%.
- Invert the P/E for a starting estimate of future return and model no re-rating: "We may get it, but it's nowhere in our models." Like a $10M apartment earning $750k, an $8M bid is a paper loss, not a lost 7.5% yield.
- Spring 2026: Davis holdings grew earnings \~17% a year over 5 years at 14.5x. The Russell 1000 Value grew 12.5% at 20x; the S&P 500 grew 18% at 26x (\~3.8% earnings yield).
- "We've been right on the businesses, wrong on the prices": over \~15 years their companies' share of S&P earnings rose as their valuation share fell. "Anything starting at 14 or 15 times earnings is fine."
- Hard rule: no holding where management incentives aren't aligned with owners. A pay-maximizing manager's compensation is "an expense for us"; they want owner-operators standing alongside them.
- Book value is distorted by accounting and misses brands and intellectual capital. Sum-of-the-parts fails when it uses the parts' prices: Sears's (as he recalls) CompuServe stake (\~80% of net asset value) more than halved, a \~40% loss.
- Texas Instruments: in the early 2000s, legacy-fab depreciation depressed EPS while capex fell and cash poured in. The stock was flat \~7 years as Davis kept buying, then tripled in \~14 months.
- Short-term activism is "hugely destructive": activists would have fired Bezos in 2002. He backs TI's US fab build (since \~2021) against an activist over 10 years, and opposes splitting Markel (a \~20% pop, then a long-term loss).
- AI (May 2026): "We are in the overestimating phase," and picking winners is dangerous (the internet's Yahoo, AOL, Cisco). Own enablers that profit regardless of AI returns: Applied Materials, TSMC, Samsung, TI, natural gas and copper.
- Also own AI users with huge data sets, cuttable labour costs and willing management, led by Capital One (top 10 globally in AI patents). Avoid the "walking dead": Kodak was still in the S&P's top third as digital overtook film.
- "Three horsemen of the apocalypse" for the next 5 years (May 2026): index funds, momentum and illiquid private equity and credit, all backward-looking. "People have underestimated how valuable liquidity is."
- In momentum markets he never goes to cash; he buys the unloved. Meta, down 60–70% \~2022, became the largest position, and UnitedHealth is owned for the third time (2026).
- Pre-mortem: for each holding, write the article 5 years out on why it "dramatically underperformed." A mistake is misanalyzing the business, not a falling price; the biggest ones are framed in the research department.
