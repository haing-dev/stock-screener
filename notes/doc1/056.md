### 56. Connor Haley — Downside first in high-quality small and micro caps (Planet MicroCap, Jul 2019)

*Founder of Alta Fox Capital, a Fort Worth long/short fund then focused on small and micro caps; the host calls him the top-ranked investor on microcapclub.com.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s2BgEUsUVbA)

**Takeaway:** Most small and micro caps are low quality, so the edge lies in search: hunt situations the market structurally struggles to value, define the downside first, and buy only high-quality businesses that are "fairly obviously undervalued." Sizing follows hard rules: 15% max at cost, 8–12 longs, and 1–2% for a speculative "flyer."

- Base rate: "most small and microcap... businesses... are low-quality businesses," as are most of their managements. Finding the rare high-quality minority early drives returns, so search, not valuation, produces most excess return.
- Ask "why might this stock be mispriced?" Best are combinations of rapid change (new CEO or business line), complex segments, spin-offs, post-bankruptcy firms, recent IPOs, no comps, no coverage, long runways or long cycles.
- Screen pre-scale small caps on high gross profit to tangible assets: asset-light businesses that can grow with little reinvestment, before ROE or EBITDA screens catch them. He runs screens monthly to quarterly.
- Buy checklist: high returns on tangible capital behind a moat, a multi-year runway with higher incremental margins, and aligned management. Add a strong balance sheet, a "masking effect" hiding value (e.g. tiny size) and a catalyst; cyclicals need trough multiples and trough margins.
- Downside first: "if you can limit the downside for a small cap company that's a high quality business, there's always a good upside story to be told." Check supplier, customer and single-plant concentration.
- Sizing: 15% max position at cost, 8–12 longs, \~3% starters with a lighter diligence bar. A possible 10x with 100% downside gets only a 1–2% "flyer."
- Full-size test: it should take "an extraordinary set of circumstances," a financial-recession scenario, for a 15% position to fall 40–50%.
- Industry drives \~50% of a stock's move. Rank structures: monopoly, then duopoly or oligopoly, then a fragmented industry you can consolidate; worst is a sub-scale outsider where a few players dominate.
- Management should own at least \~5% in small and micro caps, and pay should lean to stock over cash. Acquiring toward a five-year revenue goal is "empire building"; per-share value, dilution and margins matter.
- Valuation should be easy: survivors "should jump off the page." Use 3–4 key drivers, weighted bear, base and bull cases, and a back-of-the-napkin model; needing a big spreadsheet is a bad sign.
- The sell-side method (a 1–2-year forecast times a peer multiple) fails for high-growth, investment-cycle and cyclical firms. Prefer free cash flow, since earnings and EBITDA "can be easily manipulated."
- Selling: his biggest mistake is selling 100% at target, then watching the stock rise 5x more. "I generally try to sell once the company stops executing"; cyclicals are sold earlier.
- Monitoring: "you own that stock every day." Track Google Trends, Yelp review counts, ex-employees and suppliers, and update intrinsic value every quarter.
