### 59. Joel Greenblatt — Columbia Lecture 1: breakups, spin-offs and the Sears stub (The value investing channel, Feb 2005)

*Founder of Gotham Capital; teaches special situations at Columbia Business School; author of You Can Be a Stock Market Genius, the source of the class's spin-off cases.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5kLAPBo_hZY)

**Takeaway:** Messy front-page breakups still get mispriced, so value the pieces conservatively and buy, or build a hedged stub, only with a wide margin of safety. His Sears stub, bought for \~$5, hit $30 in two months and later $50.

- Sears (1992–93): at $54, the stock held 1 Allstate share ($28) plus 0.4 Dean Witter ($15). Net of other units, the retailer cost \~$5: a \~$1.5B market cap on $27B of sales, almost debt-free.
- He went long Sears and shorted the Dean Witter and Allstate shares he would receive, isolating the \~$5 stub. Sized "huge" on \~10x upside, it went to $30 in two months, then $50.
- Stub mechanics: short exactly the shares you will receive to lock in their value, when you have no view on that piece. With 3Com/Palm he owned the rest of 3Com "for negative three dollars".
- First step on any breakup: "tear apart the company right now" and value the pieces conservatively. Sara Lee at \~$23 is worth pursuing if the parts total $36, not if they total $27 (Feb 2005).
- Why the edge survives: analysts suspend coverage "until the smoke is cleared" and managers with 30–50 stocks skip messy situations. He bought all the Sears he wanted after Barron's published the analysis.
- Read corporate-speak: Sears's "no intention of disposing" of Allstate meant it would. Parents sell ≤20% before a tax-free spin of the rest, and spin-offs usually come "from a position of weakness".
- Quick price/sales cross-check: the Sears stub was \~5.5–6% of sales against \~55% for J.C. Penney, another "crummy" retailer. That flags something fixable or "way too cheap".
- Moody's vs Coke: Moody's earned 100%+ returns on equity with zero reinvestment, while Coke reinvested \~20% of earnings. He paid 20x for Moody's, saw \~30x as fair, and has "never found a better business".
- Discount-rate floor: compare earnings yield with the 10-year bond but "never use less than six percent", i.e. a 16.66x unlevered P/E. He thinks in pre-tax EBIT multiples.
- AmEx/AEFA (Feb 2005): one analyst's sum of the parts was \~$59 vs $55 ($2.50 × 20 for travel services, $0.56 × 16 for AEFA). AEFA's upside is earnings recovery from \~11% ROE; the parent's is multiple expansion.
- Timing: if you only want the small spun-off piece, wait for the separation. If you would own the parent anyway, buy now: "in effect i'm buying two things i might want to own".
- Segment returns hide value: Sara Lee's 11% average could mask 20% and 3% businesses, and HP's high-return printer supplies sit beside low-return PCs. Slow restructuring timetables signal room for improvement.
- ROE vs ROA: lending at \~8% on 1–2% funding gives high ROE on low ROA, while fee businesses earn high ROE without the balance sheet. Split a financial firm's returns into the two.
- Risk is permanent loss: "i don't measure risk by volatility". He wants something worth $10 bought for $5–6, held \~3 years, so being wrong means roughly breaking even.
