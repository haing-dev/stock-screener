### 60. Joel Greenblatt — Columbia Lecture 2: Value Investors Club write-ups and answering as Buffett (The value investing channel, 2005)

*Founder of Gotham Capital, run small and concentrated in 5–8 positions; co-founder of the Value Investors Club; in his 10th year teaching this Columbia class.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YRd3vGZq-JQ)

**Takeaway:** A good write-up is one page showing a stock is obviously cheap on normalized pre-tax EV earnings, with high returns on tangible capital. Even crude cheapness works (the cheapest P/E decile made \~15%/yr vs \~7% for the dearest) because most investors can't sit through the bad years.

- NVR (Charlie479 on VIC, Jun 2001): a homebuilder using land options and pre-sales, at 7–8x earnings with high returns on capital. It had bought in half its stock in five years; $143 became over $800.
- NII Holdings (Nov 2002): a post-bankruptcy Nextel affiliate at 2.8x EBITDA vs 6.7x for peers, with earnings that had to be rebuilt from filings. It went from $10 to \~$240 split-adjusted.
- Sportsman's Guide (Jun 2003): a catalog retailer moving online, cutting its costs, at 4.85x (operating cash flow − capex) with no debt and buybacks. It went from $10 to \~$40 split-adjusted in two years.
- Paper standards: EV and pre-tax numbers, return on tangible capital (working capital + fixed assets), and EV-based comps. "i don't really want to see ebitda unless there's really no cap x".
- Preview of his coming book's study: combine high earnings yield with high return on capital, and "you really probably shouldn't be doing much else". Over 17 years, low-P/E deciles ranked in order: \~15%/yr vs \~7%, market \~10%.
- Why cheapness keeps working: "if it worked every year it wouldn't work". The What Works on Wall Street author quit after three bad years; the buyer ran the same formula to \~$2B.
- Rich Pzena lost most clients in his first four years, then beat the market by \~700 bp/yr since inception. His filter: is each cheap stock's problem permanent or short-term?
- Normalized earnings: $10 for nine years and $0 in one ≈ $9. Wells Fargo in the early-'90s bust was \~$70 on \~$16 normalized; he bought instead of waiting for $60, and it reached $250.
- Hurdle: an expected 20–30%/yr depending on risk; "if you are right the market will agree with you in two to three years". Re-rating front-loads gains, e.g. +30% in year one.
- Management: stock and options owned relative to annual pay is "probably the most important thing" he checks. He judges track record and buybacks, not meetings, where everyone "seems smart".
- Key3Media/Comdex, his worst investment: a $3 stub went to $12, then the show shrank from 1M to 200k sq ft. With \~$60 of $62 rent per sq ft as contribution, operating leverage cut both ways; he exited near $1.
- Foot Locker: $1 EPS at $10 hid \~$1.50 from sneaker stores and −$0.50 from Champs stores rolling off their leases. Valued at $17–20, it was "kind of a no-brainer".
- Concentrate in 5–8 names bought at 50–60% of value. Volatility, beta and Sharpe ratios are "garbage in garbage out"; Pzena's 3-year standard deviation was half the market's.
- As of 2005: "the biggest bargains are in large cap stocks" (Wal-Mart, AmEx, McDonald's) after five down years, while small caps had risen 100%+ since end-2002/2003.
