### 62. Brian Gaines & Joel Greenblatt — Columbia Lecture 4: How to make money in a dying industry (The value investing channel, Nov 2005)

*Gaines: founder of Springhouse Capital and ex-Gotham analyst, with gains "in the high 30s a year" over \~3.5 years per Greenblatt. Greenblatt: Gotham Capital founder and course instructor.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=99m_dvObAfU)

**Takeaway:** A "dying" industry can pay well if you buy cheaply against near-term cash, but sell the moment a dominant competitor wrecks the economics. Gaines bought Hollywood Video at \~3.6x EV/(EBITDA − maintenance capex) and dumped Movie Gallery when Blockbuster dropped late fees.

- Gaines: in a business that won't last forever, "the cash can be a huge component of your return". He won't touch one he thinks is completely dead in three years.
- Greenblatt: valuing a fading business is a plain DCF. Project \~8 years, ignore the terminal value, and pay about half the cash you'll collect ($5 for $10 ≈ 24% IRR).
- Gaines: in early 2004 Hollywood Video traded at 3.6x EV/(EBITDA − maintenance capex), with a 41.7% return on invested capital and a 10% CEO stake. His base, stress and break-up cases gave \~70%, 36% and \~80% upside.
- Gaines: Leonard Green bid $14 in Mar 2004 for a 30–40% gain in two months. CEO Wattles rolled his stake from 10% to \~50%: an insider adding money is the strongest signal.
- Gaines: check whether it's really dead (CDs still sell), what insiders do, and where cash goes: buybacks, debt paydown, new stores or waste.
- Gaines: fast payback makes the death date less critical. A Movie Gallery store cost \~$126K and earned \~$45K in year one, \~$79K at maturity: money back in \~3 years.
- Gaines: a dying business has built-in downside protection: "if you think the business is dying well don't put any more money into it". Hollywood also had a $73M tax-loss carryforward and a sellable Game Crazy unit.
- Gaines: Blockbuster (40–50% share) ended late fees on 14 Dec 2004. Assuming 5.5-day holds, his 10-K math cut gross profit per rental from $2.92 to $1.69 (\~40%), twice Blockbuster's implied \~20%.
- Gaines: he sold Movie Gallery and shorted Blockbuster. Movie Gallery ran to $32, then fell to $6.80 by late 2005 under $1.1B of debt; he missed the run but avoided the collapse.
- Gaines: Netflix (early 2005, \~$400M EV) was his biggest winner. Bay Area penetration hit 11%, subscribers grew despite Blockbuster's cheaper offer, and +$2/month on 3M subscribers ≈ $900M at 12x.
- Gaines: capital structure decides investability. Net cash gives options, bank debt can force bankruptcy, and a heavily levered equity is "an option bet".
- Gaines: hunt for hated stocks on 52-week-low lists, high short interest, IBD groups losing momentum, spin-offs and industries with distressed debt.
- Greenblatt: return on capital = EBIT/(net working capital + net fixed assets) and earnings yield = EBIT/EV, with EBIT meaning EBITDA − maintenance capex. Pre-tax 14% is "not good", 20%+ starts a good business, and 12x EBIT needs certainty.
- Greenblatt: subtract only excess cash, add minority interests, credit option proceeds, and add interim cash when valuing years out. Judge margin of safety on EV: a $5 stock worth $10 with $40/share of debt has little.
