### 65. Pat Dorsey — Competitive advantage masterclass: Moats 2.0 (The value investing channel, Apr 2022)

*Founder of Dorsey Asset Management (2014) and former head of equity research at Morningstar; \~18% a year since inception vs \~10% for the benchmark, \~1.8 billion AUM, 10–15 global positions (as of \~2021).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cNTTaDz29X0)

**Takeaway:** A moat is worth most when the company can reinvest most of its cash flow at high incremental returns. Digital firms expense that investment, so ROIC, margins and GAAP earnings can hide the moat, and the edge lies in qualitative primary research.

- Company A and Company B both earn 20% on capital. A reinvests only 30% of cash flow (end market growing 5–6%); B reinvests 70% at 20%, compounding more with less capital-allocation risk.
- Payouts add risk: most buybacks happen "at overpriced levels", most M&A is poor, and dividends bring tax leakage and redeployment risk.
- Reinvestment beats payout because far more companies sustain >20% returns on capital than fund managers earn >20% net long term.
- Four tests of NPV-positive reinvestment. Is it incremental (hiring) or fixed (a gigafactory), fungible or specific, likely to provoke competitors, and extending the moat or digging a new one?
- Iridium and Globalstar show the fixed, single-purpose failure: satellite phone networks overtaken by fast-improving cell phones, leaving stranded assets.
- "investment also happens on the income statement": sales, advertising and SaaS engineering are expensed even though customers may stay 5–15 years.
- Don't auto-exclude GAAP losses. Structural margins exceed current ones because keeping customers is cheaper than acquiring them, and cash flow can exceed GAAP income (negative working capital).
- "roic is no longer the canonical test of competitive advantage". With little invested capital even bad reinvestment shows high ROIC, and a falling ROIC can signal a brighter future.
- In mature, capitalising industries ROIC works, but a visible 8%-a-year compounder is probably efficiently priced. The opportunity is in dynamic, expensing industries where the moat isn't yet in the numbers.
- Moats slow the fade of returns to the cost of capital and cut risk in concentrated books (7–8% average, 12% big positions). They are also inefficiently priced.
- You can't screen for switching costs. The edge is primary research with customers, ex-employees and ex-salespeople (e.g. bake-off win rates), since quantitative data is crowded.
- Low turnover is "an output of good decisions", not an input. Thaler's mug experiment shows the endowment bias; the right holding period is the one that earns an excess return.
- Low-reinvestment businesses aren't bad, but capital allocation becomes the main value driver. Destructive M&A is the biggest risk; only small, iterative, programmatic acquirers create value.
