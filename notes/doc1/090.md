### 90. David Tepper — Bouncing back from failure: student Q&A at Carnegie Mellon (B.E.S.T., May 2018)

*Founder of Appaloosa Management and namesake of Carnegie Mellon's business school; formerly Goldman Sachs's head junk-bond trader, passed over for partner.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f4QgMZG8LSQ)

**Takeaway:** A career is defined by how you recover from setbacks, not by avoiding them. His market lesson (May 2018): "the machine is only as good as people program in the machines", so QE-era models fail when regimes shift.

- Regime risk (2018): models keep repeating patterns learned under QE and the financial crisis. Higher rates, the end of QE or an erratic president break them unless someone reprograms them.
- Rules backtested in a low-rate QE regime need review when conditions change quickly. Machine-driven funds were doing "really bad" in 2018 while he beat them, though he doesn't say quant can't work.
- Rates (May 2018): with the 10-year near 3%, the consensus expected it to stop at 3.25% by year-end. "when so many people say that I could become worried that's not gonna hold"; if it breaks, stocks may have a problem.
- Stocks were "okay" but "might have reached the highs for the year", hinging on rates (May 2018). He was also watching whether the Bank of Japan would change policy.
- Crypto (2018): he treats it as gold-like, with no value "above what a cost to mine them"; transaction speed is a lesser driver. Prices are hard to analyze, but traders can profit from news such as exchange bankruptcies, theft or Chinese capital controls.
- Tariffs (May 2018) are "in general ... not a good thing for the economy"; steel, employing few people, was a poor place to start. A first China round was fine as a shot across the bow, but his firm's escalation analysis ends in war by the fourth step.
- Know how options, finance and accounting work, whether you program the machines or compete with them. At Goldman he saw at once that the desk's model mispriced options.
- Distrust forecasts: his first mentor's crude line, "projections are like ... everybody has one", stuck with him as a basic truth. Apply it to management projections.
- Make the most of bad situations: he joined Republic Steel, headed for bankruptcy, and took a 7% pay cut within three months. It then did more financings in 1–2 years than in its 100-year history.
- Ethics: at Goldman's post-Drexel bankruptcy fund he refused the M&A head's order for a restricted-list stock, even after legal cleared it. It "really didn't help me" at the partner vote but didn't hurt his career.
- Know the playing field: he kept consulting mentor Bob Rubin instead of new fixed-income head Jon Corzine, who blocked his third partner bid. Staying at Goldman might have left him "not nearly as successful".
- Hire carefully: his firm "started out fairly fast" but had staff growing pains, and "you can't be careful enough". Assistants who smooth a business are "one of the greatest undervalued assets."
