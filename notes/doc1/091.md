### 91. Prem Watsa — Fairfax's Graham playbook: guest lecture at Ivey (Ivey Business School, 2011)

*Chairman and CEO of Fairfax Financial, taken over in 1985 at a $2 million market value; book value per share compounded 25% a year to \~$400 and float grew to \~$23 billion (2011).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N9CCyX-40OI)

**Takeaway:** Buy a dollar for 50 cents, go against the crowd, and stay financially sound enough to survive a 1-in-50 or 1-in-100-year event. Management's long track record is "the single biggest determinant" in avoiding value traps.

- Margin of safety is the key principle: work out what a company is worth and buy it for 50 cents on the dollar. Price decides the outcome: "what you pay makes a huge difference in relationship to what you get".
- Banks: "Banks Book value is not a bad first measure especially if you trust management". Fairfax bought Wells Fargo below book in the 2008–09 crash, citing its long management record.
- J&J was \~$60 in both 2002–03 and 2011 while EPS doubled ($2.25 to $4.50–$5), so the P/E fell from 25–30x to \~12x. It had hit 12x only \~five times in 60 years, so expect 10–15% earnings growth plus a "double whammy" if the P/E reverts.
- Seagram: Fairfax bought below net cash (\~$70 of cash per share vs a $65 stock), getting plants and inventories "for free". The stock doubled and tripled; management did waste the cash, but \~25 years later.
- Nortel was 40% of the Toronto index (TSX) at huge P/Es in 1999–2000, so Fairfax skipped it. Its bonds later traded at 40 cents with \~$4bn of cash covering \~$4bn of bonds, and went to 100.
- Value traps: "the single biggest determinant for us is management". Check a long record and prior employers, avoid the two-year CEO promising great growth, and give value 3–5 years to be recognized.
- Courage to average down: write down the five or six reasons when you buy at $10. If it falls to $5 and they still hold, buy more, even with 20 analysts saying it's worth $5–6.
- Survive the tail: as insurers plan for a Category 5 hurricane, ask "what happens if things go down 50% can we survive it". Debt is what kills: nine of Japan's top ten life insurers failed after guaranteeing 3–4% returns.
- Crisis playbook: worried about US housing in 2003–05, Fairfax held government bonds and cash and avoided AAA structures entirely. In late 2008–early 2009 "you were getting paid to take risk", so it bought corporate and junk bonds at 10–12% spreads.
- Consensus is right in engineering but already priced in markets: if ten oil analysts are positive, you'll be disappointed. So each of Fairfax's \~6 investors runs his own pool, and positions can build to \~$400m as others add.
- Crum & Forster, bought in 1998 for $680m as a turnaround, took 5–6 years to fix instead of 2–3. By end-2010 it had paid $1.5bn of dividends and still held $1.2bn of capital.
- Alignment: Watsa's salary has been fixed at C$600,000 since 2000 with no bonus or shares, and Fairfax discloses the negatives. A tiny holding company over five to seven units avoids bureaucracy and "unleashes talent".
- Macro (early 2011): after markets rose 80–100% from the lows, Fairfax hedged its portfolios through the S&P 500. US total debt relative to GNP exceeded 1929's, with Japan after 1989 (Nikkei down 75% in 20 years) the warning.
- Bubbles (early 2011): copper, gold and cotton were on a "parabolic curve" like silver in 1980. Facebook's $50bn private valuation showed emotion and greed, and housing euphoria "does come down it's only a question of time".
