### 92. David Einhorn — Critical thinking, poker and the bubble basket: Oxford Union Q&A (OxfordUnion, Dec 2017)

*Founder of Greenlight Capital, which he and a colleague started \~20 years earlier with $10,000 each in 130 square feet; avid poker player.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qvz5LS9pIjs)

**Takeaway:** Greenlight's edge is critical thinking that, a few times a year, finds an important difference of opinion with the other side of the trade. Those become a few large, unlevered positions, valued imprecisely and sized as if the whole stake can be lost.

- Three layers, as in poker: what you know (price, sales), what you can surmise (CEO motivation, strategy, competition) and unknowable events within a range. Combine them and ask if this is "a good place to commit a fraction of my capital".
- The facts matter least "because everybody knows them"; the edge is inference and judging the range of outcomes. Once you have "figured out the joke", it's just a matter of watching it happen.
- Imprecise valuation: don't ask whether a $10 stock is worth $11 or $12, only whether it's worth "a lot more than ten dollars". Reassess at $15, $18 and $20 as facts change.
- Shorts need "over valuation combined with some kind of deterioration"; valuation alone is not a short signal.
- Bubble basket (from \~2014–15): value unprofitable companies blind, from financial statements only, and short small if fair value is \~90% below price. Even if off by 100%, a stock 90% overvalued is still 80% overvalued.
- The basket held \~40–50 names: many failed or were de-rated, offsetting 2–4 big losers until early 2017. Then nearly everything rose in 2017; he drops any name that starts "showing real profits".
- Losers: the presumption is "we might have missed something here", so reassess constantly, then reduce, exit or hold. Adding to a loser happens "very rarely".
- Risk: no fund leverage, the best idea is capped, and market risk is taken knowingly. A $10 stock has "ten dollars of downside", not $1, because any stock can go to zero.
- Value at risk (VaR) holds capital for 95–98% of outcomes and none for the tail, which invites unlimited tail bets. The 2008 tail proved "much more likely and much more correlated".
- Time arbitrage is "one of the best inefficiencies in the market": investors skip stocks that won't rise within six months. "Dead money" stocks nobody wants are "sometimes... just the best investments".
- Value vs growth (late 2017): the market paid for social desirability and CEO charisma, as in the "eyeballs" era, and de-rated profitable companies. "it's not different this time and at some point this will revert", though results "have certainly suffered".
- Machines win at short horizons, so Greenlight hunts what is "widely misunderstood... by a large margin". It stays in developed markets, since local insiders and rules are hard to beat from New York.
- Activism is "very rare" and only for changes that help in the short, intermediate and long term. Many shareholder–management fights are about agency, such as managers protecting their pensions, not horizon.
- Post-2008 fixes "solved none" of the problems (2017): too-big-to-fail and rating-agency concentration remain. Derivatives clearinghouses are new, undercapitalized too-big-to-fail institutions.
