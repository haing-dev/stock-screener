### 95. Dan Sundheim — Amazon, Instacart, shorting after GameStop and risk as impairment (Sohn Conference Foundation, May 2021)

*Founder of D1 Capital (2018), with privates about half its capital; the host put AUM above $20 billion, $10 billion of it in 70 private companies (May 2021). Interviewed by John Collison of Stripe.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I8vLeOTrSjc)

**Takeaway:** Risk is permanently impairing capital over \~3 years, not short-term volatility, so he buys durable businesses with valuation discipline and never shorts to hedge. His one 10-year hold was Amazon, a logistics moat at \~20x 2023 GAAP EPS, "a five percent free cash flow yield" (May 2021).

- Risk is impairment: "if risk is that three years from now i've impaired capital then i have no tolerance for risk". A 20% drop tomorrow is fine, and owning long-duration businesses means he needs no shorts to hedge.
- "durability of business models probably the most important thing". Amazon is "a logistics company", like the railroads 100 years ago, so its moat isn't technology; only AWS carries some technology risk.
- Amazon (May 2021) trades at \~20x his above-Street 2023 GAAP EPS and will grow at multiples of GDP for 10 years. Its multiple needn't shrink as growth slows: Costco trades higher at a fraction of the growth because its cash flows are durable.
- Shorting after GameStop (May 2021): shorts are smaller "just for risk management purposes" and judged on "return on time", since the team stays small. D1 shorts only "to generate alpha", never to dampen volatility.
- Short alpha shrinks faster than long alpha as funds grow, since the worst companies rarely become mega caps. Retail gambling adds volatility that helps longs, but a losing short grows as leverage rises.
- Instacart: the biggest ad budgets belong to consumer packaged goods (CPG) makers like Heinz and are spent "really inefficiently offline". If grocery moves online the ad dollars follow, and value comes from advertising because grocery margins are slim.
- Privates: "if we're gonna hold the company for five or ten years why wait until the ipo". D1 skips Series A, enters after product-market fit, and runs one fund (his money up to 50% private, outside investors up to 35%).
- Netflix (May 2021): with the world "entirely streaming" in 10 years, it could reach 500M subscribers at well over $20 a month. His test for big stakes: "are they providing a lot more to the consumer than they're taking".
- Facebook's discount (May 2021) rests on the past decade's upstarts, the wrong model; if AR/VR is next, it's the likely winner. FAANG capital allocation, except Netflix and lately Google, has been poor; they should buy back more stock.
- Macro (May 2021): the Fed was "way too lenient" given savings and pent-up demand, but 1970s-or-Japan calls are a small share of outcomes. Amazon was fundamentally inflation-neutral, yet priced as if hurt.
- Stocks outgrew GDP (\~10% vs 4.5% a year over 25 years) because of interest rates, more international earnings and offshoring capital-intensive work to China. Large-cap margins also rose faster than small caps', and indexes concentrated in fast growers.
- Carvana (May 2021, a big position): in 15 years he sees 30% of used-car sales online, \~65% of that Carvana's \[?\]. Online offers a better experience and value with "massive economies of scale".
- Canadian cannabis (his Sohn 2019 bubble call) had no moat, no low-cost producer and lots of retail investors. The hard part was whether it would "go up 100 first" before imploding.
- Errors of omission are for learning, not regret: "you don't need to get everything right you get a few things right". In privates, bad Glassdoor reviews are "a pretty big red flag".
