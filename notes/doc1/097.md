### 97. Murray Stahl — Owning what cannot be indexed: a retrospective (Latticework by MOI Global, Sep 2026)

*Co-founder of Horizon Kinetics (1994) and head of FRMO after 16 years at Bankers Trust; died 7 Apr 2026, aged 72. A narrated documentary built from his dated remarks, 1984–2026.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UHuFQATGpsI)

**Takeaway:** Float-weighted index money buys at any price, underweights owner-operators and misses dormant assets, spin-off parents, small exchanges and royalties. The best opportunities are what cannot be indexed, above all Texas Pacific Land (TPL), held from a chance 1984 find until his death.

- Float weighting (\~2006): insider buying shrinks the float and so the index weight, while insider selling raises it. "well-managed firms with substantial management ownership are of necessity underweighted in indexes."
- Owner-operators, who manage because they own the most equity, earned "the bulk of the return" in his S&P 500 study back to 1957. Yet they trade cheapest: "It shouldn't exist, yet it does."
- The index is a buyer with no sell price: at "a million times earnings, the money would still come". The result, by 2019, was "a paucity of investment opportunities"; he never claimed it overpays.
- True float shrinks: index funds sell only on net outflows, so index-held shares aren't really float. At 70% index ownership, the next 1% bought is 1% of the remaining 30%: "At some point, the curve becomes a hyperbola."
- Screen signals: high insider ownership, small size or absence from indexes, and "agent-operator" pay unrelated to long-term share value as a red flag. Watch index-eligibility events: TPL's 2021 corporate conversion put it in 32 ETFs by July.
- TPL (1995 report): buybacks in all but four of 106 years cut shares 34% since 1980, lifting acres per share from 0.26 to 0.37. The market valued it at $54 an acre vs land sales averaging $192, with "Property, no value assigned" on the balance sheet.
- After fracking, operators had to cross TPL's land, so easements, water and sundries became incremental profit (2013). Asked about trimming in Oct 2025: "The water is forever and the land is forever."
- Dormant assets suffer "hyperbolic discounting": Dundee traded at \~C$1.40 vs $10 of net asset value (NAV), too small for any index. Urbana, a closed-end fund at a \~37% discount, held Quebec land carried at no value that miners were exploring for gold.
- Spin-offs: buy the parent. Expelling the worst student raises a class's average without anyone learning more: "This is really what's happening in the world of spin-offs."
- Exchanges and royalties earn large sums without investing large sums: an exchange's marginal revenue "for the most part, goes to the bottom line". Royalties are "a call option on everything"; one royalty company held more reserves after 30 years.
- Why active lagged (Apr 2025): multinationals cut tax rates from 2007 levels (Apple to 14% from 30.2%, Microsoft to 17.7% from 30%), lifting indexes. With that advantage ending, "the edge now moves to the active manager".
- Bad-company screen from his 1985 lecture (per co-founder Steven Bregman): flat revenues, rising costs, too much debt and near-term maturities. "Why not buy all 499 companies in the S&P 500 except the worst one?"
- Short what the rules break (Jan 2018): a path-dependent 3x inverse silver ETF decays toward zero, so he shorted it. "Shorting a short is long."
- Timing: in Apr 2016 indexation was in the "late innings" and wouldn't "take too long"; by Sep 2018 he had "no insight". In Sep 2024 he said the pandemic extended tech's outperformance by 3–4 years.
