### 114. Rod Wong — From science boom to commercial wave: where biotech's edge lies (Insightful Investor, Apr 2026)

*Physician-trained founder and CIO of RTW Investments, a life-sciences investment and innovation firm; a podcast interview on how RTW evolved, how it judges biotech investments and where the sector is heading.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JlmjpU7BUEw)

**Takeaway:** The early-stage science boom of the past half-dozen years is maturing into approved drugs, which should create a new generation of large commercial biotechs over the next decade. Specialists keep an edge because the field is hard and many traders are not specialists, but the edge now needs commercial underwriting and new data, not just science.

- RTW grew in three phases. First came specialist money management with structured underwriting of "odds of success" and repeatable commercial forecasting. About a third of the way in, it joined the few firms converging public and private ("crossover") investing. Lately it has scaled into a large team that subspecializes by disease and modality and works collaboratively.
- Private stakes sharpen public investing: private investors see data that "doesn't have to be revealed once a company becomes public" and get to know managements and boards. That matters because biotechs list very young, "when there's still science experiments". Full-life-cycle flexibility lets RTW "skate to where we see the greatest opportunity": small-cap public, larger companies or private early stage.
- The boom of the last \~half-dozen years was "very much an early stage science boom", and its disappointments explain the recent bear market. Now much of that science is reaching patients and forming "a new generation of commercially successful biotech companies", "of the size that we haven't seen ever in the history of the industry".
- Timeline: discovery has historically taken 6–7 years to the "light bulb moment", plus 2–3 years of preclinical work, then another 6–7 years of clinical development. Discovery and preclinical work are getting faster, with higher odds and better candidates.
- Commercial underwriting is the growing skill. As more drugs reach approval, the question is whether the unmet need can "support a thriving and growing business", a skill "a lot of biotech investors don't have".
- Three questions (will it work, will it sell, what is it worth?): "for a great investment you have to have all three". RTW wants "high probability of success combined with asymmetric risk return because this is a probabilistic business".
- Binaries are risk but "also critical opportunity", because specialists can exploit the inefficiency around them. Prices can swing widely with "no change in fundamentals", so keep "steady hands" and ideally "play offense". Top-down, avoid overexposure to any one theme or risk factor (stage, public/private, disease, technology).
- The edge persists because biotech is hard and many traders are non-specialists or retail, so managers have shown "sustainable alpha". Core alpha is scientific and commercial underwriting; on top sits "temporary alpha", which shifts: in 2025 RTW set up a government affairs function, rare among healthcare investors, and data science and AI is its "fastest growing team" as prescription-data vendors multiplied "many many fold" in 2–3 years.
- GLP-1s: "They are the absolute real deal." They cut cardiovascular mortality, the West's top killer, by "somewhere in the zip code of 20 to 30%" for the average patient. The story "still has many chapters left to be written": expect more potent and less frequent injectables, and new mechanisms that are not GLP-1-based, within a handful of years.
- Gene and cell therapy are still niche: life-changing in severe genetic diseases and some refractory cancers, but held back by safety (gene therapy) and manufacturing that makes them costly and inconvenient. mRNA was ideal for COVID, but for targets like flu, mature vaccines set "a very high bar".
- Policy (Apr 2026): in 2025 tariff threats got most big pharmas to commit "pretty close to $400 billion" to US manufacturing. A direct-to-consumer route for the uninsured, at prices "much closer" to Europe's, "largely resolved" the pricing fight "by early fall". The open question is the FDA: turnover under Commissioner Makary, including the heads of the drug (CDER) and biologics (CBER) divisions, after earlier leadership raised the bar for rare-disease approvals.
- China has become "the second most important source of innovation" in the last handful of years. He thinks having it as a competitor "will make us better" if the US steps up.
- Leaders need three skill sets in turn: creative discovery, running a \~decade-long development effort (likened to "landing a spaceship on the moon") and commercial execution. They also need "incredible salesmanship", since the average drug costs "north of a billion dollars" raised across private and public markets. His biggest change of mind in five years: how critical leadership and management are.
- Screen implication: newly commercial biotechs, the group he expects to create the most value, fail the Healthcare-Commercial profit-record (4 of the last 5 years) and 10-year ROIC rules for years; review launch-stage names by hand.
