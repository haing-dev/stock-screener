### 115. Chris Liu — ASCO preview: RAS inhibitors, PD-1/VEGF bispecifics and China's rise in oncology (RTW Investments LP, May 2026)

*Senior research analyst at RTW Investments, responsible for oncology research and for coverage of China life-sciences companies, previewing the ASCO cancer meeting on the RTW podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6ZXoIwblZko)

**Takeaway:** This ASCO (May 2026) puts biotech, not big pharma, at the centre with late-stage data in the hardest cancers. Revolution Medicines' oral RAS inhibitor doubled median survival in second-line pancreatic cancer, which Liu calls the "first validation" of a drug class aimed at mutations found in about 30% of all tumour types.

- Treatment ladder: surgery, then chemotherapy and radiation (which kill normal cells too), then targeted small molecules and antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates and radioligands. Resistance ("cancer cells keep mutating") led to immunotherapy, whose responses tend to be "really long lasting". Cancers are now grouped by genetic subtype as well as organ: HER2, first found in breast cancer, is approved in multiple cancer types.
- Biggest unmet needs: pancreatic cancer and glioblastoma, where standard of care is "just old chemotherapy or radiation therapy". They are "cold" tumours: a thick outer layer of tissue keeps T cells, and even drugs, out. Lung cancer, melanoma and kidney cancer are "hot".
- Biotech's playbook: front-line therapy is the biggest market but needs the biggest, costliest trials, which pharma can afford. Biotechs start in later lines, where trials are smaller and enrol faster, for "a quicker proof of concept or even commercialization", then use revenue to fund the move to earlier lines. Earlier-line combinations with standard of care lead to pharma partnerships.
- Why oncology is hard to call: trials cost more per patient (supportive care), and phase 3 oncology "has one of the highest failure rates". The FDA usually requires beating standard of care on overall survival, with no non-inferiority trials. Pre-phase-3 data are small, single-arm and non-randomised, so the drug's effect is "harder to predict".
- The bar keeps rising: with many PD-1 drugs approved, immunotherapy is now the comparator. In head and neck cancer a new drug must beat PD-1 plus chemo, with overall survival of around two years: "Your new drug has to be meaningfully better." Late-line accelerated paths can use response rate or progression-free survival instead.
- RAS mutations drive most pancreatic cancer. The round RAS protein has no pocket for a small molecule, so it was "considered an undruggable target". Revolution Medicines adds a second protein, cyclophilin A, so its drug finds a "novel pocket" binding the two together.
- Revolution Medicines' phase 3 (second-line pancreatic, ASCO plenary): median overall survival of 13.2 months vs about 6.7 months on standard of care, from an oral pill. To displace front-line standard of care it needs a large randomised phase 3 against chemotherapy, which was starting (May 2026).
- Read-through: RAS mutations are in about 30% of all tumour types, 40–50% of colon cancer and \~25% of non-small-cell lung cancer, so the pancreatic data are "just first validation of this entire class". But "there's never been just one company": a class of RAS-inhibitor companies follows, and better chemistry may give better drugs.
- Akeso (China) presents front-line lung-cancer phase 3 data for ivonescimab, a PD-1/VEGF bispecific "combining two old targets", against Keytruda plus chemotherapy. A strongly positive result could "un-gate a big wave of next-gen immunotherapy across multiple tumor types". Ex-China partner Summit Therapeutics expects its first global phase 3 readout in 2026.
- China has flipped from importer to exporter of drugs. High CDE (China's FDA) hurdles once made Chinese firms license US drugs; now China shows "a dramatic increase of early phase 1 trials" and is "almost kind of leading the speed of drug development". Western licensees get proof-of-concept data early: Akeso's partners started with data on hundreds of patients in China.
- AI in drug development is in "very early innings". Patient data are sensitive and stay inside companies, and public data are mostly successful trials. Better models need industry-wide negative data, and regulators are pushing companies to publish failed trials.
- Flows and outlook: biotech was "really underappreciated over the last, you know, five years", and "2025 was the year that the sector came back and flows started coming back in." He expects more M&A, since moving drugs into earlier lines takes capital. Within 2–3 years he expects T-cell engagers and cell therapies, proven in blood cancers, to have a dramatic impact in solid tumours.
