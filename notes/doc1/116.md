### 116. Peter Kolchinsky, Zach Scheiner \[?\] & Becca Silverberman \[?\] — Back to the future: new depression drugs and psychedelics (RA Capital Management, Apr 2026)

*RA Capital managing partner Peter Kolchinsky, principal and neuroscientist Zach Scheiner \[?\] and TechAtlas senior associate Becca Silverberman \[?\] map old, current and coming depression drugs in an RA Futurecast webinar recorded in or after 2022 (Auvelity's 2022 approval is called recent).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OHXGU7AV2kI)

**Takeaway:** Depression drugs have barely changed in 20 years: they share a narrow set of mechanisms, and a third of patients are still not in remission after four tries. New mechanisms, from Auvelity to psychedelics, should make treatment "unrecognizable" by \~2030, but payers keep new brands in third line until they go generic.

- STAR\*D, the largest and longest depression trial (NIMH-funded, early 2000s), is "as relevant today" because the options are basically the same (Scheiner \[?\]). Remission was 37% on citalopram (Celexa) after 12–14 weeks, 31% at step two, then 14% and 13% at steps three and four. After four 3-month steps, a third of patients were still not in remission.
- Payers' "nearly ubiquitous rule": a branded antidepressant is reimbursed only after the patient has failed two generics, so every branded drug sits in third line or later (Silverberman \[?\]). Kolchinsky: payers won't let a well-tolerated but similar brand go first line ("We're not going to give you the easy one"), but ease off once generics have failed.
- Market size: Trintellix, a near-copy of generic drugs "with really questionable differentiation", reaches only \~14 of 1,000 treated patients yet is "on pace to sell a billion dollars a year" (Kolchinsky, \~2022). "the markets here are enormous", and even modest differentiation is rewarded in third and fourth line.
- Nearly all approved drugs are monoaminergic (dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin), so a patient who fails one has few truly different options (Silverberman \[?\]). Kolchinsky: "you don't want to create the 20th screwdriver", even a better one; the value is in new classes.
- New classes have been rare: tricyclics by 1960, little in the 1970s–80s, SSRIs and atypical antipsychotics by 2000, nothing new by 2010. J&J's esketamine (Spravato, 2019) was the first rapid-acting option, under four weeks, but is tightly restricted: a REMS program, one distributing pharmacy in the US and DEA scheduling (Silverberman \[?\]).
- Axsome's Auvelity (bupropion plus dextromethorphan in a set ratio, approved 2022) is the first rapid-acting drug that is not a dissociative, acting via NMDA and sigma-1, and any primary-care doctor can prescribe it. Head-to-head it beat bupropion alone and worked faster: bupropion's week-4 gain was smaller than Auvelity's at week 2 (Silverberman \[?\]).
- Kolchinsky treats Auvelity as a test case. If doctors just prescribe the two generic components instead, RA would probably not back another company built on a patented combination of two generics. RA is tracking its uptake to see how far physicians go to avoid a branded drug; "what needs to be rewarded is the knowledge of how to use an old drug".
- Reading the data: in depression trials "placebo works quite well", because patients get attention and weekly check-ins (Scheiner \[?\]). Esketamine needed eight doses out to day 24 and separated only modestly from placebo, though enough for approval. Kolchinsky: more intense treatments draw bigger placebo effects (sham back surgery beats a placebo pill), so a psychedelic trip may too.
- Psilocybin: Compass Pathways ran the largest randomized trial of a psychedelic in treatment-resistant depression (TRD) to date (Scheiner \[?\]). One dose "works rapidly and it works effectively and it appears to work durably" (Phase 2b), but the trip lasts 6–8 hours with therapy alongside (Silverberman \[?\]). Ketamine needs 6–8 clinic doses in the first month (Scheiner \[?\]).
- 5-MeO-DMT, where RA "made a couple of investments": a 10–15 minute trip vs 6–10 hours for other psychedelics, which matters for reaching millions of TRD patients. In an 8-patient open-label TRD study, every patient was in remission on day 1 and 7 of 8 on day 7. There was no control arm, but with the other data RA thinks "this is quite real". RA backs two routes of the same molecule: inhaled vapor and an under-the-tongue tablet.
- MDMA for PTSD (MAPS Phase 3) looked "twice as good" as intensive talk therapy alone, using three 8-hour sessions plus preparation and integration. A second Phase 3 was running, with approval hoped for "in the next year or two" (\~2022). RA has also invested in Delix, which seeks psychedelic benefits without the trip (preclinical).
- Execution risk: doctors may lack "the bandwidth to learn how to use all these new drugs", so "sales and marketing is going to be key" (Scheiner \[?\]).
- RA's 1,000-patient model (Kolchinsky): new third- and fourth-line brands by 2030 cut patients not well treated after third line from 374 to 339, and after fourth line from 325 to 278. The big drop comes \~2040, when these drugs are generic and payers stop blocking earlier use.
- Faster fix: a subscription deal, e.g. a payer paying $350 million instead of a projected $300 million to treat any covered member, could pull the 2040 outcome toward 2030 (Kolchinsky). Progress depends on keeping "the incentives in place".
