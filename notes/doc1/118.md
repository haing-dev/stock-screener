### 118. Rod Wong — Biotech's next era: from discovery to commercialization (RTW Investments LP, Jan 2026)

*Managing partner, CIO and founder (2009) of biotech specialist RTW Investments, trained in medicine at Penn, interviewed on the RTW podcast by Stephanie Sirota, RTW's chief business officer.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ygjI4mjwIj8)

**Takeaway:** After a decade of discovery and a decade of development, biotech is entering "the decade of commercialization": more drugs are crossing the finish line than ever, leadership is broadening, and most of RTW's investments are now in commercial-stage companies. AI could be the biggest lift to R&D productivity since the genome, in an industry that spends \~25% of revenue on R&D with a 90% failure rate.

- The genome cost the Human Genome Project about $3 billion and Celera about $300 million (\~1999–2000). Benefits "really started to accelerate" only after \~15 years, when the cost per genome fell below $1,000. About two-thirds of disease has a genetic contribution, and "somewhere north of 5000 rare diseases are caused by a single genetic mutation", which gave drug developers their targets.
- New technologies need time: Jesse Gelsinger died of a cytokine storm after an adenovirus-vector gene therapy in 1999 (while Wong was at Penn), and the field slowed until better vectors arrived. "Not every technology at the beginning is ready for prime time."
- 2005–15 was a small, low-innovation industry: \~$300 billion of public market cap, 80% in the US and half in four companies (Genentech, Amgen, Gilead, Biogen). A big specialist fund was \~$0.5 billion. A scientific background plus basic finance or forecasting skill gave a "major edge".
- Where to look: follow the innovation comprehensively and go where the odds of success are above average and the business is big enough to underwrite. Early on that meant rare disease and RNA.
- Bear markets: in 2015–16 (Valeant's collapse, Theranos exposed as a fraud, Hillary Clinton's drug-pricing tweets) "Nobody wanted biotech." COVID and policy, including the IRA, amplified the later boom and bust. With a fundamental value framework, bear markets are "the time where I see some of the most opportunity"; Sirota notes RTW grew headcount and AUM through the last one.
- Broadening leadership: the industry is worth "a little shy of $1.5 trillion", up \~4–5x, and about twice as many names now make up half of it. argenx, Alnylam, Insmed and BeOne are all now bigger than Biogen, one of the old big four, and he expects the trend to accelerate.
- AI may be "the most significant thing to impact research productivity in our space since the genome". Large-cap biotech spends \~25% of revenue on R&D (chips and software a bit over half that), with a 90% failure rate and over a dozen years per drug. Better odds, timelines or product quality mean faster growth from the same spend, or the same result for less.
- Risk management: "The number one thing you can do in terms of risk management is to generate alpha", because the space "remains highly inefficient". Bets with favourable up/down and valuation discipline give a margin of safety in a bear market, and conviction that prevents trading mistakes. One RTW fund can flex gross and net exposure, cutting net exposure when valuations are high.
- "Alphastacking": Sirota cites the old rule, "invest in the science and then sell the launch". Today a majority of RTW's investments are commercial-stage, so commercial forecasting is "critical" and will decide who succeeds this decade. Find sources of alpha, test them, and maximise the real ones; the two biggest are underwriting science and underwriting commercial. RTW set up a government-affairs function when Trump won the election to navigate policy.
- Longevity and prevention: "buzzwords are not businesses". The real longevity gift is GLP-1 drugs, now cheaper for uninsured patients (Lilly going direct to consumer, Novo). Nutrition, sleep and fitness are "lifestyles", not investable businesses. Wearables and self-monitoring may be "not a business yet", and RTW's medtech practice is following some such companies.
- New capital is coming from private wealth, private-bank clients, high-net-worth investors and retail. His 2035 hope: twice as many novel medicines reaching patients each year, and AI-driven efficiency making biotechs "more predictable, higher growth businesses". His health-policy book is "Innovation is the Best Medicine".
