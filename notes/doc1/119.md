### 119. Peter Kolchinsky — AI won't cure every disease in 10 years, and drugs are a bargain because they go generic (Imagination in Action, Oct 2026)

*Founder and managing partner of RA Capital (\~300 people, $16bn under management, 200 portfolio companies), debating AI in drug development with hosts John and Alex, then on drug pricing, hiring analysts and GLP-1s.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXi7idtLzfc)

**Takeaway:** AI will help drug developers make fewer bad guesses, but each guess still takes years and tens of millions of dollars to test in people, so curing all disease will take decades. Back molecules, not AI pitches; medicines are cheap per American and go generic, so price controls would only mean fewer of them.

- Co-host Alex bets that "in silico, substantially every human disease is likely to be solved in the next ten years by AI"; Kolchinsky takes "the over". Drug development is "the slowest learning loop": a math proof can be checked "in, like, seconds", but an AI drug guess costs "tens of millions of dollars per guess" and "three or four years to find out if it's right".
- Why biology resists AI: there is very little human data, mouse studies return data in "weeks or months" but have only "some predictive value" for humans, and ethics boards make new molecules prove themselves in "last line patients" first. Alex counters that digital twins could disrupt clinical testing too; Kolchinsky says such models are "nowhere near it right now", and AlphaFold "gives you a static picture of one protein".
- AI-biotech pattern: tech investors give machine-learning drug companies "blitz funding", lose interest five years later, and the CEO comes to "the boring biology folks", "prepared to raise money at a lower valuation". "oftentimes there's nothing good there. It's just a slop." He sees the same in recent AI-drug companies, though not all, and looks for "a really great molecule or two or five".
- One positive trial leaves "a one in 40 chance that it's nothing, that it's just a placebo"; two trials cut it to one in 1,600. So the FDA wants two trials for common conditions such as depression and accepts one for rare genetic diseases. He calls talk that the FDA now needs only one trial "politics": the requirement "has long been flexible".
- Cost per head: a drug with $4bn of revenue is "$1 per American per month". Vertex's $8bn cystic-fibrosis cure is $2, "only until the product goes generic and then it's pennies". All novel medicines are "about 9% of total health care spending", \~$135 per American per month.
- Medicines are "one of the best bargains in all of health care" because "Hospitals don't go generic." Without HIV antivirals, America would need "over 200 more hospitals" at \~$60bn a year (\~$15 per American per month); the antivirals cost $20bn ($5). Price controls backfire: "you don't get more of it cheaply. You get less of it." His fix is insurance design: cover an appropriately prescribed drug "from the first dollar. No deductible, no copay."
- Pricing builds categories: Mylan got the EpiPen in "a portfolio of discarded drugs" where it sold poorly at a low price. Raising the price made it "worth promoting", and Mylan then lobbied for laws such as letting a school nurse use one child's EpiPen on another. Allergy families "should be really grateful that Mylan price jacked the EpiPen."
- Launch risk for better versions of generic drugs: he invested in and joined the board of ARS Pharma (intranasal epinephrine, neffy). EpiPen is now generic, and "Insurance plans would love to force everyone to just get generic EpiPen". After "a couple of years" on the market it still fights plan by plan for coverage, and he concedes it may not sell well.
- Celiac (1% of people) has no drugs, though an anti-IL-15 molecule "suggests that maybe it could work in celiac". His gluten sensor (Nima \[?\]) is "a food tester", outside insurance coverage, so it must be sold employer by employer. "I cannot keep funding a company that's not profitable forever just because I want the product to exist for my son."
- China: blocking the licensing of promising Chinese drug candidates (a "Biotech Investment National Security Act" \[?\]) "would backfire" and shift industry leadership to Europe. Alex sees China on track to launch far more new trials than the US; Kolchinsky: "I don't care if China or Martians solve celiac", since more companies mean more shots at cures.
- How RA grew: $4 million from his mentor became $12 million, then a public biotech hedge fund. Competitive analysis of public companies kept turning up "really great private companies", so RA started "reaching through the IPO window". Board seats showed "how the sausage is made" (e.g. a bad manufacturing batch risking a clinical hold), which led to an incubator run with former drug-development executives.
- Hire for conviction, not pedigree: one hire who "had all the credentials" pitched ideas popular with his MBA peers and wanted to sell when they fell. RA's reaction to a drop is "great. It's cheaper now. We should want to buy more." His first analyst, Raj, with a chemistry background and no finance training, became co-portfolio manager; RA now looks for "who can originate conviction" ("lessons of moneyball").
- TechAtlas farm team: a new hire gets six months to meet "every single company in the world" in one disease (breast cancer: CDK4/6, HER2, estrogen-receptor drugs) and maps rivals vs complementary drugs. They make no stock calls, because judging whether "$200 million is a high or a low price" for a Phase 2 data set takes training. Get "10, 20, 30, 50 opinions"; "one out of four or five" hires become investors.
- GLP-1s for preventing weight gain: "nobody's thinking of weight gain prevention". If 1–2 billion of 8 billion people paid something (Tylenol and aspirin draw "30 billion dollars"), "this could be a couple hundred billion dollar category". One of his companies has a room-temperature topical GLP-1 patch that delivers an injection's dose, aimed at over-the-counter sale.
