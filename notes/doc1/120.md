### 120. Peter Kolchinsky — Investing through FDA delays, MFN, the IRA and China (BioCentury Video, Feb 2026)

*Co-founder and managing partner of RA Capital and a long-time drug-policy advocate, interviewed by BioCentury's Washington editor on the FDA, MFN, the IRA and China.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ifp5J19RMx8)

**Takeaway:** FDA understaffing, most-favored-nation (MFN) pricing and the IRA shave value off new drugs but don't make biotech uninvestable (Feb 2026). Bake in delays, run early trials abroad and keep control of ex-US pricing; the damage concentrates in Medicare-heavy diseases and drugs that need ex-US sales.

- FDA (Feb 2026): after the FDA refused to review Moderna's flu vaccine, he would "bake in a three-month minimum delay, you know, for even the best drugs". A delay wastes patent time and "shaves some of the value off something, but it doesn't make it totally uninvestable."
- Vaccines are the exception: filing one with this administration, "I'd be applying a really heavy discount", because he can't read the rules of MAHA.
- "if you can do your phase one two studies outside the US, do them outside the US." He doubts FDA teams have the bandwidth for timely IND reviews. Australia is efficient at Phase 1s and the UK "can be good"; come to the US for the global registrational study.
- Anything new takes extra time. With oligos (he sits on oligo-company boards), the FDA asked questions and raised animal "quirks" other agencies didn't, which turned out not to matter, even before this administration. So parallel-track submissions elsewhere.
- For years he has told companies doing ex-US deals to "bake into your partnership agreement" a way to take back the drug or control its ex-US price. One of his companies did so several years ago and is better placed for MFN now.
- MFN math: "probably about 70 or 80% of the total profits" of a drug come from the US, so MFN means "sacrificing 20 to 30% of the NPV of a novel drug". Companies may set price floors pegged to the US price adjusted for GDP per capita and not launch where countries won't pay; ex-US partnerships will bring in less, or may not be possible.
- Backfire risk: countries skipped under MFN will "pivot to China", which offers "the fifth in class". His alternative is a narrow experiment: cap US prices at France's GDP-per-capita-adjusted price and add trade pressure, "the same principle as like NATO spending". He'd like MFN for Ozempic in Denmark alone.
- MFN could still lower US prices by enlarging the market, since a bigger global market keeps a fourth or fifth entrant in a class: "that's how the price comes down in all markets". The US has \~four SGLT2s; a fourth hep C cure would have brought "slightly lower net prices".
- Where policy bites: Medicare negotiation and MFN mainly hit Medicare and Medicaid, so migraine and lupus (mostly younger patients) are "largely unaffected". Early-stage small-molecule Alzheimer's work is "squarely in the strike zone" of the pill penalty, and diseases whose patients are mostly ex-US are now hard to navigate.
- The IRA was avoidable: a No Patient Left Behind letter got "a whopping 1300 signatures", and one Democratic senator telling Senator Wyden to "change 9 to 13" would have done it. "We did it to ourselves." The host adds that pharma CEOs' silence on RFK Jr.'s nomination "was interpreted as consent".
- On drug prices, first ask "prices to whom charged by whom?": insurance plans set what patients pay and can make a $5 generic unaffordable or a $300,000 cystic-fibrosis drug affordable. And "never use the word recoup": "Nobody invests to recoup their investment."
- Why he still invests: "the worse it gets, the more people come out of the woodwork". Policy is "thick black smoke that can choke you sometimes", but the industry won't burn up.
- China: "invent globally and manufacture locally". Use China's efficiencies to reach proof of concept faster, then make approved drugs where supply can't be weaponized. China can't run global registrational studies (too little patient diversity), so reliance on its early R&D is hard to weaponize, despite bipartisan pressure to disentangle (host: the BIOSECURE Act).
