### 122. Annie Lamont — Get there before others: founder-first venture in healthcare and fintech (Generating Alpha Podcast, Apr 2025)

*Co-founder and managing partner of Oak HC/FT, a healthcare and fintech venture and growth firm (over $14 billion in assets since inception, per the host) that backed athenahealth, One Medical, VillageMD and NetSpend. She is interviewed by a student host about her career and principles.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aAiuPaeOXEg)

**Takeaway:** Back founders first, and get to a niche "before other people": Lamont built an early biotech niche, left it in 1999–2000 for cloud healthcare software, and keeps reinventing herself. In downturns, make sure companies have the cash to get through, cut deep once, and keep investing; she sees GenAI as the biggest chance yet to cut healthcare costs (Apr 2025).

- Be early: at Oak Investment Partners she started in software, then carved out her own niche in life sciences when "there wasn't much competition". Two neurology start-ups she backed (ALS and memory) succeeded: one was sold for 7 billion, the other is still a multi-billion-dollar public company. "it is good to be somewhere before other people are you don't have to be as good honestly".
- Know when to leave: in 1999–2000, she says, there were 5,000 biotech companies, 4,000 of them public, and "literally five approved drugs". Getting drugs through the FDA was harder than people thought, so she expected "a rough decade" and moved to cloud healthcare software. Biotech did come back, but it "took about a decade". She backed athenahealth, in her words the first cloud-based software company, now the leading ambulatory health-record and billing company after Epic.
- Reinvention is the job: her longevity comes from "constantly striving to figure out what's the next great thing and getting there before others", and from not being afraid to "repot yourself".
- Why Oak HC/FT split off: Oak's tech group worked like lone "Jedi", with no talent team, marketing or associates. It "kept doing the same thing over and over again" and in the end wasn't making money. She and Andrew Adams left to build a team culture; the firm is now almost 50 people, with a five-person talent team, marketing, and Oscar Health's former chief product officer helping portfolio companies embed AI.
- Learning a new industry is primary research: experts, networking and conferences, now much faster with GenAI and the internet. After investing in over a hundred companies she is "a participant in the industry". Healthcare is 20% of the economy and fintech, depending on definition, 30%, yet "there's probably 300 people you need to know".
- Founder first: the CEO and team come before everything, because an early-stage business may end up quite different from the plan. She also needs the market right, some product-market fit, tailwinds, and the ability to pivot a model that isn't working. The CEO should be someone people follow, who "knows more about this problem than anybody in the world", intense and curious, "confident but not arrogant": asking "a million questions", then deciding and course-correcting.
- Impact and returns align: after backing the first for-profit hospice company, she later backed Aspire, a palliative-care model. Its algorithms use payer data and links to primary-care doctors to flag people "probably likely to die in the next year". Keeping them out of repeated ER visits gives a saner last year and lowers system costs "dramatically", a win for payer, system and patient.
- Best founders are mission-driven: Jonathan Bush and Todd Park (athenahealth); Todd and Ed Park then built Devoted Health, Medicare Advantage rebuilt from the bottom up with its own provider group of largely virtual primary-care doctors taking risk on outcomes ("Kaiser all virtual"), now "a multi-billion dollar amazing company". Brad Smith (Aspire) built CareBridge (value-based care in Medicaid), which Oak just sold. They make money "as a by product"; if you want healthcare just to get rich, "go do something else".
- GenAI: "there almost cannot be too much hype" about it. Less-trained staff using it can give diagnoses and treatment recommendations "as good or better than doctors", augmenting scarce primary-care doctors and specialists rather than replacing them. Claims and billing (revenue-cycle management) cost \~$500 billion a year and "can be completely streamlined".
- Cancer care: a patient not within 90 miles of a major health system won't know about "90% of the treatments". Virtual care from big cancer centres such as MSK, plus AI, can lift the quality of local oncologists.
- Fintech moves slowly because of compliance, behaviour and incumbents protecting income: in Connecticut, lawyers in the legislature keep a $1,000 mortgage-closing fee that a digital closing would save. Oak finds more opportunity in Latin America (crypto, stablecoins), which is building the digital infrastructure the US already has; its last four fintech deals were all there (Apr 2025).
- Downturns: "mistakes are repeatable". The dot-com bust and 2021–22 both had overheated prices, and entrepreneurs and investors "got sloppy". Make sure each company has a sustainable model and the cash to get through, then "cut deep and then build back" once, because repeated layoffs kill morale: "by the third one nobody believes in the company".
- Stay in the game: be greedy when others are fearful, since good companies are created every day. Silicon Valley was in a "massive depression" in 2022, then GenAI arrived in early 2023. Oak has "done our best sometimes when markets are in recession", when buyers focus on cutting costs; she ignores daily market noise and builds "fundamentally good companies".
