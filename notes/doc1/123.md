### 123. Joseph Edelman — Repeat surprise, perception vs reality and a 20% bet on Verona (Generating Alpha Podcast, Jun 2025)

*Founder and CEO of Perceptive Advisors, a healthcare-focused investment firm he started in 1999; a psychology major who got his first analyst job at \~32; his first podcast interview.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M0RjFqj9ExU)

**Takeaway:** A biotech stock turns on three questions (does the drug work, will it be approved, will it sell), and everything in between is perception. Edelman profits when consensus adjusts too slowly ("repeat surprise"), sizes by confidence rather than past prices, and (Jun 2025) had 20% of his fund in Verona, whose peak sales he puts well above the Street's.

- "Repeat surprise" came from shorting Cambridge Bioscience. Its five-minute HIV test was a "stupid idea": "What you want is complete accuracy." Two analysts forecast "like 25 million and 30 million" of first-year sales vs his under $1M. First-quarter sales were \~$50,000, yet consensus only fell to \~$14M (his estimate: $800,000).
- He said sell at 15 and wrongly moved to hold at \~4; it went bankrupt "at zero" and the CEO and CFO went to jail. Repeat surprises come from "the bias of the bulls" plus "the liberalism of the bear": even his estimate wasn't low enough.
- It works upward too: Amgen had "18 positive surprises" in a row as Epogen beat forecasts. When analysts tried to raise numbers, the company called to say "you're too high you're going to embarrass yourself", and bears treated each beat as a one-off. Markets are more efficient now, but "you still see it".
- Three questions: "does a drug work" (Phase 3), "will it be approved" (the PDUFA date) and "will it sell enough to justify a higher price". "Everything else is perception of those things in between"; "short-term is perception long-term is reality". A drug you think is $1bn can easily be $500M.
- Catalysts pay only when perception and reality differ: "If everybody thinks the drug's going to work, you know, drug stock may not go up on positive results." In "a pretty long bare market for biotech" (Jun 2025) you can't "just say buy biotech"; own commercial names beating numbers or developmental ones with "a very good chance" of working.
- Since \~a year ago every analyst builds "long-term 10 years income models", which he compares. They are "100% wrong by definition" but show which side of the price you're on: if an analyst sees a $2bn drug in a stock valued at $1bn, "if it's a billion and a half it still may be a good buy." He uses them for weighting.
- Valuation: a DCF for single-product companies or short patent lives, where a P/E misleads. Earnings of $1, $1.20, $1.50 might get a 20 P/E, but $1.20, $1.50 then 50 cents "might end up being a 5p". Multi-drug companies (e.g. Nuvalent, a holding) can get \~20x when profitable, or DCF plus pipeline value; a pipeline has "negative value" if management is "throwing their money away, which happens all the time".
- The ideal stock rises short term (e.g. quarterly beats) and is worth far more long term: "those are hard to come by." When they diverge, weight the long term: an early-stage name 70% likely to work long term but "5050" short term after a trial hiccup? "So maybe I reduce the position."
- "buy low, sell high. Throw that out. That's just a bias." Ask only whether it's worth more now, with "relentless objectivity", and resize on new information: "you can go from 80% confident to 60%" without selling it all.
- Pharmacyclics \[?\]: bought at $1.50 and sold at $6.50 on one bad data point, on advice from his former mentee Wayne Rothbaum \[?\] (told in the book For Blood and Money). It hit \~$10, he bought back only \~half, and "two years later it ended up $272"; even the blue-sky target had been $18. Rothbaum later sold a company with a better, similar drug for $7bn.
- Sizing is "an art": relative risk vs return across 200+ positions. Small early-stage stakes matter: "if you have 50 basis points or 100 basis points in a company like that, you could be a 10bagger", unlike owning 200 big pharmas and becoming "an index". He passed on software that sizes from upside/downside odds with a cap (e.g. 15%).
- Verona (Jun 2025): a 20% position, his first that size in "eight, 10 years". He first dismissed its COPD drug as \~$500M. After approval the \~$20 stock reflected "maybe 700 million in peak sales"; Trinity's market research said $3bn. Doctor checks against Trelegy, the most-prescribed COPD drug at \~one-sixth the price, implied \~$15bn, and the CEO said "it wouldn't be unreasonable to think it's 10 billion".
- Perceptive assumes "minimum $3 billion peak, minimum 20 bucks a share in earnings". The stock went 20 to 100, but the most conservative DCF is "still like 120", and it holds its value over 5–6 years on "under two billion" of peak sales. At $3bn it's a \~10% annual return, "so maybe it shouldn't be 20%". He doesn't sell more because he expects a beat next quarter; the Street is at \~$3–3.5bn vs Perceptive's ≥$5bn, and the CEO says "it takes a while for analysts to catch up".
- Commercial names should beat consensus. A miss worries him, because a company with a winning drug "should have been able to sort of manage expectations" as Amgen did. Verona's management are "chirpy" and "totally unafraid of expectations for the quarter".
