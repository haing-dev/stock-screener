### 124. John Evans — Beam Therapeutics: base editing and the platform path to genetic cures (RTW Investments LP, Mar 2026)

*CEO of Beam Therapeutics, a base- and prime-editing company, interviewed by Stephanie Sirota on the RTW podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nhATgF--934)

**Takeaway:** Gene editing has moved from cutting DNA to rewriting single letters, and the FDA now lets a proven editor-and-delivery platform show only "the 5% that's new" for each new medicine. Beam's lead drug, BEAM-302 for Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, has FDA alignment on an accelerated path, and the company had $1.25 billion of capital and runway to mid-2029 (Mar 2026).

- After small molecules and proteins, genetic medicines are the third era: "incredibly programmable", with "the possibility of a one-time cure". Viral gene therapy only adds missing DNA, and viruses are "hard to make, they're hard to control, they're not very programmable".
- Original CRISPR targets precisely but only cuts, "like having scissors": good for switching a gene off, poor at repair. Base editing (Beam's original technology) rewrites single letters, which cause "about half of all mutations that cause disease"; prime editing does short rewrites. Inserting a whole gene is not yet possible.
- Delivery is the limiter: editors reach the liver "very reliably", plus blood and immune cells, but not yet brain, muscle, heart or lung. He expects "an inflection in the delivery technologies" in coming years.
- In vivo beats ex vivo: editing cells outside the body works, but cost of goods is "very significant" and the transplant is hard on patients, so it suits the sickest. Beam bet on lipid nanoparticles, proven by Alnylam (short RNA) and Moderna (mRNA) and dosed in billions of people counting vaccines: "a couple hour infusion outpatient procedure".
- Engineering, not discovery: an A base editor turns an A into a G "every time". Change the guide RNA and you have a new medicine with the same manufacturing and tolerability: "We add up a lot of little changes you end up with sort of an exponential curve."
- FDA platform path: watch "the things that didn't change" through administration changes; the support is bipartisan and started under Peter Marks, who served under Trump 1 and Biden. The FDA now says, in effect, "maybe you shouldn't have to do the 95% again, every time... You should just show me the 5% that's new." Without that, "you would be starting from scratch every single time".
- Proof of the model: doctors at CHOP and the University of Pennsylvania used a known lipid nanoparticle, a known base editor and a custom guide RNA to treat a baby with a life-threatening urea cycle disorder. The baby is home and has "at most a mild form" of the condition.
- BEAM-302 (Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, \~100,000 severe US patients): a 2–3-hour infusion carries the editor to liver cells, fixes the single-letter misspelling and washes out within a couple of days. Normal protein protects the lungs, the toxic mutant protein stops building up in the liver, and the change is "durable" for life. Diagnosis trails symptoms by up to a decade, and many patients sit undiagnosed in COPD clinics.
- Indication choice: go where current treatments are not curative and the disease still progresses (sickle cell, Alpha-1), since a transformative result motivates patients, physicians and regulators. "Long term, I think we save the system money."
- Safety: "safety first", and the real answer is "time and data", with about 6–7 years of CRISPR-edited patients so far. Biased and unbiased assays check for off-target edits across 3 billion bases ("a fraction of a thumb drive"), and observed changes are well below the hundreds of genetic changes from a day on the beach.
- AI: Beam's computational-biology, automation and robotics teams generate proprietary data for machine learning in editor design, off-target prediction and regulatory filings. But AI enthusiasm "gets a little beyond itself": "I think we only understand 20% of biology", so AI must loop tightly with a wet lab.
- Commercial maths: first indications are large enough, with sickle cell and Alpha-1 at about 100,000 people each and PKU at 20,000: "You'll get to some peak revenue. You'll be there for a while. And then there's some tail." Big markets invite competition, so the moat is a "multi-factorial recipe": technology, IP, team, delivery, own manufacturing (North Carolina), capital and the best products.
- Balance sheet (Mar 2026): market cap "just shy of $3 billion" (per Sirota), $1.25 billion of capital at the last report, and runway to mid-2029, "over three years". The FDA aligned with Beam on an accelerated path to market for BEAM-302 based on data already shown.
- Lessons: "stack the odds in your favor" with the highest probability of success, remove risk early and shorten timelines. His hardest trade-off as CEO is breadth vs focus: "focus on getting one flywheel running first before you're trying to spin ten of them."
