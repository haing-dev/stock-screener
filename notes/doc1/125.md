### 125. Peter Kolchinsky & Josh Cohen — What is a drug worth? QALYs, missing values and Medicare price-setting (RA Capital Management, Nov 2021)

*Kolchinsky (portfolio manager, RA Capital) hosts RA/TV Episode 28: a COVID update with colleagues Katie and Mike, then health economist Josh Cohen (deputy director of the Center for the Evaluation of Value and Risk in Health at Tufts Medical Center; co-author of The Right Price) on how to value drugs. Recorded in Nov 2021, the day after the CDC recommended Pfizer's vaccine for 5–11-year-olds; uploaded Mar 2026.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0VMD0Ntxspc)

**Takeaway:** A drug is worth the quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) it adds times what people will pay for a QALY. Conventional math rounds hard-to-measure benefits to zero and so calls most drugs overpriced; if US payers set prices on that math, investors will stop funding new drugs.

- COVID update (Nov 2021), Katie: Pfizer's 5–11 vaccine (10 microgram dose vs 30 for adults) was authorized on a 4,700-person study with 90.7% efficacy and no cases of severe COVID, myocarditis or pericarditis. Kolchinsky: school quarantines create "a very strong incentive to vaccinate your kid". Mike: in a small UK/Spain study (under 500 patients), mixing vaccine types for the booster gave higher neutralizing antibodies.
- Rule of three for safety data, raised when Kolchinsky asked if the trial could rule out myocarditis: zero cases in 3,000 patients only excludes a rate of about one in 1,000 ("You divide the number of patients uh by three"). Excluding the one-in-8,000 rate seen in men in their 20s would need "zero cases out of 24,000" children, so small trials can't rule out rare side effects.
- Cohen: "price is like the air traffic control of the economy." Consumer Reports ranks options against each other; health economists ask what a therapy is worth outright. Some payers act on the answer: the UK's NICE says the value "is X and we're not paying more for it". Cohen would rather assessments stay information, "a public good" for payers, as Consumer Reports doesn't dictate what you buy.
- QALYs: a year in perfect health counts 1 and death 0. Five extra years at 60% of full health is 3 QALYs. Weights come from trade-off questions: a patient indifferent between 5 years with a painful disability and 3 healthy years values the disabled years at 0.6.
- Price per QALY (Cohen's illustration): 1,000 car buyers each pay $500 for airbags that cut each one's death risk by one in 1,000. That saves 10 life-years for $500,000, or "$50,000 per quality adjusted life year". Small risk reductions across a population are used because a dying person "will pay anything to live".
- Count net costs and spillovers: a $100,000 drug that replaces a $30,000 procedure really costs "the extra $70,000" (Kolchinsky), and benefits to caregivers and families count too. When ICER began valuing drugs (\~2015) it looked only at health and cost; its pre-pandemic framework added a societal analysis, which in "a fair number of cases" adds little. Its quick first take on Gilead's remdesivir still left societal benefits out.
- Cohen: leaving out a benefit because it is hard to measure means "We're implicitly saying the value is zero." In stocks you wouldn't assume Google goes to zero or becomes infinitely valuable: "You try and get it in the middle."
- The cost of undervaluing: it sends "weak signals to industry" before the next crisis, such as antibiotic resistance (Cohen). Existing drugs won't be uninvented, but new ones won't be funded (Kolchinsky): "you're asking me to invest in building a home that's going to cost me $400,000 to construct, and you're telling me that I'm going to have to sell it for $200,000".
- Kolchinsky: the UK sets low prices with this math, industry "sometimes caves", and the UK still gets new drugs: "I've got two words for you. United States." Cohen: if the US imports the math and uses it "as a bargaining tool" rather than to seek the truth, "we're going to end up losing out on innovation".
- Value vs price (Cohen): an estimate based on the market's reaction to Pfizer's Nov 2020 vaccine results put a pandemic-ending vaccine at "$5 trillion, something like that", or $600–700 a person. Kolchinsky: that is \~10x the $20–50 charged. Charging $400 would be "a public relations disaster" (Cohen), and budget limits can "choke off access" to something that is worth it.
- Affordability (Kolchinsky, Nov 2021): US branded-drug spending is \~1.4% of GDP and \~10% of health spending, which is 18–19% of GDP. Cohen: even at 15% the economy copes; "it's not like the system is grinding to a halt".
- Kolchinsky's slide of dozens of conventional analyses (list prices, many values "rounded to zero") shows "almost all these drugs" priced above the formula's "fair" price. Cohen: that "makes a great meme", so message one is to get the math right, with evidence that makes the extra values "scientifically defensible". Kolchinsky's example: a smallpox-drug stockpile (\~$300 million a year) saves zero QALYs, so conventional math says it costs infinity per QALY and shouldn't be funded.
- Medicare (Nov 2021), Cohen: Congress was near a deal for Medicare to "negotiate at least some drug prices", and without accepted value assessment the basis will be "make the drug price lower". The plan allowed market pricing for "nine years for small molecule drugs 12 years for biologics", then negotiation, and "we have to assume it'll be as low as they can make it".
- Competition already keeps prices below value: with a cheaper "me too drug" around, "what you pay for a drug is less than what it's worth". Kolchinsky hopes generalized cost-effectiveness analysis (GCEA: caregivers, disease severity, herd immunity) will show the market "seems to be functioning". Drug CEOs should be able to say why "our medicine will be worth" what they hope to charge, before policymakers price it with conventional math.
