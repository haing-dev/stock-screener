### 127. Peter Kolchinsky — Ideas that refuse to die: landscape maps, AI's slow loop, China and price controls (Precision Signals, Aug 2026)

*Founder and managing partner of RA Capital (200+ staff and over $14bn AUM per the host, Aug 2026), interviewed over two sessions by former FDA official Sean Khozin \[?\].* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PhPpenTtGMo)

**Takeaway:** RA Capital's edge is diligence done before the company walks in: 100+ competitive-landscape maps kept current, so its first meeting is like others' second or third. Kolchinsky sees AI drug design as a very slow learning loop, China's trial capacity as a bigger force for R&D, and US price controls as the main threat to innovation.

- The 2001 genomics bubble taught that "there's not that much value in a platform technology"; the value is in the drug. Gene Logic \[?\] would "crater and die", while Incyte and Exelixis pivoted to their own lead drugs and survived.
- His first mistake: owning deCODE genetics because it had genetically profiled Iceland and was "cheaper than they once were". "Well, that's a terrible investment thesis": he lost money and switched to asking "what would actually make a stock go up".
- From the mid-2002 post-bubble low he ended that year flat on Rich Aldrich's money (started at $4M), more than tripled it in 2003, then made only \~14% in 2004: "to become an investor you have to be humbled at some point".
- Landscape maps (started \~15 years ago): a pitched lung-cancer drug faces "70 drug programs aimed at lung cancer. Like what are the odds this is the best one?" RA keeps over 100 maps covering many hundreds of diseases, plus tool maps (ADCs, AAV gene therapy). Like a chessboard, a map shows why one CDK4/6 inhibitor "is better than the other ones".
- The value is in the upkeep: "We actually don't look at our own maps all that often"; updating them keeps the team's knowledge current. RA maintains maps and experts in fields with nothing investable (Alzheimer's) so it can pounce when "someday something will crack". From \~2011–12 the maps showed which private companies would make good public ones, and RA reached "through the IPO window" with crossover rounds.
- The edge: "our first meeting with the company was closer to what somebody else's second or third meeting might be." That let RA invest in things "that took other people maybe a little bit longer to get comfortable with".
- Old maps record failures: compare today's map with one from 5–10 years ago to see "all the stuff that's failed you in the last decade and why". Recently the FDA told an RA company that another program had confirmed its mechanism's risk, so it killed the rare-disease drug: "way better than burning what money the company had on something that was never going to work."
- Speed comes from trust: RA trains front-line analysts until the PMs can approve on the written thesis. Making them "prove everything" means "you're going to be slow, you're going to lose deals". Kolchinsky and co-PM Raj Shah add most value pushing for "a much bigger check" when a name is special. Notes put "the bottom line at the top".
- AI drug design: a pitch to lift the success rate from 10% to 20% "would be worth a ton", but proving it needs \~25 programs (five working, not two) and \~7 years with the model frozen. It is "the absolute slowest learning loop that you could, you know, apply AI to": AI makes great molecules faster, but biology, unlike physics, rarely works as theorized.
- Past failures set the bar: knocking down only the mutant allele in Huntington's, or in C9 ALS, hasn't shown clear benefit, so a new molecule that does it isn't "good enough". "Huntington's to me is a little more like depression": he'd want a large controlled study. RA loses money on many experiments: "We don't consider them a loss. We consider them the price".
- China's trial capacity, built over 10 years and "especially 5 years", "is more impactful to the pace of R&D than even AI": "AI is not going to change the rate of enrollment". The US has "about 4% of the world's patients", and a Phase 2 lupus study there is now "near impossible"; China has over four times the people and more patients open to trials.
- He won't back a "not quite as good fast follower to the Chinese best-in-class drug": patients will demand the better drug, and "I don't believe protectionism holds." Fast followers are what patents intend: twice-daily Byetta, with little weight loss, gave way to once-weekly Ozempic and Mounjaro. No need for equity in Chinese biotechs either: their value is "largely in its assets", and licensing the one drug you want "is getting the best 20% of that company".
- The main threat is domestic: "the greatest threat to Americans getting drugs that don't exist yet comes from Americans", who vote for price controls. Drug models assumed "about 14 years of protection after launch" and market pricing; Medicare negotiation's "nine-year pill penalty" breaks that: "What is the value of a patent if you can't negotiate your own price?" CEOs who answer "I don't get political" are, to him, not leaders.
- FDA: investors need an agency that "ultimately shifts slowly"; when the basis for accelerated approval shifts radically inside it, "we can't project forward". His nonprofit No Patient Left Behind wrote letters on instability under Makary (staff leaving "in droves") and backed Rick Pazdur, who "modernized oncology at the FDA", for commissioner; the FDA had no commissioner at the time (2026).
