### 128. Peter Kolchinsky — Create your own luck: early trades, sell discipline and the drug-pricing fight (Harvard Biotech Club, May 2025)

*Co-founder and managing partner of RA Capital (\~$11bn under management per the hosts, May 2025) and co-founder of the Harvard Biotech Club, as debut guest of the club's student podcast, recorded live at the Broad Institute.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gU9bBaEhgzw)

**Takeaway:** Two habits "carried me for a decade": confidence that diligence can show where the market is wrong, and treating every day you hold a stock as a day you choose to buy it. On policy, he argues affordability is an insurance problem, and the IRA's nine-year price control cuts the value of a new small-molecule drug roughly in half.

- Rich Aldrich's brief when he handed Kolchinsky his stock account: "All you got to know is how much cash they've got and how fast they're burning it and what they're going to achieve of any significance at all before they run out of money." Aldrich skipped control-arm quibbles and asked whether there was "a line of sight to how this will ultimately drive profits".
- He then lost 25% in the first half of 2002 (the market was "down like 45%") by buying companies for how much cheaper they were than before, such as deCODE genetics: "That is not how you make money in anything. The question is why should this stock go up?" The reframing recovered the losses by year-end, and he tripled the money in 2003.
- Career-making trade (late 2002): South Africa's health ministry "declared that HIV does not cause AIDS" and halted trials, so Triangle Pharmaceuticals' stock cratered. With a PhD on HIV he knew better and bought; Gilead soon bought Triangle "for a big premium", and its drug became one of Truvada's two components.
- The lesson: when a stock falls on a million shares traded, "that's not just a million shares sold, that's a million shares bought." Other investors may be "smarter than you", but that "doesn't mean they're right"; skip the work and "you'll certainly never find that undervalued stock" (the $100 bill on the sidewalk).
- Sell discipline: "every day you hold a stock is a day you're choosing to buy it". The dog test: if your dog stepped on the keyboard and sold it, would you buy it back? Bought at $10, worth $30, now $25: "would you be buying it at 25 if the upside is only to 30?" "So if I wouldn't buy it, I shouldn't hold it." The same test cures holding a loser until it gets back to cost.
- RA was a pure hedge fund for a decade: first private deals \~2009–10, regular from \~2012, board seats from \~2013, then Raven (RA Ventures) to form companies. Biotech now has two kinds of investors, he says: company formers (Atlas pivoted to this years ago) and crossover investors; for a "judger of companies", "IPO is not an exit. You're going to be managing public positions."
- Access is half the job: "You can identify a company, but that company won't let you invest in them. Then there's absolutely no utility to identifying them." Because "everybody's got money", RA wins allocations by offering tools and services built from 200+ portfolio companies. And beware deals "where you're the only one who likes them".
- Portfolio math: "since most of our investments fail I need the few that succeed to be really profitable". So he told a portfolio company that wanted a low list price with no rebates to launch high, leaving room for "a big fat rebate to PBMs because otherwise they won't cover you." It launched "with the highest price", paid rebates and won coverage.
- IRA: biologics face price controls at 13 years, close to the "about 14 years" drugs average before going generic; small molecules face them at 9 years. The lost time on the market is "worth about half of the entire value of a small molecule drug" for diseases of aging, since the controls apply to Medicare and Medicaid.
- The industry's real rent-seeking: since the 1990s it has learned to "milk those drugs for profits long after their patents have expired" with biologics, which are hard to copy. A biosimilar of a gene therapy such as Zolgensma would need "an undoable trial", so it is "a natural monopoly" that should be regulated. Congress missed this "legit tumor" and cut small molecules instead.
- "Affordability is purely a function of insurance." Price-controlling a $100,000 drug to Canada's $30,000 doesn't help someone who can't pay a $5,000 deductible. A drug is expensive for its patent life and then cheap forever, while "Hospitals and clinics and doctors do not go generic."
- Value math: cost-effectiveness thresholds (say $150,000 per QALY) ignore generics. Paying $200,000 for 14 years, then "like a hundred bucks a year" as a generic, is "more like a mortgage". The IRA's climate models looked out 300 years; those used for drug price controls "only looked out 10 years". His nonprofit No Patient Left Behind pushes generalized cost-effectiveness analysis, which also counts caregivers and productivity (Trikafta in cystic fibrosis).
- Career advice (the title): "create your own luck" by doing things school doesn't require, such as a student-group project, and "see something through to completion". "Don't tell... You show it": Aldrich met him because he had written The Entrepreneur's Guide to a Biotech Startup, and wanted someone who returns "40 hours of productivity" per hour of management.
- Screen implication: Aldrich's three questions map onto hc_clinical. The runway rule (B2) covers cash and burn; whether a value-creating milestone lands before the cash runs out must be checked by hand.
