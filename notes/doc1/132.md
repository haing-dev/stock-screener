### 132. Rod Wong — Empty rooms: how RTW underwrites, sizes and learns from its losers (Capital Allocators, Apr 2023)

*Founder and managing partner of RTW Investments (80 professionals, $6bn AUM at the time), interviewed by Ted Seides on Capital Allocators about biotech as an "empty room" that most investors avoid.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IVtrbtDSbjY)

**Takeaway:** RTW hunts for high-probability drugs with asymmetric payoffs, ideally found early and backed for years until they become 3- to 10-baggers. It is wrong \~40% of the time, so the craft is sizing: no more than a 5% loss on even the best idea, and big positions only where an approved product supports the value.

- Context: biotech was "18 months into a biotech bear Market", so valuations were cheap. The longer case is innovation: the genome's first draft (2000) cost "300 million dollars" and now costs "a few hundred dollars", and drug-making technologies have "more than tripled" (cell and gene therapy, gene editing, ADCs, protein degraders, RNA), counting only those that have produced drugs.
- Price vs volume: drugs capture value well through pricing, unlike tech, where "90 some percent of the value" goes to consumers. Even with price pressure and competition replacing monopolies, going from "40-ish drugs a year" to 80 "should be a tremendous amount of value creation".
- Strategy since founding: a "moderately concentrated portfolio" of bigger bets where RTW can judge the odds, and size, better than the market. High-probability ideas "don't grow on trees" (only "50 drugs approved a year" in the US), so it accepts more single-stock volatility. He says that "if Warren Buffett were to ever invest in biotech" he might use RTW's framework, because "we're fundamentally value oriented".
- Winners: the top 10 winners since inception include "over 10 approved drugs", most found early, sometimes while private or still in academic hands. Returns come from "finding things early when they're misunderstood or simply no one's paying attention", getting convicted and backing them through several stages, "call it five years until you get three Baggers five Baggers ten Baggers".
- Losers: RTW is wrong in the high 30s to 40-some percent of cases, so the key is "making those losses as small as possible". Its second-largest loss was a short on MAP Pharmaceuticals, whose single-use migraine inhaler RTW was "very confident you couldn't manufacture". MAP sold itself to Allergan, and the drug was rejected a month after the deal closed. Lesson: "do not underestimate what Savvy business people are capable of"; RTW "slashed the max position size" on shorts.
- Sizing: on its very best idea (some years none qualifies) RTW is "willing to take up to a five percent loss if we're flat out wrong", judged on the fundamental and market worst case; only a couple of positions ever lost more than 5%. Positions of 5–10%+ need "some kind of Base business value support": "they sell a drug on the market and we're playing for the second drug". Single-product development-stage names, with 90–100% downside, "almost universally will be less than five percent". The flagship ran a little over 70% net long (\~90% long, \~20% short) within net 30–90%.
- Re-underwrite at every step: whatever the mark, ask what the next key event is, its probability and its asymmetry. In the worst quarter of the sell-off "you could have been right on everything" and still seen the stock fall, because it was a "source of liquidity" like the rest of biotech. Judge what share of a 10x outcome a stock deserves at each stage (comparables, judgment, macro) and resize as it moves.
- Private deals are a "farm team": \~80% of the business is public and \~20% private, with 10–20 private deals in a typical year, from crossover rounds to Series A company creation. The goal is that they go public; years of diligence and knowing management let RTW hold "a five or a ten percent position".
- Edge from work others skip. Statistics: constipation drugs with impressive one-month Phase 2 p-values kept failing six-month Phase 3s; RTW's study of the literature found "phase twos a lot of them work and phase threes they all fail every single one". His hypothesis: over longer trials, placebo patients go to the pharmacy's constipation aisle. Commercial: for rare-disease launches RTW counts patients at treatment centers and mines insurance and public genetic data; "it doesn't take a genius to do it but it takes a lot of effort".
- Management comes in three buckets: a few teams can "sell ice to an Eskimo" and carry a marginal drug to commercial success, a few will botch execution, and most are solid and just need support.
- Who is advantaged: big pharma faces another wave of patent expiries in the back half of the decade on a bigger revenue base. Biotechs with a potential blockbuster in a strategic area are "the kings in this cycle", with good access to capital. Worst placed is a company about to launch a sub-$1bn peak-sales drug (say half a billion) outside strategic areas: it must raise "two to three hundred million dollars for the next 24 months", which RTW sees as a selective opportunity.
- Financing rule of thumb: "you can do an equity offering for like 10 to 20 percent of a company's market cap at any given time", but some companies need "three four x that amount". With equity costly, RTW assembles "multiple pieces of a deal" to fund a launch or a Phase 3.
- Favorite bets are "lock and key": a tumor known to be driven by a mutation, or a rare disease missing a gene whose protein can be supplied or replaced; "that's probably gonna work", and such bets "continue to be very rewarding and have high odds".
- Why firms fail: crossing $1bn of assets is when lone-wolf habits and missing structure start to break them, and "falling in love with science and not doing the risk management part of the job properly" ("doctors can't balance their checkbooks").
