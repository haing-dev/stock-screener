### 133. Peter Kolchinsky, Raj Shah, Jake Simson \[?\] & Tess Cameron — Semper Maior: time to reboot biotech (RA Capital Management, Apr 2026)

*RA Capital managing partner Peter Kolchinsky and colleagues Raj Shah, Jake Simson \[?\] and Tess Cameron discuss their "biotech reboot" article with audience Q&A in an RA Futurecast webinar recorded in early 2023 (a poll asks whether biotech "started 2023" on firmer footing).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8p6W3jOzn4w)

**Takeaway:** The biotech financing "tsunami" disappears once companies are split by specialist ownership and weighted by size: specialists' share of a year's financing needs is about 4.4% of their portfolios, and below-cash stocks are only 5% of the weight. The fixes RA wants are an honest IPO process run by management, low-fee financing, and countering cost-effectiveness math with a fuller version.

- Financing "tsunami" myth: the scary stat (many companies with high burn-to-market-cap and under two years of cash) lumps everything together. Split "core" companies that specialists own from "peripheral" ones they don't, and most companies that must raise are peripheral. If specialists funded their pro-rata share of every financing needed in the next year, it would take "about 4.4%" of their portfolios on average (from 13F analyses), mostly \~1% a quarter: "It feels more like a stream".
- Strategics agree: 86% of M&A dollars went to core companies. Strip out deals irrelevant to development-stage biotech and 2022 had more acquisitions than most years, and on absolute premium paid it was "probably the fourth best year out of these nine" tracked. Premiums rose with market cap, perhaps as buyers pay for de-risked assets, but "it's just seven companies": a hypothesis, not a conclusion.
- Below cash: 214 of 660 companies, "a third", trade below cash, but "they're weighted at only 5%". Kolchinsky: "If they're trading below cash, odds are they're probably not going to go to zero"; some become reverse-merger shells or liquidate, and even a total loss is "5% further downside". Shah: many "ran their experiments, raised hundreds of millions of dollars through crossover IPOs" and saw the lead fail; he hopes those teams use the cash to buy better assets.
- Peripheral companies are "a call option" for specialists, not a duty: if they can't raise, staff move on and weak assets die, "a healthy part of the market". To an attendee's objection that "predictivity is low", Kolchinsky replied "I just don't think that good assets die". Investing is a diagnostic with an ROC curve: missing nothing would cost too much productivity, with Congress squeezing industry rewards.
- The IRA's treatment of small molecules for diseases of aging, "really any NDA path drug", is "a terrible bill" that is changing where investors allocate dollars; investors will say "no thank you" to small-molecule oncology (early 2023). But the below-cash stocks were down before it passed.
- Rates hit NPVs, but "it's a matter of degree" and don't change the sector's upside from beaten-down levels unless rates go "way higher". Specialist funds could see outflows, but not because of peripheral names, only $40bn of a $332bn universe. After being "squeezed now for two years", biotech is "much closer to what we'd think of as a floor value", so think twice before selling it for liquidity (early 2023).
- Check the stats: a claim that low-short-interest stocks led the early-2023 rally (so "real" long buying) vanished once two outliers were removed and companies were split at $200M market cap. Sub-$200M names "really don't matter" to institutional portfolios, so ask for stats split by market cap, with averages and medians.
- New modalities: an attendee argued unsolved delivery will keep productivity low for 5–10 years. Kolchinsky: RA backed those bets knowing delivery was hard, and RNAi went from undeliverable, to the liver, to beyond. Markets "drip feed things with low probabilities of success until they see that they're working", then fund them fully. RA: weighted, the riskiest modalities are a modest slice of its portfolio and future funding, "worth preserving that optionality in case it works".
- Series I IPO: an IPO is "just your entree into the public markets", usually into fairly illiquid trading, so start with a small group that "won't drop you". Management controls allocations, trades a promise of allocation for real price discovery while testing the waters, and confirms every order itself; RA's calls to peers revealed orders that differed from bankers' spreadsheets. Only credible orders count, and they get "unflippable amounts".
- Not credible: funds that don't hold biotech, skipped diligence, bid $20M when their largest biotech position is "single digits", never hold a stock more than a couple of quarters, or ask for $3–4M while managing over $1bn. Credible buyers bid early and name a price, "functionally a verbal term sheet". Maximizing last-minute demand invites speculators: "We've seen IPOs that were 10 times covered that then broke deal price."
- Fees: "what is the point of liquidity without price attached to it" (a colleague). Kolchinsky: the company already pays banks a \~6% fee, and allocating underpriced shares to flippers adds "this hidden extra fee", like $200 Ticketmaster tickets scalped for $1,000. At-the-market (ATM) offerings cost \~1% vs 6% for a follow-on, as little as 0.25% for ATM reverse-inquiry blocks: "fees can be low when you negotiate".
- Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) is an "insidious" form of math that tells the world innovation isn't worth its prices. Each kind of value is "a petal on a flower" (productivity, community and caregiver spillover, reassurance for the healthy, severity), and CEA strips them away and prices a drug as if it never goes generic, like an adviser rejecting a mortgage because payments exceed rent. Replace it with generalized CEA (GCEA).
- Example: ICER put Trikafta at over $1 million per QALY; No Patient Left Behind and health economists added back generic entry, severity and future patients, and it became more cost-effective. Eisai's US pricing release for Leqembi set out societal value vs price, a first "in the entire history of our industry" to his knowledge, though it ignored price controls after 13 years. "executives need to use GCA to convey their value proposition before others use conventional CA to do it for them."
- Screen implication: specialist ownership, measurable from 13F filings, separates "core" from "peripheral" biotechs; RA finds most financing risk in companies no specialist owns, and 86% of M&A dollars went to core ones.
