### 134. Peter Kolchinsky, Risa Stack, Ramin Farzaneh-Far \[?\] & Nadim Shohdy \[?\] — COVID pills, and how venture partners and EIRs build biotechs at RA (RA Capital Management, Dec 2021)

*RA/TV Episode 29: Kolchinsky (RA Capital portfolio manager) covers oral COVID antivirals and Omicron with colleagues Peter and Katie, then talks with venture partners Risa Stack (ex-Kleiner Perkins and GE) and Ramin Farzaneh-Far \[?\] (ex-chief medical officer of Ra Pharmaceuticals) and entrepreneur in residence (EIR) Nadim Shohdy \[?\] (ex-NYU drug-discovery accelerator). Recorded in Dec 2021 as the year's last episode; uploaded Apr 2026.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hXYkVvU2ASg)

**Takeaway:** Interim data can flatter a drug: Merck's molnupiravir fell from a 50% to a 30% risk reduction at the final readout, while Pfizer's protease inhibitor showed 85%. In company creation, RA pairs operators with its analysts to reach "a quick no", test whether an idea is really a company, and find a platform's "killer application" before funding it.

- Molnupiravir (Merck): the interim Phase 3 (early Oct 2021) showed a 50% cut in hospitalization and death. The full data fell to 30%, and patients enrolled after the interim looked "like zero or worse". Blended into "a 30% risk reduction", it "makes it sound like oh yeah it really works". An FDA panel still voted 13 to 10 in favor; Merck's defense was that the interim was the prespecified primary analysis. Genotoxicity can't be ruled out without "thousands of patients".
- Paxlovid (Pfizer) is a protease inhibitor revived from a program shelved after the early-2000s SARS outbreak. It enrolled faster than Merck's trial and showed an 85% interim cut in hospitalization or death, so colleague Peter expects molnupiravir "could become absolute obsolete or near obsolete quickly". Both trials excluded vaccinated people because they were "designed for success"; Kolchinsky: limiting an 85% drug to the unvaccinated would be "absolutely terrible and insane", since it rewards skipping the vaccine.
- Trial design decides outcomes: high-risk patients give higher event rates, and low-risk patients shrink the measurable benefit. Kolchinsky: after "really courting" Atea, Roche ran a COVID trial mixing high- and low-risk patients to enroll fast. It failed, possibly because low-risk patients "diluted the effect", and Roche "parted ways" with Atea, leaving it with the failed result. Payers may also say the number needed to treat in low-risk patients costs too much per life saved.
- What comes next (Kolchinsky): as in hepatitis C, expect cocktails pairing polymerase inhibitors such as Merck's and Atea's with protease inhibitors. The pills hit the virus's "internal machinery", which is "extremely well conserved" across coronaviruses, so they should work in a future pandemic, unlike antibodies and vaccines aimed at the more mutable outer shell.
- Match the drug to the setting: pills for a new infection or after exposure; long-acting injected antibodies for pre-exposure protection of the immunocompromised, one dose lasting "six to 12 months" (colleague Peter). Kolchinsky: a year of pills would cost "30 times, 60 times more than treating episodically", and Paxlovid's ritonavir booster interacts with the many drugs high-risk patients take.
- Omicron (Dec 2021): 50 mutations, 30 in the spike. With \~25% of South Africans vaccinated, the early data showed escape from natural immunity, not yet from vaccines; Delta was "around four-fold or so more resistant" and vaccines still worked. Kolchinsky sees an advantage in a strain-matched vaccine over Moderna's 100 microgram boost of the original: a 25–50 microgram dose would stretch manufacturing capacity and be better tolerated. Omicron-specific shots were expected "probably by March uh certainly April".
- Why RA hires operators: Kolchinsky has "never run an actual biotech tech company"; sitting on boards "makes me more like a movie critic than a director". Venture partners work on "four five half a dozen companies" at once, while the EIR title points more toward running one (Farzaneh-Far \[?\]). Both shape early companies, build teams and step in as interim executives.
- Shohdy \[?\] co-founded and ran NYU School of Medicine's drug-discovery accelerator, which ran "mini drug discovery projects" through CROs and consultants to de-risk academic science for investors and pharma. Of dozens of projects, "roughly a third of them were successfully partnered", a third were still in play and the rest missed milestones. At RA he triages very early science with the Raven incubator and works part-time at portfolio company Clear Creek Bio (small-molecule antivirals): "The earlier you go, the higher the risk".
- Farzaneh-Far \[?\], a cardiologist, ran large cardiovascular outcomes trials at Gilead, then worked at Akebia and spent four years as chief medical officer of Ra Pharmaceuticals (an early RA holding), which ran rare neuromuscular-disease trials, went public and was acquired in 2020. His typical week brings "two or three new companies" at seed or Series A, plus data rooms, interim roles and placing ex-pharma talent in portfolio companies. Most deals come through RA's network, but "two or three really good ones" came via his daughter's school and some are "a diamond in the rough" sent cold.
- Stack: PhD in cellular immunology, then seven years in private equity and 10 at Kleiner Perkins (started Veracyte; helped build Corthera and Trius). At GE her team started eight companies, including Menlo Microsystems and cell-therapy supply-chain software Vineti, born from a problem Kite and Juno brought to GE: "I ran a venture studio". Her test when starting a company: "is it a idea or is it a company?" Much inbound is "really an idea that needs a lot of work to be a company".
- RA's edge (Stack): unlike Kleiner, it "started as public company investors", sees a company's whole life cycle and is more data-driven. It can get to "a quick no", and the no comes with feedback, "here's what we're looking for"; some companies have come back with exactly that.
- Ownership and alignment (Kolchinsky): at 80% ownership a company is "basically you know us", and even a \~10% stake in a large position means "the fate of that company is intertwined with our fate", so RA lends staff as interim executives. RA employees can't own portfolio equity: the firm earns only fees on profits, and his director shares go to the fund's LPs. Operators get equity to align them, sometimes carved out of RA's own purchase when a company won't issue more.
- Farzaneh-Far \[?\]: a venture partner commits only when "three stars need to align": RA invests, the partner wants in, and the company wants the partner on its board or in an interim role. Deals fall apart: one company Stack brought in raised a huge SAFE note and no longer needed RA's money. Kolchinsky: the operators' and the investment team's views are meant as independent signals, and in 10 years RA will see whose instincts were right.
- Killer app first: Shohdy's team liked a new drug-discovery platform but judged it "needs a little bit more maturity" before RA points it at its "killer application". Kolchinsky's method: list every disease a capability could address, the standard of care and rival pipelines, and look for "a problem that will still persist even if everybody else is successful", with doable trials and predictive animal models. TechAtlas may "boil the ocean" and find nothing; investors who fund first and "figure out later what to do" can end up with "a tool waiting for, you know, the killer app".
