### 138. Bob Prince — Coding how the world works, and the market bet inside every business (Generating Alpha Podcast, Mar 2025)

*Co-CIO of Bridgewater Associates, which he joined in 1986 after heading the treasury department at First National Bank of Tulsa; a career and philosophy interview.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tczx0l9-Kv8)

**Takeaway:** Bridgewater's edge came from treating the economy and markets as one system, coding that understanding into rules and living with the results, and from separating a business's operating margin from the market bets hidden in it. His outlook (Mar 2025): modern mercantilism threatens the balance policymakers had just restored.

- Career logic: banks sit at the hub of the economy and industries are the spokes. Start at the hub and you can still move out along a spoke; start on a spoke and, as he put it, "I can't go back to the center". At 24 he was running First National Bank of Tulsa's funding area, treating the balance sheet as "a set of portfolios".
- Bridgewater idea one, in Ray Dalio's phrase: "the merger of an economist and a trader", integrated by "systemizing the fundamental understanding". Dalio had built interest-rate, stock and gold systems whose readouts went out daily in "the wire" (today's Daily Observations). Prince admired coding how you think the world works "and now you're going to live with it".
- Idea two, the risk-management plan: businesses "are really comprised of two things. One is like an operating margin and the other is a bet on the markets", often taken unknowingly or because it lifts short-term profits. "a jeweler thinks they're in the jewelry business, but they're long the gold market too", so ask how much profit comes from the bet and how much from the operating business.
- He lived it: near a Caterpillar plant in Illinois, a super-strong dollar meant layoffs as the Japanese undercut it; Tulsa had 3% unemployment (vs 18% in Boston) until oil fell, and then "the corner drugstore is going to go under because oil prices fell". Bridgewater's answer: "get off the planet", able to go long or short any market instead of being "a sitting duck".
- Kodak (1991): pension pioneer Rusty Olsen feared that in a deflationary depression "everything's going to go down together" and asked about $100 million in 25-year-duration bonds to get the price action of $500 million. After a weekend keying in railroad bond yields back to the 1860s, Bridgewater sent a study a couple of weeks later that Prince still hands new hires as "the gold standard".
- The Kodak mandate was "basically the first alpha overlay": 25-year duration via 10-year bond futures at 2–2.5x leverage, duration movable from 40 years to zero, currency ±100%, at any point on any country's yield curve; a liability hedge plus alpha overlay. The first account, a $5 million World Bank bond mandate in 1987, was "the hardest one".
- First principles: seek "the right answer, not what everybody else does". When everybody else is in one place and you're alone in another, "if you're actually right, then in time the world comes to you"; doing that repeatedly built Bridgewater.
- Know what you don't know: "your power is in what you don't know", and your risk is pursuing what you know while blind to what you're missing. Only others can show you your blind spots, so combine people with different abilities and be open about strengths and weaknesses. Markets are "a pure meritocracy": "nobody's name goes on a trade" and it's "a zero sum game", so a firm without an internal meritocracy "won't compete well".
- Hiring: "values, ability, skills in that order". Values such as openness and a "desire for truth" are hard to change, while skills can be trained; people who value improvement treat failure and feedback "as fuel to improve". Grit differs from drive: keeping focus "when things don't go well". "you learn a lot more about what people are like by working with them than interviewing them".
- Leadership, his distillation of Dalio's principles: "the who before the what the what before the how". Someone must set the purpose and goal before deciding how; leaders set uncompromisable values, model them and let go of people who don't share them.
- Outlook (Mar 2025): after five unstable years (COVID, overheating, inflation, tight money, a bear market), policymakers have brought economies into "a reasonable balance", but geopolitical chaos (tariffs, boycotts of US investment and products) threatens it. Modern mercantilism means an inward focus, "competition instead of cooperation" and more state involvement: "a philosophical change" that will last.
- Who's ahead (Mar 2025): China has done it "pretty successfully"; the US started under Biden and Trump is "extremely aggressive at it"; Europe is "left behind" without a central government. Ironically, Germany's big defense initiative is fiscal stimulus that is turning positive for the European economy.
- Play the long game: compounding at 10% vs 5% gives only "1.6 or something" times more after 10 years but "six times" after 40, and the same holds for learning. You'll only sustain the faster path if you love the work, so "live your life from the inside out"; your passion is "what do you do when you're not getting paid for it". No book explains how the world works; textbooks build theories "based on assumptions that are not true", so "ask your own questions and find your own answers".
- Screen implication: before reading a company's margins or returns as quality, split out the market bet (commodity, currency, rates) from the operating business; a jeweler's profit is partly a long-gold position.
