### 139. Felix Zulauf — Top-down cycles, a weekend chart discipline and the end of globalization (Generating Alpha Podcast, Apr 2025)

*Founder of Zulauf Consulting and a longtime Barron's Roundtable member; he ran UBS's investment strategy and institutional money management, then founded Zulauf Asset Management in 1990 and ran long/short hedge funds.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZAFHdNYs0KE)

**Takeaway:** Zulauf works top-down, from the political "zeitgeist" to a business cycle distorted by policy, then to rates, currencies, sectors and commodities. He sees the US trade shift as the end of a 30-year globalization wave (Apr 2025), and stays honest with a weekly review of \~1,000 charts and hard stop-losses.

- Process: start with the political setup, "the zeitgeist", and where the pendulum stands between conservative and progressive. Then run a classic business-cycle analysis and deduce what it means for rates, currencies, capital flows, bonds vs equities, defensive vs "deep cyclical" sectors, currency hedging and commodities.
- The cycle is no longer clean: "you have to understand the distortions to the business cycle due to the growing interventions and manipulations by the authorities", so it doesn't run as it did in the 1960s and 70s.
- Trade is now the most important variable (Apr 2025). The Trump administration's change matches the fall of the Berlin Wall or the end of Bretton Woods or the gold standard. It ends the WTO as we knew it and with it "the globalization wave that we have enjoyed over the last 30 years or so".
- Next comes capital flows: the backlash is changing the geopolitical order and the US dollar's relevance as a reserve currency, and shifting capital flows will in principle change the level of US interest rates.
- Conviction only: "I only play when I see that the odds are turning in my favor." Otherwise he stays out.
- Match the manager to the long cycle: in a bull market "you should not use a 60 or 55 year old portfolio manager", who is conditioned by the previous structural bear market; you need "a young greenhorn guy who wants to be bullish".
- Contrarian calls are lonely: "at the top with a bearish view, you are very lonely". His cure for doubt is a working discipline, kept for 40 years.
- Every weekend he spends about five hours on about a thousand charts: money-market rates, bond yields, the 30 largest currencies, world equity and sector indices, and the 100 largest stocks in the US, Europe and Asia, each on long, medium and short-term views.
- Fundamentals place the business cycle; technicals "tell me whether I'm wrong or not". Where the market disagrees with his thesis, he digs until he finds the problem.
- Read like a scientist: he skims the FT, WSJ, a Swiss daily and Bloomberg for anything that contradicts his thesis. "Something that fits I do not have to read in depth you know but something that doesn't I have to check."
- Calls at extremes: handed a fund of commodity-related equities at the peak of the 1980 commodity boom, he went to 70% cash and 30% in commodity companies' bonds. Before the 1987 crash he took all institutional accounts down to zero equities.
- His hedge funds ran long/short with little leverage, at times 30%, and could go net 100% short.
- Biggest lesson: "Never be cocky. Stay humble." Keep a failsafe stop-loss, because a big loss "kills you psychologically" and clouds decisions. His worst trade came at \~30, when he "forgot the stop loss" and finally cut it down 50%.
- Raising money: be authentic, explain the process, do what you said, and explain results, "particularly what you did wrong". A US investment bank expected \~250 million for his European-stocks sub-advisory fund after a year; he had 1 billion (late 1990s).
