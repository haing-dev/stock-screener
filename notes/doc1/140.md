### 140. Paul Krugman — Trade's two eras, US credibility and the debt (Generating Alpha Podcast, Jul 2025)

*Nobel-winning economist (2008, for work on international trade and currency crises), long-time New York Times columnist and professor at the City University of New York, in a life-and-views interview.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5-Vdm8cIkg)

**Takeaway:** There are two eras of US trade policy, "pre-Trump and then there's Trump", and tariffs since 2 April 2025 break binding agreements, so the US is "not remotely a credible country" (Jul 2025). The debt arithmetic is manageable; the real debt risk is lost credibility.

- Trade's long continuity began with the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934, the template for the postwar GATT. Rounds of negotiation ratcheted tariffs down until they were very low by 2016.
- Even Reagan, a bit more protectionist ("voluntary" export restraints on Japanese autos), treated agreements as binding: in inter-agency meetings the trade representative's office would say an idea "would be gat illegal and end of story". That is "Absolutely completely gone now".
- Since 2 April (2025), tariffs are "a flat violation of solemn international agreements". The US has a free-trade agreement with South Korea yet hit it with 25% tariffs, and its tariff rates on most-favoured-nation partners are legally bound. "the United States is a country that can no longer be trusted."
- Debt history: stable, wealthy countries have run up very large debts without a crisis. US debt is about where it was at the end of World War II as a share of GDP, and the UK left the war at 250% of GDP; neither had a debt crisis, because bond buyers cut slack for advanced countries presumed responsible and able to collect taxes.
- The arithmetic "is really not that daunting": a 5% value-added tax plus somewhat tougher health-care cost control would fix it. But a middle-class tax hike is politically out of the question, so "the biggest reason to worry about a US debt crisis" is whether the US is still credible. Interest payments are up, and why rates stay higher is partly a "mystery".
- Medicare Advantage: overpayment estimates "run as high as 80 billion a year" because insurers upcode their patients. DOGE never touched it while shutting USAID and cancelling NIH research; "profiteering by health insurance companies is apparently off limits".
- Credibility takes decades to rebuild. Latin America's "original sin" was being unable to borrow in its own currencies; Brazil now can, after decades of reasonably responsible policy. "We need to have multiple elections in which reasonable people are put in charge of the United States before people trust us again."
- China gains: its per-capita income is about a quarter of America's, but its economy is bigger on purchasing-power terms. Its contracts can feel like "a suggestion", "but they look more credible than we do at this point". The three economic superpowers are the US, China and the EU.
- Finance is an extra three points of GDP, which understates financialization, and the growth is "almost all costs". Samuelson said the ATM was the only clearly beneficial financial innovation; Krugman adds payment apps. Subprime lending and CDOs paved the way to 2008.
- Big policy mistakes are rare. The one big US mistake before the current administration was the underpowered response to 2008: the Obama stimulus was "way too small". Europe's "austerity mania" was "really really destructive". The Fed makes "misjudgments, but they're they're not stupid", and it is open-minded.
- Populism isn't only economics: Denmark, with a safety net beyond US progressives' dreams and $23-an-hour McDonald's workers, still has an anti-immigrant populist right. "I'm not an economic determinist on this thing."
- The future is unknowable: nobody knows AI's economic impact. A sustained productivity boom would leave today's young better off; zero or negative immigration would be "a real blow" to native-born Americans' future living standards.
