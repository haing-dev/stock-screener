### 142. Jeff Yass — Economic myths: debt against wealth, consumer surplus and taxing billionaires (Generating Alpha Podcast, Jun 2026)

*Co-founder and managing director of Susquehanna, in his second Generating Alpha appearance, taking on myths about the US debt, consumer surplus and taxes.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LTeX0_qklsU)

**Takeaway:** Judge US debt against wealth, not GDP: at \~13–14% of \~$230 trillion of wealth, "the debt really isn't a problem" (Jun 2026). The danger is policy that destroys "the golden goose", since entrepreneurs keep only \~10% of the value they create and consumers get the rest.

- Debt vs GDP misleads: the US has roughly $31 trillion of debt and \~$31 trillion of GDP, but wealth of "probably seven times our GDP", close to $230 trillion. Its stock market is worth $80 trillion, over 2.5x GDP; in Europe and China, market value and GDP are roughly one to one.
- A lender's view: you'd fear lending $1 million to someone earning $100,000 a year, but not if they had $5 million in the bank. "You really wouldn't care what their earnings are." US debt is "roughly 13 or 14%" of wealth, a historic low he believes, though near a high against GDP except for World War II.
- The real problem is waste: "we're spending 7 trillion when 5 trillion could have gotten the same job done", so $2 trillion a year is wasted. "But we're not going broke."
- The US goes broke only if its \~$230 trillion of wealth falls to \~$100 trillion, about where it was 10 or 15 years ago. The risks are regulation that stops businesses starting and "super high taxes", which he thinks the US might see in 2029. With about the population of most of Europe, the US is 50% richer, but "we could easily become Europe".
- Consumer surplus: Bezos noted Amazon was worth $2 trillion and he owned 10% ($200 billion), with $1.8 trillion going to investors. But the hours Amazon saves people, valued at a multiple of 20, come to something like $10 trillion, at least five times its market cap, so Bezos got \~2%. Entrepreneurs typically keep "somewhere around 10%".
- Profits understate value: you pay Tylenol a dollar for two pills but "might easily pay $100 not to get a headache". Uber is profitable, "but it's not that profitable", and its profit is a fraction of the value it added.
- The zero-sum myth: many see Musk's trillion dollars as "One big pizza pie and this guy came in and took all the slices". Editorials mock zero-sum thinking, but that view is "not really out there politically", and consumer surplus goes undiscussed because it is hard to measure.
- "Bezos pays no taxes" is an accounting error: if Amazon earns $100 billion a year and pays $30 billion in tax, "twenty percent of that or six billion a year is his". If Amazon were private, he would write that check himself.
- Philanthropy as expected value: rank ideas by the "idea times the probability that it's actually doable". School choice wins because it is "politically doable": New York City spends $43,000 per pupil, and the best Catholic schools cost less than half that.
- Hidden bills keep waste going: government never sends you the $43,000 school bill, and "If they sent you a bill, you would go nuts". Halving a two-trillion deficit motivates nobody, so reformers must hand people the savings.
- His best idea, with "zero chance", is to follow the 9th and 10th Amendments. The doable one is "voucher, voucher, voucher": fund schools and health care the way food stamps fund food, still a welfare state but "vastly more efficient".
