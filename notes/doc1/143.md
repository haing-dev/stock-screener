### 143. Panel: Pablo Calderini, Christian Derry \[?\], Nicholas Cormier \[?\] & Jackie Rosner \[?\] — Discretionary vs systematic macro: bandwidth, data and convexity (Graham Capital Management, Feb 2025)

*Global Alts 2025 panel: Calderini is president and CIO of Graham Capital (\~20bn AUM, 70–75% quant); Derry \[?\] is head of macro strategy at CFM, a Paris quant firm with \~17bn AUM; Cormier \[?\] invests in systematic macro funds for CPP Investments' external hedge fund team; Rosner \[?\] covers macro and relative value at PAAMCO Prisma.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vsf0cuWqwys)

**Takeaway:** Both styles trade the same liquid markets on similar fundamentals; the difference is method. Quants process far more data across hundreds of ideas but are rigid when news breaks over a weekend, while discretionary PMs can change their whole view overnight, so allocators hold both.

- Outlook (early 2025): Cormier \[?\] expects a well-diversified hedge fund portfolio to make "cash plus three to five" "with no exposure to Beta", helped by dispersion between a struggling China, US exceptionalism and slowing developed markets. Rosner \[?\] aims for double digits, with drawdown control and low equity correlation as the second and third goals.
- Rosner \[?\]: tariffs, immigration reform, tax cuts, regulation, deficits and a strong dollar add up to a wall of worry. "Don't fight the FED" has a twin, "don't fight Trump": just as the Fed is data dependent, "Trump is beta dependent" and watches equity wealth. She leans into "positively convex strategies that are volatility friendly".
- Calderini: both styles use the same inputs (monetary and fiscal policy, growth, inflation, plus value, mean-reversion and carry factors); they differ in how they extract signals. Quant gets the most from big data: machine learning (but no unsupervised learning), compression that makes correlated inputs a non-issue, nonlinear pattern recognition (which he calls key to macro) and robust portfolio construction.
- Calderini: the price is rigidity. Telling a system about a weekend shock (a Chinese AI program, a Trump tariff remark) would "take a lot of alpha away from the program", so Graham's quant models work on a 1–2 month horizon (maybe three), while discretionary PMs trade "more week to week". Changing your whole view on the fly is "the key advantage of the discretionary over every other strategy".
- Derry \[?\]: humans have "a bandwidth problem". An Air Force study found an experienced pilot can't watch more than seven gauges without raising crash risk, and in discretionary macro it's usually "just a couple ideas every year that generate most of the p&l". CFM ingests five terabytes of data a day (over six exabytes so far) and each strategy holds "hundreds of these competing ideas".
- Derry \[?\]: in the last five years "more than 50% of our models are essentially relying on these large unstructured data sets". Few firms have the technology, investment and experience to exploit them, and "no human really is in a position to fully leverage" them.
- Rosner \[?\]: a good discretionary macro trader has a Sharpe ratio "just under one"; the average futures strategy, trend or not, \~0.5. Systematic managers run about twice the volatility, so absolute returns end up similar, and in big moves the two styles are "very similar and correlated".
- Rosner \[?\] on picking managers: underwrite the people and a tested, repeatable process, with expertise "an inch wide a mile deep"; "we don't like to pay for someone's tuition". Systematic managers differ in strategy mix, trading speed and asset mix, so hold "more than one or two managers for sure but definitely less than 10"; with more "you just water down the soup".
- Cormier \[?\]: CPP uses macro to diversify away from corporate security selection into asset selection. From systematic funds it wants "more sophisticated versions of carry momentum fundamental momentum"; from discretionary ones "real convexity that comes out at the right time", often from one or two big ideas a year where timing and conviction decide.
- Derry \[?\]: trend-following convexity is "really just a mechanical effect". A new CFM predictor won't go into production if it correlates with existing ones, and new macro ideas often correlate with "a cluster of trend following predictors": "that's not new information". Discretionary traders have "a benchmarking problem": they are judged only on P&L, while a quant firm can attribute each embedded idea.
- Overrides: a panelist argued "systematic has to remain systematic". Derry \[?\]: "it's not a label we run a business"; markets are nonstationary, so a committee he chairs handles "non-model risk" (regulation, elections, DeepSeek). "we never override signals but on rare occasions we'll put constraints on our risk models", then codify the logic. Calderini: Graham doesn't like to override its process "ever" but holds a daily risk meeting, and interventions in massive disruptions "are not about Alpha ever".
- AI: Derry \[?\] says CFM has used machine learning for \~20 years; LLMs are the disruptive part, useful for classification, richer sentiment signals from unstructured data, and coding. Calderini calls AI "phenomenal for Alpha discovering" but avoids unsupervised learning, which can "perform incredibly well in sample but terrible out of sample". The panel's three-word biggest macro risks: "fiscal trajectory unsustainable", artificial intelligence, and "inflation higher rates".
- Screen implication: add a rule only if it brings new information. A gate that mostly duplicates existing ones adds nothing, and rules tuned to past winners can look great in sample and fail out of sample.
