### 145. Panel: Pablo Calderini, Kelly Dropping Woodridge \[?\], Peter Chapson \[?\], David Keelan \[?\] & Chris McCann \[?\] — Inflation ends the Fed put: sizing and risk in macro's new regime (Graham Capital Management, Sep 2022)

*Graham Capital's discretionary-strategies panel at its 2022 virtual investment symposium (recorded Sep 2022, posted Jan 2025): president and CIO Calderini with chief economist Dropping Woodridge \[?\], senior portfolio managers Chapson \[?\] and Keelan \[?\], and chief risk officer McCann \[?\], after an intro by founder Kenneth Tropin.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=up6xlksw1aQ)

**Takeaway:** Persistent inflation ends a decade in which central banks met every shock with easier money and suppressed volatility. Calderini calls 2010–20 "an anomaly" and expects markets to behave more like the volatile decades before 2010 (Sep 2022); the PMs size in volatility units and shrink the book when their trades converge, and the risk team trusts stress tests over VaR.

- Dropping Woodridge \[?\]: before the pandemic, too-low inflation let the Fed be "quick to ease and slow to tighten" (2015–18 hikes came every other meeting, 25bp at a time) and made the "fed put" a feature. Its August 2020 framework (flexible average inflation targeting) aimed to push inflation up, so it called the first high prints transitory.
- Her edge in 2022: a strong view on inflation and jobs showed the Fed "really wrongly positioned", which rewarded thinking "outside the box". There is no house view; in Dec 2021 her team flagged press-conference hints of an early balance-sheet runoff (while QE still ran) to the PMs, who "express it in their own way".
- Her outlook (Sep 2022): a year after QE, the debate "is about how many 75 basis point increases". With services and core goods "remaining really sticky", the surprise may be "just how high the terminal rate will be" (a funds rate in the 5% range), soft-landing odds are lower, and European central banks must "tighten into recessions". If inflation "magically cools", easing cycles bring other trades.
- Chapson \[?\]: "inflation was the hardest variable to predict", which 20 years of 0.2% monthly prints made people forget. Policy is "like Back to the Future": central banks hike on headline CPI, the Fed even on a University of Michigan expectations surprise, while governments spend to help people "afford higher prices". The 60/40 portfolio's negative stock-bond correlation is "gone".
- Chapson \[?\] also sees globalization reversing and labor regaining power over capital. Economists now know no more than traders do, which puts a premium on common sense, "using your own eyes", imagination and risk management.
- His outlook: 8% inflation sliding back to a 2% target, "kind of an artificial level", is "ludicrous". Tightening global liquidity is "bad for risk assets", so stocks and bonds both sell off. Japan can't stay "the only developed country on Earth that doesn't have inflation", but "people are so stuck in the old ways".
- Keelan \[?\]: the early-1990s starting point helped "set up risk parity" for decades; pandemic stimulus pushed it to an extreme ($7 trillion of negative-yielding debt, record stock valuations) before it was "thrown out the window". QE "brings down Vol"; buying dips on a hoped-for pivot is "the old Playbook", because the Fed put is "struck so far down that we're not quite sure where that is yet".
- 2022's trade was long the dollar, short rates, short equities, but "the bear Market rallies are so vicious": in the summer stocks bounced \~20% off the lows while 10-year notes rallied \~75bp. Sentiment "so dire that the market could snap back" is itself the opportunity (Keelan \[?\]). Chapson \[?\] adds moves inside the dollar: the yen, and a Mexican peso that rose on reshoring to North America.
- Keelan's \[?\] watch list (Sep 2022): Japan's 3% inflation, a 30-year high, against yield-curve control and a weak yen; Europe's winter ("the big variable"); China's zero-COVID policy once Xi is reappointed in October.
- McCann \[?\]: Graham builds for diversification (PM candidates are asked what they add; monthly allocation weighs pairwise correlations). In strong-theme years like 2022 the PMs converge, yet "in times of the higher correlation we actually have a higher information ratio among our PMS". Calderini calls matching risk to conviction a hallmark of macro.
- VaR vs tails (McCann \[?\]): in quiet, diversified years VaR is low but tail risk is higher because positions are bigger. In 2022 VaR ran high but tail risks were "a lot smaller because the position sizes are smaller". Unstable correlations make VaR less useful and stress tests more, since "Gap risk is really what you need to be concerned about".
- Risk numbers (Greeks, hundreds of shocks, dozens of stress tests, VaR) give "the what of the position but it doesn't tell me the why", so the risk team sits on the trading floor. The risk committee, made up of managers who aren't actively managing risk, is "a cold eyes review"; the PMs, who do the frontline risk control, have been "tending to scale down into events and then reacting to what happens", which long-only or illiquid books can't.
- Chapson \[?\] sizes in volatility units (higher vol, smaller size), stays ready for tails and gap risk, and now prizes liquidity because market liquidity is "terrible": "some trades you just might not take". He builds bottom-up, then checks total risk and a "sentiment Factor", when "everything that's overbought gets sold".
- Keelan \[?\]: "getting the themes right ... this is the key", since on the wrong side "it really doesn't matter how well structured you are". Size so a winner counts, but not so big "that if there's some noise you get shaken out". Into a central-bank meeting, dollar, rates and equities become "all the same trade", so shrink the book rather than make "a triple size bet on an outcome of a meeting". His team's daily meeting asks "what positions are too big and what positions are too small".
