### 146. Scott Nations — The Anxious Investor: the biases behind three bubbles (The Intrinsic Value Podcast, 2022)

*Former option trader (25 years on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange floor) and author of The Anxious Investor, interviewed by Clay Finck on the Millennial Investing podcast during the 2022 bear market.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eW2NkoR0wk8)

**Takeaway:** The South Sea Bubble (1720), the internet bubble (1999) and the housing crash (2008–09) show the same handful of biases. Know the two or three you fall for, test trades for thrill-seeking, and keep investing when prices fall.

- Sensation-seeking: some people trade for the thrill. People with more speeding tickets placed more trades, Taiwanese trading fell 25% once a lottery opened, and "Hedge fund managers who drive sports cars underperform hedge fund managers who drive minivans."
- His test: before a trade, "Wait 30 days." For a real investment 30 days is "meaningless in the long term"; if waiting irritates you so much you can barely stand it, "that trade is sensation-seeking".
- Risk is the price of return. US stocks have returned 7–8% a year since indexes began in the late 1800s, so "sometimes you're just going to be along for the ride". If a drawdown keeps you awake, "You're probably just thinking about risk wrong."
- South Sea Bubble: a government-debt clearinghouse with a "really mundane business", paying a £6 dividend on a \~£100 price (\~6%), rose from \~£120 early in 1720 to 300 and 500. An old holder sold at \~£350, watched it hit 500, 700 and 900, and "bought back in at the high".
- Disposition effect: selling winners and holding losers is dressed up "as discipline", but it is greed for the thrill of a sale and dodging regret. Terrance Odean (Berkeley) finds the stocks people sell "ultimately outperform whatever they end up buying".
- Loss aversion: losses hurt "about two times to two and a half times" more than equal gains please, so people sell at bottoms. The largest net equity-fund outflows in decades came in Feb–Mar 2009, "the absolute bottom", and many "didn't get back in for years".
- Overconfidence is his own main bias and the one Kahneman would remove with "a magic wand". Men are more overconfident than women, single men most; his young interns saw meme stocks (GameStop, AMC) going "to $1,000" with no thought of loss.
- "Phantastic objects" (professors Tuckett and Taffler): people buy a charismatic founder's stock (Jobs then, Musk now) hoping some of the magic rubs off. "If you want to buy Tesla stock because you want to be a little bit closer to Elon, well, that's not a very good reason".
- Internet-bubble signs: some managements thought investors would hate a profit, and Pets.com lost money "on every bag of dog food". "Just getting big is not a strategy for profit." A company that added .com to its name rallied, and the rally held (an academic paper).
- Recency and availability: since the Dow began in 1896, today's move tells you nothing about tomorrow's (no autocorrelation). A crash you can remember "is almost certainly not normal"; invest for the average, or you'll never be invested.
- 2008: overconfidence plus "a lack of imagination". AIG insured mortgage-backed securities, wrote internally "We cannot imagine how we would lose even a single dollar", and lost \~$80bn. House prices had never fallen nationwide in unison, and people were overconfident that they wouldn't.
- Overreaction: a stock-split announcement brings a "real, discernible, robust, sustainable increase in share price" though nothing changes. Investors also overreact to a one-off bad quarter, and some funds (one run by a Chicago Nobel laureate) exploit it.
- Anchoring: your entry price matters only for taxes; "the stock doesn't know what you paid for it". The trader's prayer, "God, just get me back to even and I'll get out", stops people harvesting tax losses (avoid wash sales).
- In the bear market (2022, S&P down >20% on a closing basis): "Invest, continue to invest. Don't stop investing." Hindsight bias misleads: after 1987 about a third ("almost 40%") of Shiller's respondents said they saw the crash coming, but only \~3% had acted.
