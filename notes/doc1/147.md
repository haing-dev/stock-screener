### 147. Steve Liesman — The K-shaped economy: AI capex vs tariffs, the consumer and the Fed (The Real Eisman Playbook, Oct 2025)

*CNBC's senior economics reporter and a former Wall Street Journal bureau chief in Russia, in a dialogue with Steve Eisman (Ep 29) on an economy split between AI investment and everything else.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZwoAJEzH99U)

**Takeaway:** Eisman's math (Oct 2025): consensus growth adds \~$530bn to GDP while AI capex alone is over $400bn, so the economy outside AI is about flat. Liesman agrees on the split, warns that tariffs absorbed in margins will hit small businesses and manufacturers that import inputs, and wants proof that AI profits reach the users, not just the providers.

- Eisman's math (Oct 2025): US GDP was $29.18tn last year and consensus growth is 1.8%, or \~$530bn. AI spending by Meta, Google, Amazon, Oracle and others is "over 400 billion", so the economy ex-AI is "really flat". Liesman: ex-AI is "kind of flattish and I think it may be even kind of downish", though much of the K is "flat flat. It's not necessarily down."
- Market vs economy: the market is in a "fervor". Liesman cites Josh Brown: "the momentum will continue as long as the momentum continues", i.e. AI spending lasts while the market rewards it. After 2000, companies likewise kept firing people as long as layoff announcements lifted their shares.
- AI payoff: Liesman cares about the users, not the providers: "if the profit does not acrue to the users of technology then you're going to hit a dead end". Today the market gives "multiple to the investment"; he'll be convinced when it rewards the adopters.
- Eisman's dot-com lesson: Henry Blodget said the internet would "change the world" and create "dynastic levels of wealth", and was right, "but it wasn't a straight line". This time profitable companies fund capex "out of cash flow", so a bust would be an "equity loss, not a leverage loss, not a systemic risk". Google's AI search is better but "not a thousand times better".
- Will the market switch from rewarding AI spending to demanding returns? Eisman: "That's coming. I just don't know when." The hard part is "picking the winners at the right price". Liesman: for now valuation doesn't matter; "People care about stories and in bad times they care about balance sheets."
- Tariffs (Oct 2025): companies went in with high margins and are "sucking up tariffs in margin"; how long that lasts is unclear. Small businesses can't research tariffs or "get in line to get an exemption": a couple earning $200,000 may drop to 180 or 150, and Orvis (fishing gear) is closing stores and laying people off.
- "don't put tariffs on inputs. That's what we did." Manufacturers that add value to imported inputs will "take it on the chin" unless they re-source or get around the tariffs. The effective tariff on imports is 10% vs an 18% "actual tariff rate", which may not mean half is still to come: firms find workarounds, including intercompany pricing, and if 10% is all, "we're going to be okay".
- Liberation Day (Apr 2, 2025): Liesman says the reciprocal tariffs were "completely made up"; a senior official at a large bank warned the Treasury of a meltdown and the tariffs were amended on Apr 9. Funding-market traders told him "we're not the story yet"; markets were "very close to a a real seizure". Economists' grim forecasts fit those tariffs; profiting required "a political judgment that the president was going to back off".
- Consumer: Eisman calls used cars a "canary in the coal mine": CarMax missed badly on a higher loan-loss provision, and buyers trade down to older cars. Liesman: the car loan is the bill paid first ("you got to have the car to get to work"), so auto stress matters; he is checking whether student-loan repayments are behind the car delinquencies.
- Counterpoint: Carnival is "sold out through 26" with 2027 bookings the best ever two years ahead, from middle-income buyers. Outside car loans, consumer credit "doesn't look like 2008 or 2007, anywhere near that". Eisman owns Carnival (Oct 2025): the lines spent "generations constantly building more ships" and "never really had any pricing power"; post-COVID cruise prices lagged hotels and are only now catching up.
- Jobs and AI (Oct 2025): Eisman cites 4.5% unemployment but youth unemployment "around 11" and computer-science graduates who can't find jobs. Liesman: AI is "really replacing the need for coders", even experienced ones; he has heard of AI doing in 3 hours what takes 30 days, like the machine-tool leap that cut a jet engine's repair from 6 months to \~6 weeks.
- Fed: it "does not exist in a political vacuum" and, like a hurricane, "follows the path of least resistance": none to 75bp hikes in the inflation, none now to 25bp cuts. Liesman isn't sure it would have cut at the last meeting without the president's pressure, and doubts Volcker would cut with inflation at 3% against a 2% target. Watch whether the long end resists cuts "beyond another 25 or 50 from here" (Oct 2025).
- Eisman on the GFC: banks "became way too levered", subprime blew up and systemically important banks owned it, all on Greenspan's watch; people warned him in 2005–06 about fraud "and he didn't care". By 2006 underwriting standards "were can you breathe". A head of derivatives (at Deutsche Bank, he thinks) told Liesman the banks had lent to everyone creditworthy "and we still had money left over".
- How Eisman spotted subprime: as an Oppenheimer analyst he saw half the first-generation subprime lenders go bankrupt in 1998. When a second generation went public in 2002, "the people who were running them were the same guys who were running them in '98. They just changed the names." "I saw this play once"; what he didn't know was how big it would get.
