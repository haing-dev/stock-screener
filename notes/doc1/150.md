### 150. Kenneth Tropin — Term premium is the best bet; without conviction, stay close to home (Graham Capital Management, Dec 2025)

*Founder and chairman of Graham Capital Management (\~$20bn, \~250 people, mostly macro), back on Bilal Hafeez's Macro Hive podcast at the end of 2025.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hFYMQNHbpGc)

**Takeaway:** With policy fluid and most traders running below their risk limits, Tropin's highest-probability macro trade (Dec 2025) is a US curve steepener, because term premium is underpriced against an "incurable" deficit. His rule for everything else: "when you don't have conviction, stay close to home".

- Philosophy: most risk sits in macro markets (rates, FX, equity indices, some commodities, plus fixed-income relative value, steepeners, breakevens and inflation trades), with some equities, credit and a little EM. It is "on the conservative side ... we're in it for the long haul", built to survive many market cycles.
- Versus pod shops: a multistrat might hold 10–15% of its risk in macro; Graham holds 75–80%, which is what a client adding macro exposure wants.
- Grow talent rather than "overpaying to get talent from a competitor": Jim Simons wanted mathematicians from the best universities, not hedge-fund hires. It takes longer, but "you have a better control of the outcome".
- Graham builds bigger teams (\~25 PM teams, some with 15 people) rather than hiring "600 PMs", and errs toward being "too patient instead of too impatient": a multistrat PM who doesn't perform quickly moves on, while Graham's get time. It also scouts cheaper talent abroad, and its New York office, opened in March 2025, is already 2/3 full.
- 2025's lesson: with policy "somewhat fluid" (tariffs, a possible Supreme Court reversal), Graham's traders did well "by being very tactical, opportunistic and not having really high levels of risk". Most traders he knows are "using less of their risk limits than normal" for fear of a disruptive policy or geopolitical shock.
- Top idea (Dec 2025): "term premium is underpriced", so he likes the steepener, but "you got to know when to put it on and take it off". The US deficit is "so extraordinarily large and so incurable", and duration risk has been "a non-event and a non-factor" for so long that markets should grow more sensitive "in the next year, maybe the next couple years".
- The curve steepened for part of 2025 and then went sideways, which he puts down to normal trading and "pressure from the administration to get rates lower".
- AI stocks: "AI is so for real it's kind of crazy and scary but having said that it's expensive". It is "a very concentrated bet in the equity markets", so a correction at some point would be normal.
- Central banks (Dec 2025): Kevin Hassett, an outsider, is the expected next Fed chair. Japan is "the most hawkish that I can remember" and is driving global rates, and Canada and Europe have turned hawkish; "sometimes the market can get away from any central bank". With everyone hawkish, the dollar has been in a range for six months, so he keeps little risk on it either way.
- Inflation: two-thirds of the economy outside AI is slowing, which is "slightly deflationary", but tariffs are "a tax on consumers": whether the tariff on European goods is 15% or 20%, companies pass it on. "Car manufacturers are passing that on. They're not eating it. They can't afford to."
- Private credit fears "may be a little overblown", since most managers know their risks. But in a liquidity shock "all bets are off" and even well-managed funds may "suspend redemptions", so pick funds with "really good risk management".
- Risk rule: "when you don't have conviction, stay close to home". Of "really aggressive traders that have prevailed over the long term", he says: "Not too many." He runs Graham conservatively and accepts that "We're going to get some cycles that are really not good for what we do."
- Humility and effort: "every time I lose my humility, I ... also usually lose money". A penniless sculptor who took an entry-level Wall Street job in the late 1970s, he got ahead because "I showed up first. I left last." His advice to graduates worried about AI: if you "outwork everyone around you" with a good attitude, you will do well.
