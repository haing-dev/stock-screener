### 153. Pablo Calderini — Navigating a new world order: the inflation shock, stock-bond correlation and the case for macro (Graham Capital Management, Jan 2025)

*President and CIO of Graham Capital Management, responsible for all its alpha development, in a client webinar recorded in spring 2023 (about a year after the Fed's first hike on Mar 16, 2022) and uploaded Jan 2025.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x3HYAHlKe-c)

**Takeaway:** The post-2008 decade, when central banks met every shock with easing, is over: an inflation shock has to be met with higher real rates, so volatility stays high and the 60/40 struggles. Macro earns its place through negative conditional correlation to equities and positive skew, not just its average return.

- Graham (host, its chief client officer): founded in 1994 by Kenneth Tropin as a purely systematic macro firm, it added multi-PM discretionary macro in the late 1990s. Of \~$18bn (spring 2023), about two-thirds is quant (trend and quant macro); the discretionary side backs \~15 high-conviction teams "rather than have 50 or 150".
- Regime change: for 10 years after the financial crisis, inflation was "sticky down", the main worry was the zero lower bound and every shock met a "super accommodative" response; the taper tantrum "lasted I think two weeks and the FED gave up". An inflation shock instead "needs to be matched by another shock", higher real rates. Even once inflation falls, central banks will be far more reluctant to rescue markets, so expect "much higher volatility" and a harder time for "the 60 40 canonical portfolio".
- Cycle size: the four post-Volcker hiking cycles before this one averaged \~220bp: 1994–95 (\~300bp, then the Tequila and Asian crises), 1999–2000 (170–175bp, until the Nasdaq tanked), 2005–06 (425bp, followed by the financial crisis) and 2015–18. This one is double that average in half the time and "presumably" reaches \~500bp with one more hike.
- Pricing test: in past cycles, forward curves on the day of the first hike priced roughly what the Fed delivered over the next \~18 months, so markets were "very well prepared". In this one (checked in Dec 2022) the Fed hiked 300bp more than priced, "a Calamity" he blames mainly on the Fed's guidance.
- Core, not headline: studies of inflation and asset prices look inconclusive because the sign flips (higher inflation helps assets in a deflationary period and hurts them in a high-inflation one). Two Wharton professors split it by driver: core inflation is "always a massive negative shock" to asset prices, while energy is volatile, mean-reverting and matters little. Core is persistent, so it "requires a much bigger policy response".
- Stock-bond correlation: with an equity risk premium of 400–500bp, bonds are held for their hedge. The long-run correlation is \~zero, but as an average of strongly positive periods (1960s to 2000s, when inflation dominated) and strongly negative ones (the last 20 years). It was \~zero in spring 2023; he expected it to stay near zero, not return to "the great days of minus 50 correlation" that were "a massive Bonanza" for the 60/40.
- Stocks for the long run: long-horizon volatility looks low because historical estimates treat the mean as known; looking forward it must be estimated too. Done properly, "stocks are a lot more volatile in the long run": a 3% variance (\~17% standard deviation, about the S&P's history) becomes \~5% (\~22%) at long horizons. Mean-variance investors should hold "a lot less equities", and low-return bonds with zero correlation aren't the obvious substitute.
- The case for macro: positive expected return, zero average correlation to risk assets and "negative conditional correlation", i.e. negative when equities do really badly. Returns are positively skewed: no 3-sigma loss but almost a 4-sigma gain. Investors focus too much on Sharpe ratios when "skewness and conditional correlation are super important".
- Portfolio math: on his chart a 60/40 sits at \~10.5% volatility and a 7% return. Over the last 20 years, adding 20% macro trims return slightly but cuts volatility more, lifting the information ratio from 0.66 to 0.75 and cutting the worst drawdown from -36% to -29%; he expects macro to do better still "when things get really bad".
- Graham's approach: macro as "a complement to the 60 40", with no long-term secular views (those are "already embedded in the 60 40"), dynamic and open-minded on a fundamental base, and "not shy to cut positions" when a thesis isn't working. The recent Treasury move, a "20 Sigma event", barely touched Graham, but it is hardening its risk systems for shocks that seem to come "more frequently and much more violently than before".
- Equities (spring 2023): short, because they are "in the complete wrong quadrant": earnings must fall and multiples are too high. Either core inflation stays sticky and the Fed hikes while markets price cuts, or core (mostly services) falls only through a jump in unemployment, i.e. recession; a hard landing would be worse still. But "we were not short on the way up": Graham could be long "in a month" if tech earnings prove resilient, and admits forces supporting equities that are "beyond our understanding".
- Other views (spring 2023): long the euro (US banking issues stop big Fed hikes while the ECB stays hawkish); long the yen, "extremely extremely undervalued", expecting the BOJ to tackle yield curve control eventually; most conviction in gold, since real rates should fall as breakevens priced "very low" rise, and a US debt-ceiling fight "usually is also fairly bullish for gold". His pitch: "we can talk in a month and have completely different views".
- Quant side: the trend program (time-series momentum) "did really well last year" (2022), adding value through portfolio construction (mean-variance optimization, PCA for fast covariance estimates). The quant macro program, trading since 2014 with a Sharpe "north of one", blends trend, value/long-run mean reversion, carry and fundamentals (GDP, inflation, earnings, inventories, NLP sentiment) in nonlinear, regime-dependent models. It has less crisis protection than trend but steadier returns and a higher information ratio.
