### 154. Chris Jones — Completion portfolios: customizing systematic macro to fill the gap in a client's portfolio (Graham Capital Management, Nov 2021)

*Head of Graham Capital's London office, co-head of institutional relations and part of its quant client-solutions team (ex-fund-of-funds CIO; teaches hedge funds at Cambridge), on a Talking Hedge webinar with allocators Judy Posnikoff \[?\] (PAAMCO co-founder, now Martlet Asset Management) and Ella \[?\] (ex-head of total fund management at BCI); recorded Nov 2021, uploaded Feb 2025.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FPCCLkxBY-Y)

**Takeaway:** Customization has moved from turning a single dial (a volatility target, a currency, a removed market) to building completion portfolios with the client: define the gap and the constraints, then optimize for that outcome. With inflation threatening stock-bond diversification (Nov 2021), allocators want downside diversification, not just low average correlation.

- Posnikoff \[?\] on how customization evolved: first a share class of an existing fund, used to add leverage to a quant fund returning low single digits; then funds of one (own terms, leverage, concentration, carved-out exposures); then, "a little bit early", an internal managed-account platform in 2005. That gave "actual control of the portfolio" plus economies of scale with prime brokers.
- Ella \[?\] sees three drivers: a long equity bull market in which "anything that competed with equities look dismal"; manager proliferation, so much of what was alpha "became Beta" and true alpha got scarce and expensive; and the move to a total portfolio approach (TPA). If the bull market lasts, manager selection matters less and only managers who can "be an extension of these teams" survive; a decent correction would revive manager selection and slow the shift.
- Jones: early customization at Graham (almost half its business is managed accounts) was "turning a dial": a higher volatility target, a sterling-based account, or excluding a market or commodities. Constraints enter at the last stage of the quant process (construction, optimization, risk overlay), so "any sensible constraint or cap" still yields an optimal portfolio rather than "a suboptimal Remnant". To limit equity risk, cap overall beta (not necessarily at zero) rather than strip out equity longs, so equity exposure doesn't "pop up in Commodities".
- Jones on completion portfolios: "outcome oriented investing rather than kind of strategy selection", combining alpha strategies, optimal asset baskets and volatility exposure ("a deck of dials"). Both sides must "sit on the same side of the table"; most time goes into formulating the problem and constraints, which may be governance constraints, ideally using the client's actual portfolio data. "the best manager is the one that can produce that portfolio with that kind of fit", and honesty about what's achievable matters because over-optimizing is easy.
- Posnikoff \[?\] on the total fund approach vs strategic asset allocation (SAA): it needs generalists rather than specialists, uses fewer external managers (no duplicated exposures across buckets) and makes overlays such as options and risk-mitigation strategies easier, without internal politics over moving money between buckets. Large adopters report "significant Savings" from fewer, more concentrated manager relationships.
- Ella \[?\]: SAA turns long-term capital market assumptions ("say 12% for Equity 6% for bonds"), a return target and funded-status volatility into an asset mix, then judges each bucket on beating its benchmark by 50–100bp. Under TPA asset classes are "more for Optics": capital follows macro risk factors (inflation, real rates, credit), all opportunities compete, and managers are hired only if they improve the whole portfolio's risk-adjusted return. With trend following available as beta, only very differentiated managers justify full hedge fund fees.
- Ella \[?\]: a risk-mitigation portfolio she worked on at BCI (not launched; details proprietary) targets the drawdown from the total portfolio's biggest risk. If that is equity, it cuts conditional beta and adds liquidity and dry powder, dynamically, using passive strategies plus a handful of active managers willing to customize for that portfolio.
- ESG views (Nov 2021): Posnikoff \[?\] says greenwashing worries are rising, a Stanford paper "this month" found divestment hasn't worked as well as hoped, and environmental portfolios had outperformed mainly by excluding energy, which reversed in 2021. Ella \[?\]: after nine years of ESG due diligence the industry is "very very far away still from quantifying consistently"; E is crowding out S and G, and each institution builds its own risk-factor framework.
- Jones on ESG futures: open interest in the S&P ESG future and a European counterpart (captioned "eurosock 600" \[?\]) is up two to three times. Replacing Graham's equity index futures with the two most liquid ESG contracts could run \~100 million while staying under 30% of open interest, and a small, "very rudimentary" test showed no return drag (a slight pickup). Exclusions, such as grains for some European investors or energy for those avoiding fossil fuels, go in as optimization constraints.
- The end of a "20-year free lunch" (Nov 2021): Jones cites Calderini's paper: the \~30-year positive stock-bond correlation (essentially the '70s to '90s) went with hawkish Fed policy and inflation shocks. If it returns, a 60/40 core could "drop like a stone together in a crisis", so investors "maxed out on private markets" are turning to completion funds aimed at diversification, risk mitigation and loss control.
- Inflation playbook: Posnikoff \[?\] says old academic papers find "equities do very poorly in an inflationary period", which favors deep-research long/short managers who can tell which companies lack market power and will be "really hurt" by rising supplier prices; AQR argues trend should do well. Ella \[?\]: inflation-sensitive privates and commodities have limits, so consider inflation-focused risk parity, linkers or gold "if you prefer a more blunt object". She expects inflation not to be permanent; the bigger problem is low returns and bonds that "may join equities on the downside".
- What allocators want (Nov 2021): Jones sees a shift from correlation to "conditional correlation", i.e. downside diversification, plus ESG products from managers who are good corporate citizens. Ella \[?\]: offsetting equity drawdowns (lower conditional beta and downside capture) and esoteric strategies "worth paying the active fees for". Posnikoff \[?\]: diversity, both among managers (loosening track-record and personal-investment requirements) and in their own staff.
- Screen implication: inflation splits companies by pricing power, so margins that held through 2021–23 (Fortress F13) are a filing-based proxy for the market power Posnikoff \[?\] says long/short managers must research.
