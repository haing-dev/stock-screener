### 157. Andrew Law — Boardroom masterclass: macro trading, crises and careers in finance (Sheffield Alumni, Sep 2020)

*Chair and CEO of macro hedge fund Caxton Associates, formerly head of Goldman Sachs's proprietary trading desk in London, giving a careers masterclass to University of Sheffield students and alumni in 2020.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ju2U3xm7mvA)

**Takeaway:** Markets are economics plus psychology, and the best macro traders read fundamentals, policymakers and the crowd together. Crises always come and markets "flip a switch" from ignoring them to panic, so keep winners, cut losers and build resilience.

- Caxton forecasts macro trends and economic cycles and how asset classes behave through them. It buys what it thinks will rise and sells what it thinks will fall, over "several weeks to several months", sometimes a year.
- Psychology matters as much as economics: greed to join trends and a "deep fear of losing too much money" drive prices. The hedge-fund legends combine a grasp of economies, of how central bankers and governments decide, and of what everyone else in the market is doing.
- He gets "half of the things wrong" that he forecasts. His skill is "not to let it bother me", to "sell the things that aren't working", keep the ones that are, and lose less on losers than he makes on winners.
- Real-time data edge (2020): Caxton web-scraped reported COVID-19 cases worldwide. Email alerts at 10am on the Saturday of the late-February half-term showed a spike in Italy, and "by sunday morning we knew the game was up", a month before the UK government chose to lock down.
- Crisis memory: his first month in the City coincided with the 1987 crash (the equity market lost 25% "over a couple of days"), and its lessons helped in 2008. In Feb–Mar 2020 the US market lost 35% in six weeks; the best guides were the Spanish flu and the plague's spread along the Silk Road in the 1300s.
- History suggests that "crises always happen": Wuhan data existed in late December and China locked down Hubei by mid-January, yet markets didn't react until the Italian ski weekend. Markets "literally flip a switch one day from ignoring events to panicking about them", which is what keeps him awake.
- Business risk came back in 2020: a travel company's business "was pretty much over" in February, and one that "borrowed too much money" would probably have gone bankrupt without government help. Owners earn more because they bear risks "none of us even know exists", so build resilience into business models.
- Machines vs humans: AI does much of finance's repetitive work, and perhaps 50–80% of what many people do daily is repetitive. On 2016 election night US stocks fell 6–8% in Asian hours and reversed within 12 hours; the quant and machine-learning models "all lost a lot of money", while a few people who bet on Trump cutting taxes made a lot.
- The big opportunities come in new situations such as politics, where each government differs from what went before. Nearly everyone on Caxton's floor codes, but the best combine coded or scraped inputs with "their own human intuition".
- Hedge funds run \~$3 trillion, \~3% of the \~$100 trillion in global equities and bonds, and nearly all of Caxton's money now comes from pension funds.
- ESG (2020): unlike niche, exclusion-based socially responsible investing, it will be "a really central part of all investing". E, S and G go together, since bad governance tends to mean poor treatment of consumers, employees and the environment; a large asset manager called ESG "a massive commercial opportunity".
- Careers: get into the industry through any company and find the right role from inside (his 13 applications led to six first interviews, one second interview and one job). Be "one of the best" at a core skill, know what sets you apart, and build "cultural capital".
- Resilience is "the most important skill": "you're going to fail at many things in your lives". Know your downside early in a career; bigger risks can come later, e.g. once the mortgage is paid down.
- Work-life balance: markets run from 6pm Sunday (Asia opens) to 9:15pm Friday (US close). Most Caxton traders close nearly all positions before a one- or two-week holiday to get a real break; he doesn't, and relies on exercise instead.
