### 159. Luke Gromen — A trapped Fed, peak cheap energy, China's water and the petrodollar (The Intrinsic Value Podcast, Jan 2022)

*Macro analyst at research firm FFTT and author of The Mr. X Interviews, recorded on 12 January 2022, the day CPI printed 7%; released in Apr 2023 as "prescient predictions".* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FV67-bcrgyc)

**Takeaway:** This is not a normal hiking cycle (Jan 2022): a bursting sovereign-debt bubble needs sustained negative real rates, while "peak cheap energy and metals" and China's water-power squeeze keep inflation sticky, so the Fed is trapped. Saudi oil sales to China in yuan are an "inevitability" that is structurally bad for the dollar.

- Tightening is happening amid things "no one alive has ever invested through": the first pandemic recovery in 50–100 years and "the first bursting Global sovereign debt bubble in 100 years", which needs sustained, increasingly negative real rates (Jan 2022; the host notes CPI at 7% and the 10-year at \~1.7%).
- "Peak cheap energy and metals": the world isn't running out, but the ore grades needed to meet demand will likely require significant price rises. That means "a real sort of sustained inflationary impulse" from commodity and metals markets.
- China's water: the north is short of water, rivers are drying up, and aquifers are drawn far faster than they refill (which takes tens of thousands of years). Low rivers cut hydro base load, while nuclear and coal use a lot of water. "if China has a water problem China has a power problem and if China has a power problem the world has a power problem and the world has an inflation problem".
- Signs (2H 2021): power plants shut "ostensibly for environmental reasons" in 3Q21, Apple asked to shut factories for lack of power in 4Q21, Bitcoin miners pushed out, and Shenzhen and Guangzhou asking citizens to ration water in December. He expects trouble "within a couple years", sooner if the weather goes against China.
- So "there's like zero chance we've seen Peak supply chain disruptions". Endemic shortages breed an inflationary mindset: firms swap just-in-time for 1970s-style ordering, when "carrying inventory starts to be an asset on your books". If supply problems persist, CPI is "probably going to run seven to ten for the next two three four years".
- Geopolitics: citing Rush Doshi's The Long Game, no US adversary (WWI Germany, Nazi Germany, Imperial Japan, the USSR) ever had GDP of 60% of America's or more; China reached that level in 2014. Trade and supply-chain problems will be "much stickier than people think".
- The WWII playbook: let inflation run hot and cap yields. In 1946–51 US debt/GDP fell from 110% to 55% with real rates as low as -14%, delevering the system so the Fed could later normalize. Gromen calls tightening now a policy mistake; the host notes debt/GDP fell only from 135% (late 2020) to 122%, and it took 7% CPI.
- Germany 1931: heavy debts, war reparations it couldn't print, and a de-industrialized economy. US entitlements are its reparations, owed in "Health Care goods and services" the Fed can't print. Bondholders lose either in real terms (inflation) or through austerity: "riding two horses with one ass". Germany chose austerity, and the world paid for 15 years.
- Druckenmiller's flow thesis (CNBC, May 2021): foreigners funded US deficits by buying tech stocks, not Treasuries; foreign holdings of US equities rose from \~2 trillion (2009) to 12 trillion (2020). A rotation out of tech into commodity cyclicals, banks and value would hit the dollar, and oil, cyclicals and gold "is going to have to rise a whole lot" to balance the deficits.
- The dollar sets how far yields can rise: with the DXY at 96–100 markets are already close to their limit, while a much weaker dollar (say 70 within 12 months) would allow the balance-sheet run-off and four hikes the banks talk about. Seeing the dollar fall, he now thinks the Fed "could do the three or four" in 2022, in effect "slowing the rate at which the dollar Falls".
- Structural deficits (entitlements, defense, interest) and too few foreign Treasury buyers mean rules keep bending: "a classic Emerging Markets move you've seen it in Argentina". After banks' SLR exemption for Treasuries ended (April 2021), the Fed's reverse repo went "from zero to two trillion in eight months", "basically just an Enron like accounting creation".
- "yield curve control is the Hotel California": once in, you can never leave, so the Fed prefers "yield curve bumper bowling", e.g. fixing the repo spike in 24–48 hours by growing a balance sheet now at nine trillion dollars. Ultimately "they'll be forced to".
- Petrodollar: 18 months earlier he heard "very credible Rumblings" of a deal for Saudi oil sales to China in yuan or dollars, China's choice. China is Saudi Arabia's biggest client, so yuan oil is an "inevitability", probably under way for close to five years. It may help the dollar and hurt oil in the short run, but structurally the Fed must absorb the deficits, and "when the FED buys 60 to 100 issuance the dollar goes down" (Mar 2020–Apr 2021).
- Endgame and sizing: Bill Miller's 50% Bitcoin, 50% Amazon portfolio shows senior investors see the Fed as trapped; "they're going to have to fully reserve the debt", which for the eurodollar system means a Fed balance sheet of 70–100 trillion. Two things matter, "the end game and how you manage your chips along the end game": even in Weimar's hyperinflation, people sold gold for marks four or five times, so "be careful with leverage".
