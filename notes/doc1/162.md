### 162. Alan Schwartz — Bear Stearns' overnight repo run and building Guggenheim from scratch (Generating Alpha Podcast, Mar 2025)

*Executive chairman of Guggenheim Partners and CEO of Bear Stearns when it was sold to JPMorgan Chase in 2008; a career interview on the 2008 run, crisis decisions and leading change.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yk5nz9OA4YY)

**Takeaway:** Bear Stearns fell to a 1930s-style bank run: repo lenders cut term lines to overnight and then, on rumors, stopped lending. Never assume short-term funding will be there, keep emotion out of crisis decisions, and build a firm around clients and one culture.

- Ace Greenberg's rule: "everybody's greedy but you either have to decide to be short-term greedy or long-term greedy". Long-term greedy means putting clients' needs first and winning over time.
- Try new businesses, but "make your mistakes while we're small" and "don't put the franchise at risk". Figure a business out before expanding it, and never cross the ethics line.
- Consolidation: in the 1970s there were \~50–70 firms of similar size; by the 1980s, "five big ones and a few others". The survivors were nimble and entrepreneurial.
- Pre-crisis warning signs: mortgage originators no longer bore the losses, so loan quality was at risk. Structured and corporate AAAs were "totally different but they used the same definition", and AAA-only buyers mixed them up.
- Liquidity dried up first in repo. As 180-day and 90-day lines came due, lenders would only roll them overnight. Bear ran its balance sheet assuming it could always borrow overnight against AA/AAA collateral such as Treasuries, "never dreaming" that would go away.
- Repo was "a one-on-one business", not a marketplace: a nervous lender could just do the same repo "across the street". On rumors many stopped, and Bear hit a liquidity crisis "pretty much overnight", "the equivalent of the run on the banks in the 30s".
- He had urged the Fed to open its discount window to non-commercial banks, which now held many of the good assets. The answer: "that's a good point but we don't have the authority to do it".
- The rescue: he called Jamie Dimon because JPMorgan, Bear's clearing bank, held \~35 billion of Bear's assets and could see a credit line would be safe. Dimon brought in the Treasury and the Fed; a weekend deal "still didn't stop the markets", and a second one followed the next week.
- Financial media such as CNBC passed on short sellers' rumors. He told the SEC to pull major reporters' phone records to see how many callers were hedge funds short Bear's or Lehman's credit: it was "the way for short sellers to yell fire".
- Crisis decisions: "IQ plus emotional level equals 100% of IQ", so the more emotional you are, the less of your brain you can use. He calls a time-out, asks a trusted in-house lawyer "anything I'm missing here" and does "not react quickly".
- Guggenheim was built from scratch as a full-service firm (banking, sales and trading, research, fixed-income and equity capital markets) with one culture: "I don't believe in monolines". Successful firms "all looked the same on a spreadsheet" but were known by the business they grew up in; only Goldman built both at once.
- Change: "humans are bred to habituate", and each layer up an organization resists change more. Anchor change in fixed principles (client first, transparency): the product "wasn't what made us successful solving the problem was".
- For juniors building a model: ask what it is for, which client question it answers and "are we looking at the right group of comps".
- Winners used to be whoever had the breakthrough technology. Steve Jobs led the shift to winning by putting existing pieces together into something people want; today's winners also build a network effect of users.
