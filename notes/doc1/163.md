### 163. Glenn Dubin — Highbridge as a process business, and alpha in capacity-constrained niches (Generating Alpha Podcast, Mar 2025)

*Co-founder and former CEO of Highbridge Capital Management (started 1992, fully bought by J.P. Morgan Asset Management in 2009), now principal of his family office, Dubin & Co.; a career interview, his first podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6Z10HzK3i-w)

**Takeaway:** Highbridge was built as a franchise, not a star-trader shop: uncorrelated, process-driven strategies run by teams, with the founders as business builders and risk managers. His family office now looks for alpha in small and middle-market niches that big managers have outgrown, and backs roll-ups of fragmented service businesses.

- Fund-of-funds roots: Dubin & Swieca, started in 1984, had over $1 billion of assets by the early 1990s. It earned a 1% management fee and a 5% incentive fee above a 10% hurdle, while the managers it picked earned 2% and 20% (some 4% and 30%). So the partners chose to be principals rather than agents.
- Highbridge started in 1992 with $35 million, first in US and then non-US convertible arbitrage. It added strategies "uncorrelated to one another", applying the fund-of-funds' portfolio theory to in-house teams to cut volatility and raise the whole fund's return.
- Build for franchise value: the firm was named after a Northern Manhattan neighborhood, not the founders, because Steinhardt Partners had no franchise value once Michael Steinhardt retired. Dubin and Henry Swieca were the business builders, risk managers and asset allocators, never the traders, and chose "process oriented businesses" where the portfolio manager is part of a team, not one star stock picker or currency trader.
- Convertible arbitrage works like "a non-bank bank": lend to non-investment-grade companies below the market rate in return for a call option on the stock, and short the stock to hedge the loan. Risk arbitrage underwrites the probability that a stock-for-stock deal closes: buy the target, short the acquirer and "deliver the package over a period of time for a defined rate of return".
- J.P. Morgan bought a strategic interest in 2005, when Highbridge was a $7 billion single-product multi-strategy fund, because the alpha did not depend on the founders: it "really did have franchise value above and beyond just the two of us". When he left at the start of 2013 it was a $35 billion alternatives firm with physical commodities, merchant energy, quant equity and private credit (HPS, Highbridge Principal Strategies).
- Family office (since 2013): only active businesses where his experience gives an edge, since allocating to outside managers is "pretty boring". First deal: with an investor group, at arm's length, he bought the Louis Dreyfus family's and Highbridge's stakes in their merchant-energy joint venture, now Castleton Commodities International, with Dubin & Co. as lead shareholder and himself as executive chair.
- Engineers Gate, a quant-equity multi-manager platform seeded in the family office, needed sophisticated technology, risk systems and historical data, so the J-curve was deep and becoming profitable "takes a long time". It was spun out to take third-party capital in 2020, with Greg Eisner as CEO.
- Common theme: small and middle-market niches that large managers can't play for liquidity or capacity reasons. Great managers start small, grow, "move up market" and leave a void. He looks for "markets or strategies or sectors that still have a lot of alpha" but limited capacity: energy assets "below the radar" of the majors, capacity-constrained quant strategies, and seeded small biotech and consumer private-equity funds.
- Outside money vs own money: running third-party capital made Highbridge more risk-averse, very concerned about drawdowns, consistency and "delivering bad news to your investors". With family capital the "emotional volatility" is much lower, which helps in markets like early 2025, "not for the faint of heart" (Mar 2025).
- Leadership "is most important during difficult times" and shows in bear markets and dislocations. Judging talent is pattern recognition built over 44–45 years, and "not foolproof".
- Paul Tudor Jones's 1987 crash call rested on an analog: 1986–87 markets looked "eerily similar" to the 1928–29 charts. He was "only right about the crash and not right about the depression" he expected next; that conviction led to the Robin Hood Foundation (early 1988), which Dubin co-founded.
- Trend (Mar 2025): roll-ups of fragmented service businesses, from home services (his son's garage-door roll-up) to accounting, insurance brokerage, plumbing, HVAC and landscaping. Gains come from best practices (digital advertising, procurement savings, training, cross-selling, now AI). Upside is venture-like but more likely, because you buy existing businesses at "very reasonable multiples" and earn the multiple arbitrage on a sale or IPO. Still "relatively early days".
- Advice to his younger self: slow down; careers run for decades and "experience is going to be a compounding asset".
