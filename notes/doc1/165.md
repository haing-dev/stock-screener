### 165. Barry Ritholtz — How Not to Invest: win the loser's game by making fewer mistakes (Generating Alpha Podcast, Apr 2025)

*Co-founder, chairman and CIO of Ritholtz Wealth Management and host of the Masters in Business podcast; a career interview with a student host about his book How Not to Invest (bad ideas, bad numbers, bad behavior).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8IrCaF_pkDg)

**Takeaway:** Investing, like amateur tennis, is a loser's game: most investors, institutions included, lose through unforced errors such as overtrading, market timing and panic selling. Make fewer decisions, argue the other side before betting, and be choosy about whose views you consume.

- Loser's game (Charlie Ellis's 1970s paper, later Winning the Loser's Game): pros win points by "generating alpha". Amateurs, including "foundations and endowments and institutions", lose by overtrading, taking too much or too little risk, ignoring taxes, timing the market and picking stocks. The fix: "just make fewer decisions, make fewer mistakes".
- Argue both sides (his lesson from law school moot court): if you think stocks are overvalued, "you should be able to articulate what happens if stocks aren't overvalued". Think in probabilities, not up or down; most outcomes land in "the big fat middle".
- Short-run results are noisy: on his trading desk, the neighbor making money one month lost it the next, and vice versa; "it seemed almost random". That led him to behavioral finance (Schwager's Market Wizards, Gilovich's How We Know What Isn't So): "My entire operating system is not designed to manage this at all."
- Panic selling is the costliest error. A study after the 2008–09 crash found that of those who panicked out, "31% of them never returned to equities". They left with stocks "on sale 57% off" and missed "one of the greatest decades in market history", often out of embarrassment and ego.
- FOMO "is nothing more than greed by another name"; chasing the hot stock "rarely seems to work". Bill Bernstein (neurologist turned investor): investors who fail to control their limbic, fight-or-flight system "will die poor".
- Hindsight-bias test (talks in 2010–12): two-thirds of an audience said they saw the financial crisis coming. Asked who had shorted Lehman, Bear Stearns or AIG, bank or homebuilder stocks, or the market, or even moved into bonds and cash, almost no hands went up.
- Market commentary is mostly narrative fallacy. During the Iraq war one Wall Street Journal story first had oil rising as allies accidentally destroyed a mosque; when oil reversed that day, the headline said oil fell despite it: "they have no idea why these things are happening". He passed on a daily TV show for this reason.
- Media diet: as a trader he saved articles for the ride home because "whatever the last voice in my head was affected my outlook and affected my trading". Media sells your attention to advertisers, so "most of the media out there does not have your best interest at heart".
- Discount what CEOs say: he stopped believing conference calls once he saw management cared about the quarter and "getting people to buy their product and their stock". Follow people "who've lived through a few cycles" with "a process that's defendable".
- Say "I don't know": anyone who asked why blood is drawn from a vein would have learned a finger-stick damages the sample. That is why "none of the medical technology, biotech, other health related hedge funds and venture capital funds" invested in Theranos. Nobody was willing to say "I don't understand this", and "$10 billion of capital" was lost. Enron was the same.
- Luck is the most common answer to his closing question ("what do you know now that would have been useful when you were first starting out?"). Howard Marks told him the people he worked with were "every bit as hardworking, every bit as smart": "Sometimes you just get lucky."
- His reading process: \~40 tabs most mornings across major papers, plus Wall Street research from people who "have earned my trust". After \~25 years of a daily reading list he can tell "in a sentence or two" whether a piece is worth finishing. He takes pushback from proven investors such as Cliff Asness (AQR), not anonymous accounts: "Who are you listening to and why?"
