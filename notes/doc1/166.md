### 166. Howard Marks — Buying things well: price, risk and building Oaktree (Generating Alpha Podcast, May 2025)

*Co-founder and co-chairman of Oaktree Capital Management, the distressed-debt and credit manager; a career interview on the Generating Alpha podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aeALq7jNlk)

**Takeaway:** Success comes from buying things well, not from buying good things: no asset is too good to become overpriced, and few are too bad to be cheap enough. Risk is acceptable when you can quantify it, diversify it and are paid enough to bear it.

- Nifty Fifty lesson: they were "the best and fastest growing companies in America" with "no price too high". Bought when Marks started at Citi (Sep 1969) and held for five years, "you lost about 95% of your money".
- His conclusion: "success in investing doesn't come from buying good things but from buying things well". No asset is so good it can't become "overpriced and dangerous", and "very few things" are so bad they can't be cheap enough. Asked when price matters: "Always."
- Edge sits in neglected niches. Convertibles were an "inefficient market niche that most people didn't care about or know about", where hard work and skill paid. In large-cap equities "very few people can" get an advantage.
- Depth beats breadth: as Citi's director of research (at 29) his job was "two sentences on 400 companies". In the bond department he had to know "everything on 30 companies", and was "ecstatic".
- Distressed-debt logic: a bond bought at 100 occasionally defaults and drops to 10; negotiate well with a valuable claim and you might recover 30 in the restructuring. Bruce Karsh's distressed fund appealed because "what if you could skip the 100 to 10 part and just do the 10 to 30 part?" The first fund (Oct 1988) raised $65M, then 96 at a second closing.
- "if you are willing to do things that nobody else is, you might get a bargain". Lending to single-B companies, then buying bankrupt companies' debt, both looked crazy at first, and raising money was hard.
- Risk is a quantity, not good or bad: life insurers insure lives knowing everyone dies. "you can take risk intelligently if you think about it, if you can quantify it, if you can diversify, and if you're well paid". Assume some defaults, compute their impact against the compensation, and use numerical tools to do it.
- A great investor is numerate, then has insight into an asset's potential, the probability of achieving it and the risk of failure. The test is whether "the upside potential more than merits taking the downside risk".
- Outcomes are balls drawn from a bag of possibilities. The superior investor knows when the bag is 75% winners and "worth playing", and when it is 25% and not.
- Data alone is no edge: "everybody has the same data". His son Andrew, a venture capitalist, argued that "readily available quantitative information about the present cannot make you a success". The edge is the data's implications, qualitative factors (management, product pipeline, research effort) or the future.
- Read psychology, not forecasts: are people too optimistic (prices likely high relative to intrinsic value) or too pessimistic? On which asset class will pay in 10 years: "I have no idea."
- Oaktree's growth was "demographic luck": after the S&P fell in 2000, 2001 and 2002 (the first three-year decline since 1939) and rates were cut, investors turned to "alternative investing" (\~'04–'06). Oaktree started with \~$5bn in 1995 and has \~$200bn today (May 2025).
- A partnership needs shared values and complementary skills. Marks is the fast, conceptual thinker who meets clients; Karsh is the slow, analytical one who runs the investing.
- Screen implication: a screen only sorts the data everyone has, so it narrows the list but is not the edge. Price must enter the decision even for the best companies.
