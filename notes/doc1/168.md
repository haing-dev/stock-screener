### 168. Dan Ariely — The vintage Swiss Army knife: intuition, long-term effects and the last chapter of life (Generating Alpha Podcast, May 2025)

*James B. Duke Professor of Behavioral Economics at Duke University and author of Predictably Irrational and The Honest Truth About Dishonesty; an interview with a 15-year-old host on intuition, the brain, decisions and end-of-life choices.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Xke-jNcGmk)

**Takeaway:** Intuition predicts how a choice will feel on day one but not its long-term effects, and our brain is a "vintage" toolkit built for a different world. Take the outside view, treat life as a portfolio of decisions and take more risk; he now applies this to the mistakes people make at the end of life.

- The half-beard story: burned at almost 18, he shaved his scarred face for most of his life. After a month-long hike he kept a half beard, expecting stares. About four months later, at 50, he suddenly felt better about the injury and no longer at war with it ("the pain is the same"). He still gets "multiple notes a month" from people who say they will stop hiding their own injuries.
- The lesson: day one is easy to predict, but "a journey of self-acceptance is not something we have good intuition about"; in general, "what we're not good is predicting long-term effects". Social science should surface "half a beard sort of things": choices "your intuition say don't do it, but in fact are very good".
- Progress has hidden costs: we see the gains (kids reading earlier, programming by third grade), but "What we're not so good at is understanding what we're giving up". His elementary school near Tel Aviv ran 8 a.m. to noon with no homework; he believes "we are depriving kids of childhood" in some developed countries.
- How he picks problems: size the gap between "where we are now and where I think we could be" (how we use money, the environment, health). He takes on a problem only if the gap is big and the fix lies within social science, not medicine or policy ("the war in Ukraine").
- Current project, the "last chapter": the time from a terminal diagnosis to death, "slightly longer than five years on average" in the Western world. The medical system makes people see themselves as patients: "You're an illness first".
- Interviews with about a hundred end-of-life doulas found four parts to a good death: no pain, no loss of dignity in the body, being surrounded by love, and a sense of legacy. Medicine is close on pain and gaining on dignity; the last two are social problems, and with five years "Legacy is is solvable".
- About 80% of Americans want to die at home, but "Only 20% slightly more" do: someone faints, an ambulance takes them, and once on machines "nobody's going to disconnect them". Many regret extra treatment taken "to please the doctor, to please their families". The result is "incredibly expensive" and "Everybody loses".
- As a trained end-of-life doula he helped a man on open-ended chemotherapy, who first moved his treatment so he could go back to Vermont to make maple syrup with his wife, as when they met. Told stopping was his decision, he stopped, sparing himself and his family "a tremendous amount of of suffering".
- The brain is a "vintage Swiss Army knife": "not particularly good at anything" but does many things "kind of okay" in a compact package. It was "honed in a very very different time", with no cryptocurrency, no school choice and no living to 80.
- Old tools misfire: gossip enforced good behavior in small communities with "no police force and no judicial system"; on social media we still spread bad news, but "it doesn't do any good". A taste for sugar, salt and fat helped when food was scarce: "Now we have donuts."
- Design choice: car engineers study people's mistakes to reduce them; social media and information technology study the same mistakes, then "push them to make more of these mistakes". His last book was on misinformation.
- Human capital: his group tracks "how companies treat their employees and how the employees feel about the company and the consequence in the stock market" (its "happy index" \[?\]). When companies invest in a warehouse "it's an investment", but spending on people is treated as a cost, which it "shouldn't be". Bureaucracy "kills human capital".
- Better decisions: rationality is not always the goal ("Poetry is wonderful. Falling in love is wonderful."). When it is, take the outside view: "What would I advise somebody else?" Then "Give it and then turn around and take it."
- Advice to a 15-year-old: "life eventually is a portfolio of decisions", and judging each one alone makes "a life that is too timid". "Take more risk", fail more and treat failure as "the entry ticket" to success.
