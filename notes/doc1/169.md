### 169. Scott Bok — Surviving Wall Street: culture, leverage and M&A for scale or focus (Generating Alpha Podcast, May 2025)

*Chairman and former CEO of Greenhill & Co., the independent M&A advisory firm he joined in 1997; a career interview around his book Surviving Wall Street.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ymlr7QsbspE)

**Takeaway:** Most Wall Street firms from his early career are gone; the survivors kept strong cultures and avoided leverage that only works in up markets. Good M&A is strategic, done for scale or for focus, as Gannett's decade of spin-offs, sales and TV-station purchases showed.

- Since he graduated, the Dow is "up 40 times", private equity owns 30,000 companies and M&A runs to trillions of dollars a year. Yet "a majority of the firms in business when I started go out of business".
- Survivors share two traits: strong cultures (at Morgan Stanley "you didn't use the word I") and risk management. Many firms washed away after taking on leverage or riskier businesses, "too eager to sort of amp up their earnings": that "works great in an up market", but when it turns it can "sink you in a hurry".
- Greenhill has been through five crises. The GFC was the biggest since the 1930s, and like COVID it shows once-in-a-century events happen. Big, respected names like Lehman "can disappear": "Don't think any place is kind of impregnable or bulletproof".
- With no big balance sheet, pure advisory Greenhill was "out of the line of fire" in the financial crisis. It stayed busy and hired "refugees from the big banks" who were fed up with the risk in those models.
- M&A happens for two reasons: scale ("synergy to just being bigger") or focus. Gannett, a client for 10–12 deals, used both over \~10 years.
- Gannett: newspapers were "an extraordinarily attractive business for a long time", but advertising moved to TV, so "your TV assets ought to be separate from your newspaper assets". It spun off the newspapers and split off the faster-growing digital units (cars.com, apartments.com, CareerBuilder) by spin-offs or sales.
- The broadcaster left behind, Tegna, became one of the biggest US TV groups and then bought more stations for scale. M&A is "not just deal making", nor just buying cheap: "There's something very very strategic about a lot of M&A."
- Dealmaking skills change with seniority. Juniors run the numbers: does it "make sense to pay $40 a share for this company, but not 42", and what is the right debt level? Seniors judge whether a deal is worth doing, the price and the capital structure.
- The hardest call: "do you pay the incremental dollar to acquire the business or do you let your competitor outbid you". Even good judgments "aren't worth anything if the client doesn't really trust you".
- Big banks have people, brand, marketing budgets and many products, so each "always has a reason to call on the clients". A focused firm wins by being nimble and serving the client's specific need: no structure or business model is "by definition a winner".
- Culture: flat teams, aligned with clients, never pushing a deal for the fee. Greenhill parted ways with "sharp-elbowed, political" people, even rainmakers: "it just wasn't worth risking the culture of our firm".
- IPO idea (summer 2003): Greenhill was better known, with more growth potential, higher margins and a bigger brand than some listed companies. He waited for one more strong year and made every policy (pay, stock ownership) "very very investor-friendly" to take no chances. It was the first such firm to list, "16 times oversubscribed", and the stock roughly quadrupled in 18 months.
- Where Wall Street makes money swings like a pendulum: trading vs banking, big vs small firms, retail in and out of favor. A trading leader peaking in 2009 was unlucky; one rising in 2002–03 had "nothing but upside for many years".
- Bill Gates is the CEO he admires most because "companies that are great one generation are gone in the next. That's true in every industry." Microsoft, started \~50 years ago, kept evolving and still at times ranks as the world's most valuable company.
