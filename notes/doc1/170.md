### 170. Asher Edelman — From merger arbitrage to corporate raider: hidden hard assets, The Art of War and art finance (Generating Alpha Podcast, May 2025)

*1980s corporate raider and activist (one of the inspirations for Gordon Gekko in the film Wall Street, per the host), founder of Edelman Companies, former Columbia Business School teacher, art collector and art financier; a career interview with a student host.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=62t3unEyP5M)

**Takeaway:** Edelman moved from merger arbitrage to raiding once he saw that arbitrageurs carry the risk of other people's deals. His edge was hard assets the market ignored because shares trade on earnings and growth: Canal-Randolph, a real estate company "hiding behind an auction business", paid out \~110 against his average cost in the high 20s.

- Why he became a raider: running merger arbitrage, he was "taking the risk for the companies who are buying other companies"; if a deal broke, "we're the ones who are going to lose". Better to be the buyer and let the arbitrageurs carry the risk. He was "always a value player".
- Canal-Randolph: a block was offered to him as a livestock-auction business ("cows and pigs and sheep"). Reading the file over a weekend, he found "basically a real estate holding company". It sold "in the low 20s", his back-of-the-envelope said "its worth in the 80s", and the real estate specialists he hired (Eastdil) came back with "a similar number".
- Why it was cheap: "in those days shares didn't sell against their hard assets at all"; they sold on earnings, cash flow and growth, "and they still don't really" (May 2025). It was also "a very quiet company".
- The payoff: he bought the block, then fought for control, at one point having to "risk all of the rest of my money", which "isn't my nature". On an average cost "in the high 20s", the company paid out "90 some odd dollars in the first year" from selling real estate, and \~110 in all on full liquidation.
- The pattern repeated: many later targets were computer companies, not because he liked tech but because "a lot of them had a lot of inactive assets", such as land bought for future buildings, which he liquidated. Results: "some of them worked very well, some of them worked okay, and a couple of them didn't work at all".
- Edge: know "what it is that you know better than everyone else or think you do", then ask why the stock is undervalued. Puhov \[?\] had a breakthrough in truck brakes, but there was "enough value there to support the company the company's price without the breakthrough", so the breakthrough was the upside: "it's almost a stock pick".
- Know your limits: running operating companies was "not my my bag". Where he ended up owning the operations, "you really had to change a corporate culture", so he brought in other people and got out of most of them.
- Early quant roots: Harry Markowitz worked for him, he says, and they developed hedging and arbitrage techniques; in the CBOE's early years "we were the biggest traders on the CBOE".
- Herding: by the 1980s raiding became "institutionalized" and "a bit rapacious", and Wall Street's ethics "a zoo", so he stopped. Most people then "just followed everyone else around"; the winners were creative and "first into the transactions that made sense".
- The Art of War is "an appropriate textbook for life"; he used to reread it every month and still does before any challenging move. A "spy" is just "someone in management who wasn't necessarily giving him any inside information, but who liked him"; know "when to withdraw and when to move forward"; avoid a war won with "nothing left".
- His Columbia Business School seminar on corporate raiding through The Art of War was capped at 15 students. Each presented a case for the others to attack, covering the assets, the businesses and "the psychology of the management": how to get managers "either on their team or to or to get rid of them".
- On today's activists, he names Dan Loeb as among those who have succeeded but does not admire his approach: "I don't think it's really very solidly based but it works". Once people start to succeed in activism, "they really become very caught up in themselves".
- Art as an asset: "lenders make more money than galleries" and more than collectors ("Well, not all collectors, but most"). A secured lender to galleries such as Leo Castelli and Mary Boone from the 1970s, he was "very early into guarantees and very early out of guarantees". He now runs what he calls, by paintings and artists, "the largest fractionalization company in the world", backing emerging and re-emerging artists; it was "profitable immediately" in its first year.
- Building an "eye": "nobody is born with an eye" and "reading doesn't give you an eye". He looked at art two days a week from 18 or 19 until well into his 60s and views new work "raw". He was a major collector of Cy Twombly "when people didn't know who he was" and of Brice Marden, bought his first Jasper Johns in 1961, and found an artist in \~2021 who had "not really ever sold a picture" and has since sold about 300.
