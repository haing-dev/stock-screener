### 171. Tully Friedman — "We buy companies not models": people, advantages and the end of cheap money (Generating Alpha Podcast, Jun 2025)

*Co-founder of Hellman & Friedman (1984) and Friedman Fleischer & Lowe (1997), and earlier a Salomon Brothers banker; his first podcast interview.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YCoxAquwS2Y)

**Takeaway:** Private equity can no longer buy cheap, lever up and sell; returns need operating improvement, and no corner of the market is uncompetitive. The decisive call is on the people, and even careful buyers get management wrong half the time.

- Hellman & Friedman began as advisers because "no one cared about our capital, but everyone cared about getting our advice". Stanford's treasurer then led its first raise: $300 million for "plain old good deals" (not venture), backing "good businesses led by people we wanted to really be partners with".
- Unusually for the time, "we didn't care if we had control or not with the right people". Two more funds followed, and he raised and deployed "something over $2 billion" while there.
- Wins: the Levi Strauss buyout, the largest to that date, "put us on the map" though the firm had no real buyout experience. Friedman Fleischer & Lowe's first $300 million fund "returned a billion dollars of profit", with Tempur-Pedic "a meaningful part", a deal that was "very risky, very hard to decide to do".
- By 1997 the alternative-asset niches (venture, buyouts, hedge funds; Yale's David Swensen was an early backer) had been "competed away". You "no longer could find if you will cheap investments, buy them, leverage them and sell them on"; you had to "make an operating improvement in the companies to get full value out of the deals".
- Smaller deals are no refuge. That the middle market is less competitive is a "fantasy": "There's just too much money, too many smart people trying to do stuff", and private equity is "just not as interesting as it used to be".
- Investment talent is rare and innate: "very few really good investors in my life", maybe two or three hands' worth. Experience develops it, "But it's a gift". Big firms now rely on process, "the system is the solution", and he is "not sure that system is good enough".
- Proprietary deals are "pretty rare these days". Analysts model and diligence, but partner Spencer Fleischer's rule was "we buy companies not models". The senior questions: are the analysts right, do the businesses have "differentiated advantages", and "do we want to be in business with these people?"
- Good management is "pretty uncommon". He was told almost 60% of the CEOs of bought companies are replaced within two or three years; his firms' rate was 50%. They foresaw half of those and lined up a successor, but "half of them we didn't know were going to change out".
- The recent deal frenzy, with preemptive processes and less time to study prospects, means "people are spending less and less time with management": "a real shortfall". Even during COVID he insisted on meeting management or a long Zoom call.
- His checklist: partners with competence and character who take and give criticism; "competitive advantages"; few commodity businesses; "a path to a lot of growth"; and a business "where the growth will be rewarded", since the "S&P 493" lacks big multiples.
- Mistakes: "maybe you shouldn't do shipping deals. They're capital intensive." Crown Zellerbach, a forest-products client he and Warren Hellman spent two years fixing, had "issued too much debt to build green field paper mills".
- As competition grew, firms specialized in "narrower and narrower things"; he preferred being a generalist. Arthur Brooks told him research finds lucky people "better at recognizing opportunity than most people" with "more tolerance for risk".
- Markets (Jun 2025): the past 10 years were "a real bubble in financial assets and markets driven by both fiscal and monetary policy", run by people who have "never suffered the reverse". Caution cost him returns, "but I've slept better".
- Outlook: "the era of cheap money, massive amounts of equity flowing into all these niches is over". With geopolitical risk, weaker leadership and a US that can't fund both its welfare state and a defense revival, a younger him would be "very conservative".
