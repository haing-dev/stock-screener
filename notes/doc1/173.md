### 173. Annie Duke — Thinking in bets: luck vs skill, expected value and kill criteria for quitting (Generating Alpha Podcast, Jul 2025)

*Former professional poker player (winner of the 2004 World Series of Poker Tournament of Champions, per the host) who studied cognitive science at the University of Pennsylvania; decision strategist and author of Thinking in Bets and Quit, interviewed by a student host.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E7ZltyNlBvY)

**Takeaway:** Outcomes are a noisy signal of decision quality, and biases such as mental accounting and identity make us hold losers too long. Knowing about the biases does not fix them; structure does: record each decision, set kill criteria when you enter a position, and have someone hold you to them.

- Poker and investing share a feedback problem: there is "so much luck involved" and you rarely see the other cards, so "closing feedback loops become really hard". Self-serving bias fills the gap: we blame luck for bad results and credit skill for good ones.
- Risk attitude depends on the path (the subject of her 2002 talk to a hedge fund's options traders): when losing, people turn risk-seeking to get their money back; when winning, risk-averse to protect the gain. This is prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky), "known in science since 1979" and "very well replicated".
- At the table, losing players play more hands, raise more and are "much less willing to quit the game"; winners (say, bought in for $200 and now "winning like $350") get pickier and leave to "lock the gains up". Across sessions it flips: after a 3-week winning streak players come in too risk-seeking because they overrate their skill; after a long losing streak, too risk-averse.
- Archetype, not stereotype: she starts each opponent on two scales, tight vs loose (how many hands) and aggressive vs passive (raising vs calling), from context. A retirement-age player at 11 a.m. on a Wednesday in a local casino starts tight and passive; a Vegas vacationer at 2 a.m. who has "had a few" starts loose and aggressive. Then "I need to start adjusting immediately".
- Luck vs skill: chess has no dice and shows all the pieces, so the winner made the better decisions; "chess isn't actually as hard as most of the other things that you do". A big sample helps, but we "tend to draw conclusions before we have a large enough sample", judging on "sample size that's too small for the volatility" or "just anecdotes".
- The better fix is "an artifact of the decision": record what you knew, the judgments you made and what you forecast at the time. Across many decisions you can then close the feedback loop on how good you are, but "you have to be pretty rigorous about it".
- Expected value is each outcome's probability times the ground it gains or loses toward your goal. A fair coin paying $2 on heads and costing $1 on tails is worth 50 cents a flip, or $5,000 over 10,000 flips. Yet "there's no coin flip where you give me 50 cents", and over three flips you lose all three, down $3, 12.5% of the time. For non-money goals, rate options 0–10 or ask what you would need to be paid to give one up.
- We quit too late: "anybody over the age of 25 and many people under the age of 25 stick to things too long", above all in the losses. Mental accounting: buy at 50, it trades at 40, and you won't close the account in the red, though "It doesn't matter if you own 20 other stocks". The proof: buy at 50, it rises to 75 and falls to 65, and "you will also feel like you're in the losses even though you're not".
- Identity makes it worse: we want to look consistent and smart, and we fear others will judge us harshly for quitting, "even though in general they don't". A lower expected-value holding hurts twice: the position itself, and the money you cannot move into something better.
- Traders who ask each morning "if I weren't holding these positions, would I buy them today?" have the right test, but "that's only the right question if your answer is going to be a rational one", and once you hold the position it won't be. Your confidence rises while accuracy doesn't: "knowing about it doesn't help, but it really feels like it will".
- Kill criteria: when you put a position on, write down the future signals that would make you take it off, hold it or add risk. A stop-loss (buy at 50, sell at 48) is the "brute force" version and "better than nothing" if you follow it. Better is to "link it back to your thesis", e.g. sell if interest rates fall below a set benchmark after a set date.
- Decide in advance: even when the facts are identical (rates up 50 basis points), "it really matters if you decide it in advance than if you're deciding on the fly". Telling someone who will hold you accountable "really, really, really increases the chances that you'll quit".
- How long to decide: overanalyzing "is by definition an irrational thing to do". Weigh long-term impact (a bad restaurant chicken has zero effect a week later; take time hiring a CEO, not an intern) and cost to quit: "the lower the cost to quit, the less effort you ought to put into a decision". Her six-session course spends five sessions on process and the last on "mostly you should go fast".
- Better inputs and calmer choices: ask people "what they think without telling them what you think first". When upset by a bad grade or a stock loss, ask what your goal is before acting; deciding in a "hot state" breeds more bad outcomes, and pre-commitments keep you out of it.
