### 174. Michael Rees — GP stakes, "survivability" and the long runway for private markets (Generating Alpha Podcast, Jul 2025)

*Co-president and a founder of Blue Owl Capital, who built its GP stakes business (Dyal, at Neuberger Berman); a career interview.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qt5aBZmILLU)

**Takeaway:** Rees buys minority stakes in alternative-asset managers and now underwrites whether a firm can outlive its founders, not how long the star stays. He argues private-market returns come from duration, and that alternatives (\~$13 trillion) are only in the "second or third inning" (Jul 2025).

- Origin: on Lehman's strategy team (2000–01) he worked out which asset managers to buy outright, which to build and which to buy a piece of "to capture the economics of without controlling it". That became the GP stakes business, run at Neuberger Berman after Lehman's bankruptcy and part of Blue Owl since its 2021 IPO.
- Lehman's GP-stake deals were arm's-length: "pay me some money and leave me alone. In fact, don't show up." Dyal turned this into a strategic partnership. With about 70 partner firms it sees what works, and a 60-person business services team helps each partner reach best-in-class.
- Underwriting flipped too. For the first 12–15 years the goal was to "lock that person to the desk": keep the senior founder in charge as long as possible. Now the test is whether the firm has "the framework and the mechanisms", financial arrangements included, for great people to leave and the next generation to take over.
- The core test is "survivability": "more firms go under because of internal reasons than because of external factors. They just can't seem to get that transition right."
- Patient sourcing: the average deal takes "seven or eight years" from first meeting a founder to closing; some take a year or two, some a decade. Some firms first meet them at fund two. He likens it to a marriage.
- Private-market alpha comes from duration: investors give up day-to-day liquidity, and the best managers can "buy a company, add value to it, and on their timeline sell it". They are never forced sellers if assets and liabilities are matched.
- Blue Owl's products (GP stakes, real assets, direct lending) aim to pay that long-term return "consistently over time through yield", so investors never hit a moment when they "have to sell something".
- Firm design: be the market leader or "darn close" in each business (direct lending, triple-net lease, data centers and digital infrastructure, GP stakes). Shareholders get mostly management-fee profits; the "more volatile and harder to measure" carried interest goes to employees as pay, because "Public shareholders want something that they know, they can measure, and it can be consistent."
- Crises are constant (rate hikes, the election, the "tariff tantrum"): "If it doesn't feel like a crisis, something's wrong." Calm means everyone is too happy and a crisis is likely near; "it's never as bad as it seems and it's probably never as good as it seems either".
- Size is no shield: he thought Lehman, then the fourth-biggest investment bank, was "big enough and stable enough that it would survive basically anything". The government hadn't let Bear Stearns fail, but "they had to make a example out of someone".
- Runway (Jul 2025): alternatives took 25 years to grow from a niche into a \~$13 trillion industry. Adoption looks like one S-curve but is many: wealthy individuals, endowments and foundations into hedge funds 30 years ago (then under $1 trillion), then pensions, sovereign wealth, the wealth channel and insurance; 401(k)s are next.
- The ideal private-markets investor is young: a 21-year-old who won't need the money for 50 years. "Some amount of that $500 should go into privates" when someone opens a 401(k) in a target-date fund.
- AI and data is the busiest team serving partners, since every partner wants to avoid being "left behind". He expects "more change in the next three to five years than we've seen really across financial services historically", and prepares partners for deal sourcing done largely by AI models and 10 hours, not 100, to evaluate a new opportunity.
