### 175. Jim Chanos — Five models of fraud, pro-forma games and sizing shorts (Generating Alpha Podcast, Jul 2025)

*Short seller, founder of Kynikos Associates and known for calling Enron, who also teaches a class on the history of financial fraud; a career interview on the Generating Alpha podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=29C0N5AP9PY)

**Takeaway:** Fraud follows recognizable patterns (pressure, a big idea, easy credit, profits built on assumptions rather than cash) and is revealed after the cycle turns. Today (Jul 2025) it hides "in plain sight" in pro-forma numbers; on the short side being right is not enough, so positions stay small.

- First short, Baldwin-United (1982): it sold annuities at what looked like a negative spread yet reported huge profits. It assumed how long policies would persist and what they would earn, then booked almost the entire value at sale ("foreshadowing of Enron"). His rule: "if you read the company's 10K three times and still can't figure out how they make their money", "you better dig deeper".
- Right but early: the sell/short report came out on 17 Aug 1982, the day the market bottomed. The stock more than doubled over 3–4 months despite public evidence in Arkansas insurance files (probable insolvency, double-pledged collateral). It filed for bankruptcy in early 1983: "you can be right, your timing can be wrong".
- Five models from his fraud class. Micro: the fraud triangle of pressure, opportunity and rationalization. Macro (Kindleberger–Minsky): bigger waves of fraud are revealed in the downturn after long bull markets, driven by a big idea ("displacement") plus easy credit. Past ideas were mass production and consumer credit (1920s), the space race (1960s) and the internet (1990s); now "we probably say it's something around AI".
- Frauds lag the cycle because they need financing: "A lot of frauds have negative cash flow or have growing by acquisition". When capital markets shut they can't raise money, roll over debt or keep trade credit. In bull markets "promises trade at a premium", while in bear markets "reality trades at a discount".
- Governance model (Bill Black, *The Best Way to Rob a Bank Is to Own One*): fraudsters use the corporation "as both a weapon and a shield". Options, bonus plans and deals with affiliates or relatives loot it; limited liability and expert sign-offs defend it, so "financial crimes come with the alibis already built in".
- Legal-fraud model (from Bethany McLean after Enron): "everything the company does is technically legal, but yet there's an intent to deceive". Board, lawyer and auditor sign-offs make prosecution hard, but investors still lose money.
- Ethics checklist (Marianne Jennings, *The Seven Signs of Ethical Collapse*): "innovation like no other company", a young staff around a larger-than-life celebrity CEO, and "goodness in some areas atones for evil in others". Fraudulent companies are often among the most charitable and respected in their communities.
- Where fraud hides now (Jul 2025): "the misuse of pro-forma numbers", which companies now lead with in their releases. IBM has taken restructuring charges "for 10 of the last 12 years or something like that", he thinks: "if you're doing it 10 out of the last 12 years, it's not non-recurring". Stock compensation is routinely added back, yet "It's compensation. You're diluting your shareholders".
- Valeant (2014–15) built its model on pro-forma accounting. It bought drugs near patent expiry, booked the price as acquired R&D or goodwill and told investors to ignore the amortization. At $260 (from $130; it later hit $5), next year's pro-forma cash EPS estimate was \~$18–20 while GAAP showed losses. Drugs losing patents in 3–4 years were written off over 10–11 years: "they're purchasing their R&D. They're not developing their R&D."
- Short-side temperament: Wall Street is "a giant positive reinforcement machine", so a short seller is told he's wrong every morning, though "95 to 99%" of it is noise already priced in. Good short analysts stick to their facts when the stock rises; the portfolio manager decides on exits and size.
- Sizing: long indices, short idiosyncratic names (mid-1990s until recently), whose volatility may be 2x the market's. Limit 5% per position (4% in practice), 40–60 names, \~1.5% average given betas above 1. AOL rose 12x in the late 1990s, but a 1% position you keep trimming won't "carry you out on a stretcher". Also watch industry concentration and market-cap mix.
- The edge moved from getting information (10-Qs days ahead, until the mid-1990s) to filtering a fire hose after EDGAR and Reg FD. AI output so far is "almost harmful": "supercharged" consensus (Jul 2025).
- Learn accounting, including how the three statements interact and the footnotes: "you can take apart a business by reading their financial statements".
- Screen implication: GAAP-based rules avoid pro-forma games, but recurring "non-recurring" charges, stock pay added back in cash flow and acquisition-fuelled growth need explicit checks.
