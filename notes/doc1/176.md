### 176. R. Martin Chavez — Digital twins, SecDB and how AI changes the work in finance (Generating Alpha Podcast, Aug 2025)

*Vice chairman and partner at Sixth Street and an Alphabet board member; formerly Goldman Sachs CIO, CFO and co-head of the securities division; a career interview.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o-bnp9vl1xA)

**Takeaway:** Chavez built his career on "digital twins": software models of a business that can blow up in simulation instead of in reality. Goldman's SecDB let it act on 57 defaulting Lehman counterparties within hours. He sees AI as a repeat of trading algos: it changes what people do, and those who collaborate with the machines win.

- Digital twins: "the value of a digital twin is you can blow up the digital twin", inspect what is left, "run it backwards in time" and ask what you could have done differently. The math he first used to simulate neutron bombs is "the same math used to price financial derivatives and to manage risk".
- Goldman's rule when he joined (1993): "The only thing crazier than writing all your own software is not writing all your own software." SecDB first modeled the FX business "so that we could lose money in software simulation without losing it in reality", which gave it an edge. On a hostile oil desk (1994) he won over the head trader by automating risk and P&L: "I will get you out of the office within 15 minutes of the market close".
- SecDB grew over 25 years into Goldman's firm-wide risk platform. In 2008, knowing the firm's risk against all 57 defaulting Lehman counterparties became "not a useful thing to know but an existential thing to know". Goldman delivered ISDA close-out notices at Lehman's headquarters "within hours of their bankruptcy"; a year later other firms were asking for help with the same task.
- Owners spend like owners: the J. Aron commodities trading floor was "a dump". In hindsight, the partners spent nothing on decor "because it's a partnership and that would be money coming out of their own pocket".
- Preparation beats timing: a friend put his success down to "the right place at the right time" and "the right preparation". You control only the preparation, and "you only think you know what you're preparing for".
- AI timing lesson: in 1993 AI "had hit a wall", and a wave of AI businesses "all failed. They all got bought for scrap". Reed Hastings, from his Stanford circle, used techniques too weak for medicine on a smaller problem, "what movie to watch tonight": "the right early application for that time".
- His Stanford camp built expert systems that "were really brittle and didn't scale". The neural-network camp they thought "nuts" won after 30 years of Moore's law doubling every 18 months. Only since the ChatGPT moment of late 2022 has scaling been obvious to all; hallucinations, reasoning and whether "more compute is all you need" remain open (Aug 2025).
- Sixth Street: alternatives are "the last frontier on Wall Street for people with my skill set". Deals are complex and custom (structured multi-tranche investments in middle-market companies, a deal to help Real Madrid build its new stadium), "Typically modeled in spreadsheets" and then stacked into fund models with cross-collateralized waterfalls. He is building the platform to replace the spreadsheets, as Goldman once moved trading risk off Lotus 1-2-3.
- Algos were early AI agents that "autonomously put buy and sell orders into the exchange. What could possibly go wrong? Well, the answer is lots and lots and lots of things did go wrong."
- Three strategies he gave the equities division as its new co-head, and still gives (2025): tell the computers what to do; "collaborate with the computers and the people who tell the computers what to do"; or stonewall them, "a dumb strategy" he would end careers over. Traders who claimed a "sniff" for markets were offered a six-month contest against the algos, keeping their jobs only if they made more money.
- Fighting the algos was like racing "my HP12C multiplying 10-digit numbers. I am going to lose." Automate what you do and move on to more interesting work: "there's always something for people to do". Chess is "more popular than at any prior moment in human history" since computers beat the best humans.
- Team diversity as math: "diversification of your team is the other free lunch". Goldman "loved doing experiments with people", such as making him, a fixed-income and commodity derivatives specialist, co-head of equities. Such bets need self-confidence, "a large appetite for experimentation and a suitable time horizon".
- Advice: "Computer science is not coding." Learn "the algorithmic datadriven approach to problem solving and decision making" and apply it to any field, or collaborate with the people who do.
