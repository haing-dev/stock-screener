### 178. Alan Zafran — Risk, time horizons and the fine print of "democratized" private funds (Generating Alpha Podcast, Aug 2025)

*Co-founder and managing partner of IEQ Capital, a $41.7bn wealth manager (per the host), after Goldman Sachs, Merrill Lynch and Luminous Capital; a career interview.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zZTK9fhPudk)

**Takeaway:** Size risk to each client's tolerance, time horizon and the purpose of each pool of money, because 20%+ sell-offs keep coming (he counts 13 or 14). He warns that evergreen private funds sold to individuals promise monthly or quarterly liquidity that can be gated just when markets are under stress.

- Going independent (2008): after 18 years in wealth management and past 40, he decided it was now or never. At a big firm he couldn't offer hedge funds and credit funds he rated highly because they were "not officially approved by the firm", which "felt a bit disingenuous". He and three partners founded Luminous Capital to offer a wider range.
- Risk tolerance errs both ways. Some clients are far too aggressive, like the 80% of Americans who think they are above-average drivers; others are too conservative, and inflation erodes cash. Gauging it "is as much art as science", learned by listening and asking open-ended questions.
- A $10 million client can be harder than a billionaire: there is "less cushion for failure". The work is fundamentals: are goals in line with spending habits, and are there family health costs? Billionaires bring more entities (trusts, family LLCs, homes worldwide), not necessarily a harder path to their goals.
- Every period feels like "the most challenging time I've ever seen in my career", yet "things just tend to work out over time". In a 36-year career (Aug 2025) he counts 13 or 14 equity sell-offs of 20% or more. The defense: decide up front to take only as much risk as you can tolerate.
- Match risk to each pool's purpose. A young saver in a low tax bracket can fund a Roth IRA, buy a no-fee all-equity index fund and "close your eyes", ignoring CPI reports, elections and wars on a 50-year view. Money for a house down payment within three years shouldn't be in stocks: a fall can take several years to recover.
- Risk is variability, which mainly creates chances to buy after prices drop. Using it depends on knowing the investor's temperament, "what entity it's held in" and what the money is for.
- Democratized information should make markets "more efficiently priced", with fewer anomalies. The catch: people may assume easy information lets them take more risk.
- Democratized alternatives (Aug 2025): evergreen private-credit, private-equity and real-estate funds sold to accredited investors promise monthly or quarterly liquidity, but managers can "gate the fund", e.g. capping exits at 2.5% or 5% of capital per period. Values are model estimates, and if everyone wants out at once, "it's probably at a time of stress when prices are probably falling anyway". "So it's buyer beware."
- Marks matter most in open-end (evergreen) funds, where investors enter and exit at model values; in closed-end drawdown funds everyone exits together, so interim marks matter less. He calls it "something to understand", not a proven problem: most managers' methods are "very sophisticated, thoughtful and consistent".
- Spread capital so no single source of liquidity is relied on in a crisis: "don't put too many eggs in one basket".
- Heirs: "actions matter a lot more than words"; children notice whether you fly commercial or private. The same trust structure was "brilliant" for one family and "a disaster" for another, so there is no single right way to pass on wealth.
- Client focus: "If you don't have a client, you don't have a business." On wealth itself: "net worth is not your self-worth".
