### 179. Michael Chu — L Catterton: consumer brands that win on emotion, from P.F. Chang's to Birkenstock (Generating Alpha Podcast, Sep 2025)

*Global chairman and co-founder (1989) of L Catterton, which the host calls the world's largest consumer-focused private-equity firm (over $35bn under management); on finding, scaling and exiting consumer brands.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z1VEwg-r6T4)

**Takeaway:** Consumers are rational, adaptable and increasingly alike across countries, so a brand that works in one region can be lifted into another. The winners move from a transactional, utility relationship to an emotional one, and technology now lets new brands take on incumbents that once controlled marketing.

- Catterton began in 1989 after Tiananmen Square sent Hong Kong's market down "like over 20% in one day" and his Hong Kong employer withdrew "a couple hundred million of capital". He launched anyway, consumer-only, when PE was "pretty generic and mostly leverage buyouts".
- The founding bet joined behavioral economics ("cognitive biases" shape demand) with demographics: baby boomers, "quite different psychologically" from their parents, were creating new companies, brands and channels. He started in "quite a deep recession" for consumers.
- Restaurants: in the 1980s and early 1990s demand exceeded supply, so you "could predict with some level of efficiency how the next unit would look like"; today it is a market-share fight. He backed P.F. Chang's at two units as a new "polished casual" ethnic category.
- Each deal "widened the aperture" into a new category: Odwalla juices (sold to Coca-Cola), Restoration Hardware (mid-2000s), Frédéric Fekkai beauty (bought from Chanel, sold to P&G), ClearChoice (health services), Kettle chips, pet care, and MonoSol, maker of the water-soluble film in Tide pods.
- He met Bernard Arnault around 1989, "before Arno was Arno", and "bear hugged" big multinationals as investors for their insight. LVMH scaled luxury, which is "predicated on scarcity" ("Never goes on sale"), in two ways: brands moved into new products such as sneakers and sunglasses to become a lifestyle, and "they went global".
- By 2015 LVMH saw buyers in Japan, China, Australia and Latin America "just as hungry for these brands as they were in Paris", while Catterton was "very North American centric". It merged in LVMH's private-equity arm (hence the "L"), with LVMH as a minority shareholder alongside the Arnault family office.
- "lift and shift" works because consumers everywhere are "quite similar and thereby, you know, more predictable than people would have thought". His last four Japan deals (a high-end steak restaurant, specialty furniture, high-end apparel, sport fishing) "could have been anywhere in the world", so analogs elsewhere give conviction and a growth plan.
- Rule one: "the consumer is always right". They know what they want, and "the right price value relationship" wins. They also adapt: baby boomers built big-box and specialty retail, then moved online and to mobile; AI will make the brand relationship two-way.
- Winners evolve "beyond a functional utility, beyond a transactional relationship" to "a little more emotional connection"; luxury, where buyers pay "much higher than the cost", is the extreme. The best brands also have attributes that are "relevant and scalable and ownable".
- Birkenstock (2021): "familyowned for eight generations", "very insular, highly protected" and opaque. A CEO the family brought in, whom "no student ever would have hired", had run it for \~9–10 years and cared about "the future of the brand" as well as the sale price. L Catterton's consumer-only record and LVMH link helped win it.
- Why it bought: "an end of one" with no analog, "a health and wellness play", a "huge TAM", "great margins because it was vertically integrated" and buyers with an emotional bond. It added "process and systems and disciplines", since "one of the risks of growth is that it could topple over if it doesn't have a good foundation".
- The family first wanted "all their money off the table", then rolled over a stake now worth "a lot more than they ever thought".
- Time-bound capital, stated upfront: "we are not forever investors". L Catterton opens up global capabilities faster than an owner could alone, then "at some point we're going to have to get out", because "scaling a business no matter how good or successful it is is not a given".
- Technology is "the great equalizer". Big incumbents once had a "massive" advantage because "It controlled the marketing"; now marketing runs on TikTok and Instagram, and technology will read your mood and know when you got paid. Expect many more founder-led brands, and incumbents must adapt.
