### 180. Thomas Kaplan — Silver at $3.50, selling Leor at the top, and gold's "foothills" (Generating Alpha Podcast, Sep 2025)

*Chairman and CIO of The Electrum Group, which holds one of the largest private portfolios of gold and silver assets; he walks through his silver, platinum, gas and gold bets and how he builds conviction.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iSisbyNI88U)

**Takeaway:** Kaplan buys a resource when its price sits below what producers need to make money, scrubs the idea until he has "metaphysical certitude", then goes all in and sells some on the way up. Being right on gold is worthless if the mine is seized, so he now owns assets only in the US, Canada and Mexico.

- Style: from John Train's *The Money Masters* he picked Templeton's "buy during periods of maximum pessimism", the approach that fit his "feel for cyclical turns" and taste for deep value. "know thyself": choose a style that suits your temperament.
- Silver had fallen from $50 (the Hunt-era peak) to $3.50, and consensus saw $2 as digital photography replaced silver halide film, \~a third of demand. Kaplan's math: \~90% of film silver was recycled, the second-largest supply source after mines, so the real loss was "10% of 33%" against a 20–25% supply deficit.
- Floor logic: "there's just literally no way that a silver mining company can make money at three and a half dollars. There's got to be a reversion to the mean." He put his savings into the longest-dated Comex silver options (over a year). Within a month silver bottomed at $3.40 and rose to $5.50.
- He sold at $5.50, his estimate of "the break-even price for the silver mining companies in Idaho to be able to reopen mines". He bought back at $4 after Jack Nash's "Tom, don't be too cute. $4 is good" and sold again at $5.50. Silver then sat between $4 and $6 for 10 years.
- He swapped options for silver properties ("over leverage to the theme") and built a company from Mexico to Mongolia. Its Bolivian find, San Cristobal, was "the biggest discovery of our generation" at odds of 1,000–10,000 to 1. On luck: if you think it is genius, "you're dead, they just haven't buried you yet."
- Platinum gave a "100x return" on companies in South Africa and Zimbabwe. In the early 2000s oil was \~$18 a barrel (it had been below $10) and consensus saw $12–15 as normal; his analysis said $100.
- His gas explorer Leor made one of the biggest Texas gas discoveries in over 30 years and became the fastest-growing privately held gas producer. He sold it to Encana for $2.55bn in Nov 2007, "I think, the highest price paid for reserves or production". A year later it might have been worth "10, 20 cents on that dollar".
- Why sell in 2007: he saw the financial world as "a Ponzi scheme", and shale meant he "could no longer quantify whether oil was worth 100 or 120 or 140" (or 20). He also sold his platinum company to get away from "anything that was economically sensitive", rang "the cash register" and hoped the buyer would double or triple its money.
- In the crisis he bought large, sometimes controlling, stakes in precious-metals companies. NovaGold (Donlin Gold in Alaska, with partner Barrick) had gone from 50 cents to over $20, and Barrick had bid $16. At the end of 2008 Kaplan tried to buy it all at $2; warned that a full bid would be walked up "dollar for dollar", he went for "30 40%" and took effective control as a white knight. In 2025 John Paulson ("a billion dollars") freed it from Barrick, "a very misaligned" partner.
- Process: an insight arrives like "a lightning bolt"; he sleeps on it, scrubs the thesis "over and over again" and hunts for someone to contradict him. With "metaphysical certitude" he goes all in and waits years, without being "flushed out" in washouts or "seduced" by the bubbles that follow. He is not a contrarian: he looks for "a reversal" that can make 10x or 100x, best of all "a great asset" with macro tailwinds.
- Jurisdiction: he was once "the largest holder of mineral rights in the Islamic world" (West Africa to Pakistan), then sold or gave it all away for the US, Canada and Mexico. Jurisdiction became "my number one fear": maximum leverage only counts "in a jurisdiction that will allow you to keep the fruits of that leverage". If he is right on gold and silver, he expects them to be nationalized in most jurisdictions as "strategic monetary assets".
- Gold: at a Dubai PE conference in 2007, with gold at \~$550–600, he set a "first equilibrium level" of $3,000–5,000 an ounce. By Sep 2025 "we're now well into that zone", "in the foothills of a massive, massive bull market", like the Dow in the late '80s. The aim is to hold "a currency that can't be debased at will"; his risk: "I can't see how I'm wrong. And that scares me."
- Exits: "sell on the way up" and "ring the cash register every now and then", since you never know when a thesis peaks. Selling Leor at the very top still "puts a knot in my stomach": "If you sold at the top, it means you were effectively too cute." Selling some lets you hold the rest when "the pain is too much".
- Screen implication: for a commodity producer, compare the price with producers' break-even and with the price at which idle mines restart. Kaplan bought silver below the first and sold at the second.
