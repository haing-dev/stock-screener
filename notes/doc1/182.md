### 182. Mickey Drexler — Hiring on instinct, flattered bosses and why big companies kill creative ones (Generating Alpha Podcast, Oct 2025)

*Former CEO of Gap and J.Crew, known as "the merchant prince"; part 1 of a two-part interview, on his Bronx childhood, his first retail jobs and how he judges people.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cBydy3JvPFo)

**Takeaway:** Drexler judges people in about five minutes, on energy, drive and adversity rather than schools or grades. His early jobs taught him that big corporations breed flattery and bureaucracy: subordinates know a boss best, data only shows the past, and a corporate buyer takes "the soul out" of a creative company.

- Hiring: he ignores grades, board scores and school ("I don't care where people go to school") and admits "a built-in bias against fancy kids". He favors hard-working immigrants and people who have faced adversity.
- Speed: he interviews everyone, as he did at the big companies, and decides in "five minutes", on "energy", the way a casual meeting tells you "if they're fast or not fast". "of course, you make a lot of mistakes".
- He thinks key traits are inborn ("You can't learn style") and does "DNA interviewing": "I want to know where the parents are, what they did." On training: "you don't learn anything on training programs. You learn something from great bosses."
- Drive: his came from a hard Bronx childhood. Taking the payroll to the bank for his father's employer, he found his "big shot" father was among the lowest paid: "I think I was trying to not be my father."
- First job: Abraham & Straus offered him $11,000 and a classmate $11,500, so he chose Bloomingdale's: "if that's the way they are, I'm glad I went to work for Bloomingdales". He was a buyer at 23–24 with his own open-to-buy and ran his two departments as if he owned them.
- At 28 he read the proxy and financials of parent Federated, saw "I don't really want to do what the CEO does" and that he would "have to wait 25 years", and quit.
- Bosses: "most people in big corporations suck up to their bosses", so "the best review of a boss" comes from "their subordinates". Macy's recruited him brilliantly, but he quit after a year and a half over the culture. "if you can hire your boss do so."
- Ann Taylor: he turned the CEO job down twice until a friend said "I rather be the president of a $25 million company" than a vice president of a $500 million one. It had 23 stores and was "losing a lot of money", and his boss warned that without a turnaround the board would block further acquisitions. With no training or mentor, "I followed my instincts", and "it was hugely successful".
- Acquirers: Allied Stores' purchase of Ann Taylor was "the saddest day in my life". "when a big corporation buys a creative company, they take the soul out of it in a minute always", like Marriott buying "a fancy hotel". Allied's "total bureaucrats" wanted "a unit of allied stores" on every shopping bag, "bragging about that a downscale business owns an upscale business".
- Details: "I'm a very minutia minor operator because you have to be or else you don't know what the big deals are." Called a micromanager: "Proudly so." He clipped a Range Rover ad that named Jaguar in small print for his team: "why the hell would you brag about Jaguar?"
- Data: "Data is rearview mirror"; he prefers "to look through the windshield" and go "where the puck is going" (Wayne Gretzky). He thinks technology has made the world less creative.
- CEO-speak: "no one speaks the way CEOs speak on the TV or in the earnings reports". PR people write it, while the world is "desperate to be authentic".
- He named Old Navy after a dive bar's neon marquee he saw on the way to the airport; his chairman did not like the name.
