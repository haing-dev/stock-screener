### 183. Mickey Drexler — Rebuilding Gap: clear the inventory, keep it simple, price honestly (Generating Alpha Podcast, Oct 2025)

*Former CEO of Gap and J.Crew and a 16-year Apple director; part 2 of the interview, on rebuilding Gap and what a leader's job is.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=36pODDFK82c)

**Takeaway:** Drexler grew Gap from $400M to $14bn of sales by liquidating bad inventory first, taking the earnings hit, then rebuilding stores, product and team around a famous name. He judges retailers by honest pricing and product-minded leaders, and distrusts companies that play price games.

- Starting point (\~1983): Gap "was not considered a discounter but practically speaking it was a discounter". \~1/3–40% of it sold only Levi's, "all discounted", and "there's no one to blame except always leadership". "founders invent to a degree and when they don't know how to go to the next step businesses suffer", and founder Don Fisher knew he couldn't take it there.
- Inventory first: "the inventory is a liability. It's all that cash tied up in garbage." Joining in November, by January (the fiscal year-end) he was "liquidating like crazy bad goods" with huge markdowns, and the stock halved. When Fisher warned "The earnings are going to be destroyed", he answered "Yes, they have to be." and "If we don't take the markdowns, we don't have the cash to buy better goods."
- The low point: an offsite in Carmel drew up "a bankrupt contingency". He owed the company $500,000 on a loan and his options were worthless: "I'm not fearless. I was fearful."
- The fix took \~18 months: 400 stores redone in six to eight months (Fisher "was a builder"), "all the crap" cleared, and a new creative team whose two key players came from Ann Taylor. Then the business and stock went "like a rocket". Gap was worth "about 200 300 million" when he joined and "6 7 billion dollar value, I think 10 billion or whatever" when he left.
- Pick famous names: "it's much easier like with J. Crew to take on a business that has critical mass and is a well-known name even if it's not a good name". He calls it "building a new brand", not a turnaround, and rates Old Navy and Madewell, which he started, above the reinventions, which were "much easier".
- 1987: the stock went from \~$20 to "70 plus dollars" and back to $18 a share. Asked about his net worth falling from $75M to $18M, he said "It's long-term." Public-market "friends are the short-term friends mostly"; he learned the same with private equity partners.
- The new Gap formula: color, nice goods, "fair pricing", integrity and creativity. "good taste and style doesn't cost anything extra", yet it "enhances the value of every product".
- Go to the stores: his "big shot bosses" at the department stores never visited the selling floor. At Houston's Galleria he found windshield flyers for "Today only, Gap 30% off": a store manager was "deciding the worldwide strategy of Gap that day".
- "keep it simple": he couldn't follow headquarters jargon, so he put that plaque on every desk (\~2,000 people). "You must distill everything into its simplest definition so everyone understands."
- Focus: Gap owned Banana Republic and then bought Pottery Barn: "You can't take care of the two businesses you own." He never felt he ran Banana "very well".
- Fired: after 18 years the board fired him in a day and "ushered me out within 2 minutes", although "I knew the turn was in". He says the way it was done "was terrible for the company" and "it's never been the same".
- Leadership: "look at the top man or woman who set the tone"; "leadership destroys companies". Everyone calls him Mickey because a title "creates a barrier", slow replies are out ("It's rude not to get back quickly"), and the sales and factory floors "will tell you everything that's wrong". "Big is not best. Big and acting small is best."
- Pricing: "to me the best retailer in the world is TJ Maxx" for "honest, fair prices", while "The whole world now is playing price games". Told a brand is "doing great", he says "go online": "Their pricing is online discounting. In the shop, you pay full price." When a five-and-dime sold a Koosh ball he had bought for \~$3.50 at \~$2.50: "I'll never shop in that store again. I don't trust them."
- Product: "People who are in charge of product companies need to be product people", but "most CEOs are not inventors or creative" in his industry. Merchandise is an investment: "invest in the styles with your cash that will give you a good return".
