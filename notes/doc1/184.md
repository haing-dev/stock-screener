### 184. Jeff Yass — Prediction markets as the best source of probabilities (Generating Alpha Podcast, Oct 2025)

*Founder and managing director of Susquehanna International Group (SIG), a trading firm built on poker, probability and decision theory; a short interview on prediction markets, one of his first.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qU9N75Fe1yU)

**Takeaway:** Good decisions need probabilities, and prediction markets give the most accurate ones because whoever gets the analysis wrong loses money. He expects them to cut betting costs, expose politicians' rosy forecasts, offer cheap hedges against events like hurricanes and, with regulatory clarity, draw institutional money (Oct 2025).

- "You basically can't make a good decision without knowing the probabilities of events happening." Prediction markets are "an objective trusted source" because bettors who get it wrong lose money, and a few informed experts set the price for everyone.
- Betting costs: the sports-betting vig is \~5% today. Customers trading with each other on an exchange, as on Europe's Betfair, should cut it to 1–2% (Oct 2025).
- Iraq: President Bush put the cost at $20 billion and adviser Lawrence Lindsay, who said up to $50 billion, "was sort of punished"; the true cost was $2–6 trillion. A market might have said $500 billion and stirred pushback, since "politicians always tell us that the wars are going to be cheap and quick". In 1862 the Union's War Department stopped taking recruits, expecting the war to end in weeks; Lincoln was "off by 650,000 deaths".
- Manipulation is expensive and self-correcting: "we will bet you hundreds of millions of dollars, that you're wrong", far more than a misleading ad campaign costing millions. On the Polymarket story of a French trader backing Trump: "we bet against him". Fear that bets swing outcomes is "vastly overstated".
- Decision use (Oct 2025): with New York City's mayoral election 15 days away, cable news called it close while prediction markets had Mamdani "a little over 90% to win". A firm weighing a move to New York needs that number, and a real-estate developer can hedge it.
- SIG trades on these odds: stocks move with who is likely to win an election, and SIG uses the odds to judge whether "a stock has overreacted or underreacted".
- Kalshi (SIG is one of its primary market makers): still "a bespoke product" with mostly small bets, and no big institution yet hedges questions like whether the Fed will raise rates (Oct 2025). With regulatory clarity he expects Wall Street-sized bets; Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley are still cautious. Liquidity will come from both institutions and retail.
- Insurance by event contract: where rate caps drove insurers out (Florida), a market could ask whether winds will top 80 mph in your area in the next 2 days. At a 10% chance, $10,000 wins $90,000, bought only when a storm is coming and without claims adjusting, overhead or advertising. Less exact than a claim, but cheaper and "so much more quantifiable"; local weather experts could list such markets and earn from their expertise.
- The main obstacle is fear that overstates the downside: "something could go wrong but something is already going wrong". Acceptance "may take years".
- Policy use: \~40,000 Americans will die on the roads in the next 12 months; he guesses driverless cars would cut that to \~10,000 (down 75%, 30,000 lives saved). A market on 2030 road deaths "would make policymakers hustle".
- His mother's test, "if you're so smart, how come you're not rich?": if you think the odds are wrong, bet and fix the price; "if you can't make money, you may want to consider being quiet like maybe the market knows more than you do". When a famous political scientist had Hillary Clinton up 30–40 points over Obama in the primary, TradeSports gave Obama "a 22% chance of winning".
- Learn probability, statistics and Bayesian analysis; calculus got the emphasis after Sputnik but is "of limited value to most people". Without statistics you can't tell signal from noise (is a busy hurricane season an outlier?), and Harvard medical-school researchers given basic questions were "off by a factor of a hundred".
- "the bigger the decision, the less time we think about it": people spend a lot of time on a stock trade, where "it's basically irrelevant what you're doing because the markets are fair", and little on whom to marry. His advice: poll your friends anonymously, like a market.
