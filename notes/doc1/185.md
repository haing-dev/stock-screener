### 185. Noah Yechiely — Efficient markets, real hedge funds and hiring for creativity (Generating Alpha Podcast, Dec 2024)

*Former head of equities at Bridgewater Associates, where he spent 20 years from 2004 (when it managed a bit under $10 billion), moving from currency and bond research to equities and the management committee; a careers interview with two teenage hosts.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JD-CkCfpHqw)

**Takeaway:** Markets are very efficient, so genuinely uncorrelated returns are scarce and many hedge funds just sell market returns at higher fees. What lasts is understanding why something works, assuming you are wrong, and hiring for curiosity and creativity rather than experience.

- A real hedge fund's "primary objective" is returns uncorrelated with the market, which help most when everything else is falling. Many funds called hedge funds don't hedge: they drifted into holding assets long because that earns returns and fees, "selling a beta" at a higher price.
- Uncorrelated returns are hard: "you have to be the smartest person on the poker table and do it thousands and thousands of times again and again against the smartest people in the market".
- Scale: markets worth "hundreds and hundreds of trillions" move 1–2% a day, while the whole hedge-fund industry makes "a few billions", "a few basis points" of the total. Good funds pair risk management with "particular niches in the market that are inefficient", where the money to be made "is not large".
- Efficiency migrates: once a strategy works and becomes public, many investors chase it and returns fall, while other markets become less efficient. The big change since 2004 is technology: data once available to "a particular handful of people" close to a company or industry, at high cost, is now public or scrapeable, so data-driven corners of the market became much more efficient.
- Bottom-up vs top-down, with Boeing vs Airbus: product and CEO decide which one wins share, which is what most equity analysts try to call. Own both and the share shifts cancel; the pair moves with total spending on airplanes, which follows travel. Add up sectors and those shifts cancel too, leaving the economy-wide forces (spending, growth, inflation) that move whole asset classes.
- Asked what made Ray Dalio great, he said the best investors try to "figure out why things are happening rather than trying to see what worked in the past", going deep rather than staying at the surface of the market.
- Learn from failures and question yourself: "always assume that you're wrong rather than that you're right". When a strategy shows a high Sharpe ratio, ask why it works and whether it will keep working the same way.
- Hiring: Bridgewater looked for curiosity and analytical creativity, not years of experience, whether from engineering, philosophy or literature. Tools like Excel and data analysis can be taught quickly, but "you can't teach them how to be creative".
- Consulting and engineering carry over: break a new problem into pieces, build a framework, find the data. Math and science matter less for the content than for learning to think "in a mathematical way".
- Read widely: financial gurus can inspire, but no one will teach you the secret of making money in markets; science, sci-fi and fiction add as much (and avoid TikTok). Even "a micro investor needs to understand how the world works": politics, relations with China and where tariffs go (Dec 2024).
- Career advice: follow your interest, not the best-paying industry; interest and skill usually go together because getting good takes hard work through ups and downs. Learn every day, get back up after failures and keep people you trust around you.
