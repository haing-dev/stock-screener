### 186. Thomas Peterffy — Technology as the edge, from Timber Hill to Interactive Brokers (Generating Alpha Podcast, Oct 2025)

*Founder and chairman of Interactive Brokers (IBKR), who arrived in New York from Hungary in 1965 and first built the options market maker Timber Hill; a career interview at 81.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aDKzqdEXib8)

**Takeaway:** His edge has always been a new technology that others in the business weren't using: a computer-derived options formula gave Timber Hill tighter quotes than the floor, and automation lets Interactive Brokers earn 70–80% margins (Oct 2025). Keep a long-term plan, measure value precisely, and expect the event you never planned for.

- "most successful businesses take advantage of relatively recently emerging technologies", as computing was in the 1960s and 70s, when few businesses used it. His advice today: find "a technological development that nobody is using in that business" and be first.
- Timber Hill's origin: over-the-counter options were European-style, so winning holders couldn't exit before expiry. Knowing an in-the-money option is worth more than its in-the-money amount, he ran computer simulations, derived a valuation formula and joined the American Stock Exchange, which by then listed options, to trade on fair values.
- The edge in practice: with an option worth $3.50 trading anywhere between $3 and $4, he bid 3⅜ and offered 3⅝ (prices were in eighths), a tighter market than anyone's: "I could bid higher than others because they were more uncertain than I was". That won more business and more profit. "if you cannot measure them you cannot value them".
- Floor technology: handheld computers with his formula burned into their chips gave his traders any option's value. When NASDAQ insisted that people, not computers, enter orders, he built a device to type them (the host's account). His rule: with "a better mousetrap" and a barrier that "doesn't make any sense", "it's your responsibility to try to overcome those barriers".
- Interactive Brokers (started 1993, per the host): in the open-outcry era two of his staff stood in the Chicago Mercantile Exchange's S&P 500 futures pit with radio-linked handhelds, one holding all buy orders and one all sell orders. Customers anywhere saw the pit on a downloaded trading screen over the internet and could trade with it, hence the name.
- In investing, "an excellent product is something that helps people" make money by investing. As he has said (per the host), a big business is a really good product delivered at a better deal.
- Economics (Oct 2025): profit margins of "70 to 80" percent, and he still owns a majority, never needing outside investors, "By automation": "once something is automated it basically cost nothing" beyond electricity and hardware. IBKR has over 3,000 employees; in 10 years, "Hopefully less than 3,000."
- Near-bankruptcy (1989): his fully automated market-making system fed bids and offers to floor staff. On a day he was away, a United Airlines shock crashed the market at a speed "that never happened before", the programs couldn't keep up, and staff kept buying higher than they sold. The firm lost "over half of our capital" while office staff worked on "esoteric stuff" instead of "pulling out the wires and stopping the whole music".
- After that loss he had \~$10 million left (like $50 million today), enough to live on without a job or degree, and decided to go back and risk it. What keeps him going is the challenge of what he might achieve, "like climbing a mountain".
- The biggest inefficiency in finance is "the laziness of people", who don't look for what is most missing; the advantage goes to whoever makes something better, easier, cheaper or more profitable for others. On human nature: "people are incredibly gullible and lazy" and don't learn from history.
- "automation is always good" ("that's what AI is basically about"). IBKR's four long-term trends, as he has framed them (globalization, technology adoption, a spreading equity culture and better allocation of resources), "still exist today".
- Forecast trading (Oct 2025): IBKR's yes/no contracts pay $1 if right, on questions like next month's CPI or who will be New York's mayor. That "the socialist" is very likely to win "staggers my mind": "people never learn from history".
- A long-term plan is "not that it's important. It's essential." His advice: "always have a plan and work on your plan. Look at it every day. Change it when you need to, but make a step no matter how small". At 81 he still works 8–9 hours a day, seven days a week.
