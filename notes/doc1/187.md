### 187. Antonio Gracias — Growth plus ROIC, pro-entropic founders and the six-month rule at Valor (Generating Alpha Podcast, Nov 2025)

*Founder and CEO of Valor Equity Partners, an early investor and board member at Tesla, SpaceX, SolarCity and The Boring Company; a career interview.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QAv5TUzeT1k)

**Takeaway:** Valor backs companies that grow very fast and earn a high return on capital, run by founders who profit from chaos rather than fight it. Gracias sizes up in stages, takes about six months to judge whether a founder tells the truth, and adds capital when something goes wrong at a company he knows well.

- First deal (1995): in his first year of law school he bought a connector-plating subsidiary of bankrupt KDI, a cheap way into the internet build-out because "connectors are the internet backbone". It grew from $10M to $125M of revenue in about five or six years before it was sold.
- Operating roots: as a plant manager he raised factory throughput with the theory of constraints (from the book *The Goal*); combined with Six Sigma, it became the lean toolkit Valor still uses. The early model bought older industrial assets in the connector supply chain (stamping, plating, molding, assembly) on 90–95% leverage; at that leverage, he learned, "your return on capital matters".
- Evolution: early funds held industrial assets, a tree farm, a ski resort and Tesla. Valor then dropped control turnarounds for technology, keeping its operators in a "scale group". Founders know "how to make the the drivetrain for a car" but not how to build the whole company at scale, and that is where operators help. Portfolio companies are treated as customers; serve them well and deal flow and returns follow.
- Two metrics: companies "growing very fast and that also have high return on capital. Those are the two basic metrics". Growth investors fixate on revenue growth, "but man if you don't someday make money it won't work": a company is "the machine to make the machine and that machine must be a very efficient producer of capital".
- Physics as framework. First law: "money is energy", and ROIC measures how efficiently it is transformed; "The most efficient people at making that transformation are the ones that win over time." Second law: entropy rises, and "If you try to fight it, you're going to lose." Third law: it can never be reduced to zero; "There may be moments of stability, but they're like fooling you."
- Pro-entropic companies: most try to damp change or merely react; the rare ones are "really great at surfing it". Example: Tesla bought the Fremont factory out of the GM bankruptcy "for pennies on the dollar". It "took billions of dollars to create", and Tesla is what it is because it got the plant "for such a low price at that time in history".
- He expects more chaos (Nov 2025) "because of deglobalization and because of technological change", moving "like a sine wave". Practice "mapping the whole probabilistic future so you aren't surprised": surprise pulls the brain's 20 watts away from thinking into safety systems.
- Staged sizing: "a small check, a bigger check, a bigger check" over time, to learn whether values align and whether the founder executes. Watching people through good times and bad works because, "believe it or not", past performance does predict future performance.
- The six-month guideline (used in about 90% of deals) arose because Valor's macro and micro analysis was right, but it kept "getting fooled by people who were bad". He found "it takes about six months to figure out someone's bad", across multiple interactions and multiple people.
- Base rates: about 5% of people have the brain anomaly behind psychopathy; a psychologist Valor hired told it to assume \~10% in "power" professions such as entrepreneurship, law, medicine and politics. Dishonest risk-takers "at the edge" can look like great entrepreneurs, and honest optimists also overestimate, so triangulate statements against reality over time. His old base case that everyone was good was wrong: "everyone's not good".
- Bad news at a company you know well is the time to add: "something bad happens, that's the time to double down", because you know how the people will respond. He still holds his Tesla shares after well over a decade.
- Fix problems at the source (gemba, from lean): "You can't do this from an office" in New York, Chicago or Palo Alto. Ask the people doing the work; "they already have the answers... No one's ever actually asked them."
- Decision hygiene: let default biases handle small calls; build big ones from first principles, root-cause mistakes, and sleep on them. In high-stakes calls, pause and root-cause your intuition: "Almost always you have the time" unless you're a trader.
- From Musk: "if it's possible in the physics it's possible period", so back long shots with high expected value (landing rockets). Mission-driven companies "attract better people", and a company is "a sum total of the vectors of the people" in it. Stay flexible: "a firm fixed position is weak", and "if we don't keep evolving as an organization, we will die".
