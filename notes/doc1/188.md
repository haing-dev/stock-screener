### 188. Collin Roche — GTCR's change agenda: buy good businesses at value prices, then make them better (Generating Alpha Podcast, Dec 2025)

*Co-CEO and managing partner of GTCR, a 45-year-old private-equity firm he joined from Goldman Sachs in 1996; on how GTCR finds, improves and exits businesses.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9R5H91LRVGo)

**Takeaway:** The best deals buy a good business at a value price, then work with management on a change agenda until it is a more predictable grower that earns a higher multiple. Lean against the cycle: when multiples and industry results sit well above long-term averages, expect mean reversion.

- Start with basic questions: "why does this industry exist?" What forces shaped it, "Do those forces still exist", and is inertia hiding a chance to do things differently? Then picture the business three or five years out across several scenarios, not one forecast.
- Value roots, growth payoff: grounded in Security Analysis and McKinsey's Valuation, he finds the most successful deals were "priced on a value basis" but had growth potential. "you bought something for a fair price", made it a more predictable growth business, and it "would therefore earn a much higher multiple". That takes being "a bit contrarian".
- VeriFone (point-of-sale terminals) was a bet on the "electronification of payments". The former Hewlett-Packard unit "was just a mess", with a bad financial back office and bad data, "but I I believed in the manager". Industry work built conviction that demand was there although "the numbers were just kind of wild".
- In many investments "there is a little bit of a jump of faith somewhere", but it should be "an educated bet": sort "what's knowable, what's not knowable". GTCR bought when values were depressed, with a base return from "a highly recurring business". Luck (broadband, better video terminals, faster, cheaper chips) then set off a "virtuous cycle" of better products, more demand and higher prices.
- Why private equity over stock picking: "you can impact outcomes" by working with management, while venture is "much more binary". He is "much less good at predicting where the market's going to go".
- Target: "this is a very good business, but it can be even better", e.g. it grows slower than its industry for lack of product, geographic coverage or leadership, underprices, or gives poor service. GTCR never buys to do "exactly the same thing and keep going"; every deal has a "change agenda".
- Levers differ by deal: invest in the sales function, open a different distribution channel, push operational excellence and technology spend, or add scale and market coverage, where M&A "may be a more effective way" to add products or geographies.
- Sourcing: banker-run processes take "three to five months", "but that's not how deals get done" at GTCR. It stays deep in its industries and knows managers for years, then acts on a corporate carve-out, a founder- or family-owned business ready for its next step, or a good business that needs a new leader.
- Mega funds are "trying to do a lot of different things"; GTCR is "trying to do substantially one thing well", in four sectors: healthcare; technology, media and telecom; business and consumer services; financial services and technology. Deal sizes run up to "five or 10 or 20 billion of EV". Themes are top-down, diligence bottom-up: "we're not thematic investors in the sense we're just playing the theme".
- GTCR tries to "think like perpetual owners", not renters. Then the exits "take care of themselves": "If you build a better business", you're "likely to get a better multiple for it than you paid".
- Focus on inputs, not outputs. All good or great investments "always have challenge"; if competitors face the same problem and you handle it better, "you're going to win in the marketplace". Good leaders use data, know their value proposition and talk to customers rather than stay insular.
- Cycles: the industry "has gotten carried away". From 2011 to 2021 rising multiples did much of the work, which "works for a while". When multiples or industry performance are "well above long-term averages", or rates "way lower than long-term averages", expect "mean reversion".
- He leaned against 2021's "record high multiples record low interest rates" and post-COVID growth. In 2023–24, as the industry rationalized and multiples fell, "that's where the opportunity is": "when it doesn't feel as good that's probably the better opportunity set".
- AI (Dec 2025) is "a massive overlay" that GTCR is deploying across its companies. But some categories face "a lot of value destruction", a "race to the bottom on pricing", or whole activities that become "essentially digitized".
