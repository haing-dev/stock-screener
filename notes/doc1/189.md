### 189. Paul Wachter — Client-first dealmaking for LeBron, Arnold and Beats (Generating Alpha Podcast, Dec 2025)

*Founder and CEO of Main Street Advisors, which advises LeBron James, Arnold Schwarzenegger, Jimmy Iovine, Drake and Billie Eilish among others; lead negotiator on LeBron's Nike deal and the sale of Beats to Apple; a career interview.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eIaeaqdnqo0)

**Takeaway:** Wachter's edge is client service and deals that work for both sides: start from what the other side cares about, because "there's always the next day". The celebrity businesses that last are organic fits (Planet Hollywood, Beats), and the partners worth backing are solid builders, not "fast buck artists".

- Career lesson: early on he took on work he disliked to prove he could, such as tax law. A boss at Kidder Peabody taught him to "play to what you're good at and what you like and what you know", so by Wertheim Schroder he focused on sports, media and hospitality. Also: "if you team up with other good people in your field, you're more powerful than always trying to do everything alone".
- Main Street began with Arnold Schwarzenegger, a friend since 1981. Then "the biggest movie star in the world", Arnold knew real estate but said "I don't really know anything about the stock market". Tom Werner (then probably TV's biggest comedy producer, with Marcy Carsey) and Jimmy Iovine followed.
- Two formative deals predate the firm. Planet Hollywood was "the first sort of real celebrity deal that was organic": stars co-founding a company rather than taking endorsement fees. The idea worked, though the business later struggled, "mostly probably having to do with the food". An early investment in Dimensional Fund Advisors taught him to back people for their "morals and ethics and intelligence": "I'm not looking for fast buck artists and slick people."
- First-of-a-kind structures: the best-known early deal bought two 747s and leased them back to Singapore Airlines. "They weren't tax deals" but economic ones, and "no individuals had ever done that before". It leaked "maybe 20 seconds after we signed" and became famous when Arnold became governor.
- Beats (from \~2006): in the Napster era Jimmy Iovine saw that "nobody wants to pay for music anymore" but people still want music, so use music to sell something that makes money. Dr. Dre and headphones was a natural fit: "It wasn't Dr. Dre and cornflakes". Authenticity is his constant theme, and he thinks it is now "being challenged" by thousands of influencers chasing brand deals.
- Negotiating: start with what the other side cares about. Ask "what are your red lines in the sand?", try to live with them, then share yours; if that works, "you're 90% of the way there". He aims for a really good result, "not necessarily maximize every dollar".
- Win-lose doesn't last: approach a negotiation as "how can I win and how can you lose" and "you'll have a short career". Most people "think that they're a good negotiator when they're crushing the other side". Goodwill pays when a deal must be renegotiated: "there's always the next day". LeBron got "an incredible deal" from Nike, and Wachter later did other deals with Nike.
- "we are a client service firm", "first, second, and third". Main Street's private equity, venture and incubation businesses all grew out of client work; M&A assignments for non-clients "never really works out". Helping portfolio companies through clients and relationships "is why we get good deal flow". He guesses, stressing it's a guess, that it has invested in 70–120 businesses.
- Picking young clients: he met LeBron at 20 and was impressed by him and business partner Maverick Carter, "curious and naturally intelligent" without Wharton degrees. Talent can be obvious, as with Billie Eilish at 16 or 17, "screaming off the page", and he checks with his kids. He also helps established stars: after a "very, very bad experience" owning the San Diego Padres, Tom Werner bought the Red Sox, and later Liverpool, through a Wachter connection.
- What his icons share: "a very strong sense of what's good for them". He told Arnold not to run for governor; Arnold "loved being governor. And I was wrong." Most people are "floundering around trying to figure it out".
- Creator economy (Dec 2025): "if everybody's influencing everybody, where's the money coming from?" Feeds are more diluted and commercial, and few creators break out. He worries Gen Z is pushed away from training: "there's nothing wrong with being trained", and "If you put up an Instagram account and try and be an influencer, no one's teaching you."
- Advice: his success rested on long preparation (Wharton, Columbia Law, a Ninth Circuit clerkship, 15 years on Wall Street), so early clients felt "this guy actually knows something". Failing is fine: six unhappy years at Kidder Peabody were "kind of a failure".
