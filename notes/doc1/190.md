### 190. Keith Rabois — Seed investing on a "non-zero chance" of changing the world (Generating Alpha Podcast, Jan 2026)

*Managing director at Khosla Ventures, formerly at Founders Fund, an early PayPal and LinkedIn executive and COO of Square, interviewed by a high-school host about how he picks and advises seed-stage founders.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4W_b1QyZJSs)

**Takeaway:** A seed investor's job is to spot the rare founder with a real chance of changing the world. He decides in the first meeting 90%+ of the time, makes \~8–10 investments a year and hunts for anomalies, because bland, homogeneous companies rarely become outliers.

- The test: "do I have a reason to believe this person has a nonzero chance of changing the world". For a normal person that chance "is zero or rounds to zero"; the question is whether this one can "rearrange the world to their will".
- Know your edge: "every sustained consistently successful seed investor has their own comparative advantage". His is judging founders in "3 to 30 minutes"; others are technology-based investors who see a new technology's market implications at once.
- The X factor is "hard to describe, but easy to know": "your ears just perk" at the smartest, most tenacious or best salesperson you've ever met. He compares it to scouting reports that flagged future Hall of Famers at 16.
- Speed and volume: "90 plus percent of the time" he knows in the first meeting, then is "just rationalizing it and making sure like I haven't missed anything". His ears perk "about eight times" a year; in his \~13th year as a VC he has averaged 10 investments a year, "maybe a few at the margin I shouldn't have made".
- What still stops a deal: colleagues who know the person or industry better, something found in follow-ups, or terms "that just don't make economic sense for us".
- Anomalies: in intros and decks he looks for "anomalies, something unexpected", because "the forces of history like are towards inertia". Exceptional companies "almost always have it fairly early"; an anomaly and Peter Thiel's "secret" are "close cousins".
- "Homogenization is not your friend": in an outlier, power-law business "anything that feels bland and homogeneous probably isn't going to be one of those outliers". Negative anomalies are rarely positive signals, "but not never".
- After investing: list the company's key inflection points and "Take the most difficult ones first not last". Solving one or two in year one brings "perceived momentum investor momentum valuation increases" and confidence. "critical density of talent" is the common refrain of successful companies.
- Khosla Ventures vs Founders Fund: similar taste in founders and ambition. KV is "very highly technical", takes technical risk and wants to be the first institutional investor ("bold, early impactful"; when it isn't, "it's a flaw"). "FF is the best momentum growth investor", putting a lot of money into likely winners over multiple rounds.
- Advising: he basically "never" tells a founder what to do. He lays out trade-offs, because "the grass isn't usually greener", and explains where he or others got burned. "being a founder is extremely lonely".
- Network edge: his first five angel investments (LinkedIn, Palantir, Yelp, YouTube, Xoom) all came from PayPal contacts, "cherrypicking" who would be a successful founder, and all thrived. Board seats (Xoom, Yelp) were "a closer proxy for being a VC" than passive checks.
- Bubble tell: in Feb 2000 talent was so scarce that startups "hired like recovering lawyers like me, management consultants". He joined one, and "6 weeks, 7 weeks later" the market collapsed: "The timing was about as bad as you can get."
- Finding your own edge: ask 5–10 people who like you why, write the answers down verbatim and look for "common refrains". His motto, from Jerry Garcia via Pat Riley: "You want to be the only that does what you do."
