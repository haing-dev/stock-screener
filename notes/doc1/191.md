### 191. Pete Muller — PDT Partners: trust, restraint on size, and the 2007 quant crisis (Generating Alpha Podcast, Feb 2026)

*Founder and CEO of PDT Partners, the quant firm he started in 1992 as Morgan Stanley's Process Driven Trading desk and spun out in 2012; also a singer-songwriter with seven albums.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X0RutePdtSE)

**Takeaway:** PDT's edge rests on people and restraint: hire smart, collaborative people, share the models in a culture of trust, and stay small enough to keep Sharpe ratios high, even returning outside money. The August 2007 quant crisis, after PDT had scaled up crowded low-Sharpe books, forced it to cut two-thirds of its book and taught it how to liquidate under stress.

- Track record, per the host: 20% a year on average for nearly two decades as Morgan Stanley's most profitable group, no down year for the flagship fund since 1993, fees of 3 and 50, and a 3.5% turnover rate.
- Roots: at Barra (founded by Barr Rosenberg) in 1985–92, when quant was new and data and technology were hard; pension funds judged their managers with Barra's models, so the managers hired Barra too.
- The pitch (1992): Morgan Stanley wanted him as "the face of quant", writing papers and seeing clients. He asked to trade its money instead, citing poker: "I think I can figure this out". It gave him a couple of years, expecting him to fail; after a few years PDT "figured out how to make money", just as his sponsor fell out politically, and six years in it was "by far the most profitable group".
- In those unstable early years he seriously talked to Long-Term Capital, which lacked the statistical equity trading PDT did well, about moving the group there; it fell through and he "dodged that bullet".
- Burnout (\~1999–2000): having "made more money than I ever dreamed of", he stopped having fun, phased out over \~5 years and handed the bonus pool to the group. He co-founded ChalkStream Capital to back new hedge-fund managers: "it's really hard to do and really competitive".
- On returning he broke a secretive culture ("Let's not tell anybody") and hired many smart people: "the only way to get really good was to keep growing and to keep learning".
- 2007 quant crisis: in low-Sharpe quant "people just got bigger and bigger". His decision to scale PDT's low-Sharpe books "almost took down the firm": he wanted outside capital for them, but Morgan Stanley said "we can handle massive draw downs", so they grew in-house. In late August 2007, as crowded books were cut and weak hands sold into thin liquidity "even though the market was flat that week", PDT cut two-thirds of its book and "almost went down to zero", then made back the year on the third it kept, trusting his "poker sense" that the selling was over.
- Lessons: "we just never thought we would have to emergency liquidate so we didn't know how to do it", so they traded sloppily; now "when crazy events happen in the marketplace now we're much better prepared". In 2008, as Morgan Stanley nearly failed, PDT was "making tons of money".
- Spin-out: the Volcker rule barred bank proprietary trading, so PDT split amicably, first running Morgan Stanley's money from outside for a couple of years. A core investor locked up capital for seven years to replace Morgan's; all 80 people moved to the new firm in 2012, and the Dec 31 book was transferred into the fund without paying trading costs.
- Size kills returns: "the bigger you get the more volatile you get", the Sharpe ratio falls, trading costs rise and "the lower realized alpha is"; "there's a formula that trades it off". PDT could raise "20, 30, 40, 50 billion dollars more in low sharp strategies" but won't: it would need a marketing team (now basically one person) and client meetings that distract from research.
- Own money first: PDT has "kicked everybody out" of its partners fund, now "more than half of what we manage", and will slowly do the same in other funds (AUM in the mid-teens of billions, per the host, Feb 2026). The aim is "consistent returns": even in the lower-incentive-fee fund "a losing quarter should be very unusual".
- Drawdowns: always ask "did something change?" A low-Sharpe strategy can lose for years, and after "losing three years in a row it gets very hard you start doubting it", which is another limit on how big to get.
- Culture as edge: the goal is "to be the best place to work in quantitative finance", with smart people who are collaborative and kind and trusted not to leave with the models. "we pay people for being collaborative, not just for making money"; a talented jerk is "not going to last"; be "ruthless, just not ruthless versus their colleagues". With close to 300 people, PDT maximizes "dollar profit per employee".
- Research bets are judged on conviction and credibility (past success); a smart, trusted researcher who won't stop until they crack a problem is allowed to try, since the downside is limited. His ego "is not attached to being the guy who could figure it all out", and his advice is to "follow the energy".
