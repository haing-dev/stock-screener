### 192. Dmitry Balyasny — Building a multi-manager firm: the PM's box, talent and culture (Generating Alpha Podcast, Feb 2026)

*Founder, managing partner and CIO of Balyasny Asset Management (BAM), the multi-strategy fund he co-founded in 2001 out of his trading group at Schonfeld Securities; per the host it manages over $30 billion with 2,300 staff, and he still runs a book.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O1M8y4QybU8)

**Takeaway:** Decide the firm's architecture first and stick with it: BAM's specialized multi-manager model was hard to raise money for, but diversified, partnership-run firms outlast boutiques built on one star. Each PM trades inside a defined "box", talent is won with resources and coaching rather than guarantees, and culture pays off in bad years (2008, 2018) and good ones.

- Early lesson: as a college stockbroker he funded his own trading with commissions and levered it with credit cards, "my early version of prime brokerage", losing "that and more". He concluded he needed formal mentorship.
- Getting in: after a large stack of rejection letters he answered the one newspaper ad Schonfeld ran. It wanted people with little trading experience and no preconceived notions; trainees got no salary, a "free lunch" and a share of profits on a small account. He made nothing in his first year, and some trainees made nothing for years and dropped out.
- Scaling at Schonfeld: he hired traders to take parts of his risk, then, 5–6 years in, fundamental analysts, the first group at the firm to do so in a 1990s momentum market he thought "wasn't going to last forever". That let them hold positions longer; sector PMs followed, and the group became a division and then BAM.
- The box: most people try something, do more if it works and switch if it doesn't. Instead define where you have an edge, holding period, factor risk, concentration, liquidity and "here's what I'm trying to make, here's what I'm limiting myself to lose". New PMs start in a tight box that widens with success and shrinks after a drawdown ("go back to your basics"); each lesson should improve the next thousand trades, not just this one.
- Fund-of-funds lesson: allocating Schonfeld money to outside funds, he saw two kinds: boutiques built around one risk taker (most hedge funds) and businesses with many risk takers (prop firms like Jane Street or HRT, or multi-manager funds). The single-manager book had "a tremendous amount of turnover"; the diversified firms lasted decades because they can add strategies, shift capital to better opportunities and refresh teams.
- Architecture before asset-raising: don't start from "how much money can I raise?", since investor appetites change and follow returns. The model "was really hard to raise money for", and AUM went up and down for years, because 200 PMs and a couple thousand people are hard for investors to underwrite. "that conviction gets tested", and switching from single-manager to multi-manager later is very hard.
- Partnership plus P&L pay: his two early partners have been with him \~25 years, and there are now 20 partners across strategy heads, department heads and PMs. Individual P&L incentives and firm-wide partnership "really reinforce each other", though "most firms are kind of one or the other".
- Analysts and PMs both need "variant ideas": a steady flow of views that differ from consensus over the next few months to couple of years, always studying "the other side of the coin and what might cause people to come around to your view". They also need process discipline; brilliant idea people often fall in love with one idea and struggle at "running models on 100 different companies".
- PMs differ in risk tolerance and juggling: an analyst can spend 90% of a week proving or disproving one big thesis, while a PM juggles "50 different things" and the whole portfolio. "I think risk is tough to coach": risk personality is innate and can only be nudged, so BAM screens for it and for passion, because everyone has "miserable periods when nothing is working". Analysts learn mainly from their PM ("a bit of a Jedi relationship") plus recorded firm-wide sessions, and a PM program gives strong analysts or traders capital and teaches team and risk management.
- Paying for talent: "99% of funds don't really compete" for PMs running meaningful books, and among the big firms sign-on guarantees are "fairly commoditized". Like athletes taking less to join a winning team, PMs choose on central resources (strategy, policy and economic research, cross-asset collaboration, quant researchers who optimize risk-taking) and coaching, "kind of like venture capital, we're both making a bet". A team that loses money for years still gets changed.
- Culture pays defensively: in 2008 BAM made "a little bit of money" but faced "50% redemptions" as investors needed cash elsewhere, and covered costs from the management company; "90% of the time we lose money on management fees". 2018, its one down year in 25, cost a lot of AUM and a restructuring, but few people left. Without a strong culture, businesses fold in hard times or get "pretty permanently impaired", public companies included; lists of top funds change hugely each decade largely because staff buy into nothing beyond their own job.
- And offensively: sharing an insight "that doesn't cost you anything", a team-management tip or a new AI trick across teams and asset classes gets "the flywheel going". Because BAM is specialized (one sector, region, macro mandate or commodity per team), with team counts sized to each strategy's "alpha pool", talking carries little risk of "unintended correlation". Three PMs debating usually end with three views; if they agree from different research, "that's like a pretty good signal".
- Capital and edge: a sovereign wealth fund invested recently, 17 years after he first met them. "it's not that hard to raise some capital but maintaining it and growing it through the ups and downs like that's what's hard". Edge comes either from managing, recruiting and allocating, or from trading one area with capital matched to capacity; trouble starts when founders veer from that core edge, since a great risk taker is usually not a great talent manager or business builder.
- His job (Feb 2026): trading through earnings and recent "crazy volatility in the commodities market"; strategy reviews for each vertical to separate "trades versus businesses"; trading technology; and recruiting, "a couple interviews every day".
