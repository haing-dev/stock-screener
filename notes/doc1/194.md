### 194. Fabrice Grinda — Marketplace metrics, fast decisions and the Alibaba entry at FJ Labs (Generating Alpha Podcast, Apr 2026)

*Serial entrepreneur (Aucland, OLX) and founding partner of FJ Labs, a high-volume early-stage investor focused on marketplaces, interviewed by a high-school host.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9nC7Ewmhs2I)

**Takeaway:** Marketplaces are unit-economics businesses whose health shows early: 25%+ sell-through, good retention and acquisition costs that fall with scale. FJ Labs decides fast, takes no board seats and spreads its bets widely to catch the power law; he doubts that more diligence improves decisions.

- Seed marketplace checklist: sell-through of "25% or more" for goods, or for services a 25%+ share of the supply side's revenue; strong retention; and network effects, where "customer acquisition costs decrease as you scale". In other businesses acquisition costs keep rising until returns diminish.
- Small is fine: a seed marketplace may do \~$150k a month of GMV (B2B, at lower take rates, $750k–$1 million), but "something that works at a reasonably small scale in a large category will keep working throughout". The best founders can explain how they build supply, build demand and match them.
- Marketplaces "are really unit economic businesses": "if the unit economics work you can scale forever", and if not "you may not have a business".
- Process: FJ Labs doesn't lead rounds or take board seats (board meetings "were boring", "admin overhead") and decides fast, since he doesn't think more calls or more due diligence improve decisions. It began as one call with him; now a colleague takes the first call.
- Portfolio construction: an AngelList \[?\] analysis he cites found that \~500 companies per fund gives enough diversification to "hit the power law"; more brings diminishing returns, fewer is too few. His breadth comes from curiosity, not a top-down rule: he invests when he meets founders he likes. FJ Labs has backed over 1,100 startups (host).
- Alibaba: in 1998 he offered Jack Ma $10,000 for the alibaba.com domain and was refused. After eBay bought EachNet (95% share in China) and "completely screwed it up" with heavy fees, Alibaba's Taobao gained share.
- Alibaba entry (\~2010): early investors held a tender at "$4 a share", "like 15 billion valuation". With the company now monetizing and early in payments, he judged "Now is the time to enter" and "wrote a big check".
- Copy what works elsewhere: "ideas that work in one country work in other countries". Aucland took eBay to Europe and became one of the top three European auction sites. He picked it because the marketplace chicken-and-egg problem fit his market-design training, while banking licences or inventory didn't fit a 23-year-old with no money.
- Bubble lesson: Aucland was sold to a company whose stock fell from a "10 billion market cap to 30 million", a "99% decrease": "I'd lost everything".
- Next came a "reverse arbitrage": ringtones and mobile games were profitable in Europe and Asia but missing in the US. He borrowed $100,000 on credit cards and "missed payroll 27 times"; revenue grew from $1 million in 2002 to $5 million in 2003, and the company was profitable when sold.
- OLX: in 2006 he offered to run Craigslist for free; unmoderated, it was full of scams and "the least female friendly site in the world", though "women are the primary decision makers in all household purchases". Rebuffed, he launched OLX in over 100 countries at once, "like throwing spaghetti in the wall".
- OLX took off in four (Brazil, Portugal, India, Pakistan). It focused there, became profitable, added cars and real estate, then re-expanded to win about 30 countries, with 11,000 employees and 300 million uniques. Lesson: serendipity matters, so "give yourself at bats".
- Risk: "you can't shave an egg" (a French saying): with nothing to lose, failure is cheap, yet most people "are more riskaverse than they should be". One VC yes can take "99 nos", or "299".
- Working-capital lesson from college: US retail prices were below European wholesale, so he shipped hard drives, processors and memory to European retailers who paid upfront, while paying wholesalers on net-30. It was cash-flow positive from day one, \~$50k a year of net profit.
