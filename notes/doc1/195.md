### 195. Michael Paulus — Bootstrapping Assurance IQ, tax-aware endowment portfolios and apartments as a rates option (Generating Alpha Podcast, May 2026)

*Founder and CEO of PCM Encore, an employee-owned multi-family office; earlier president of Addepar, an Andreessen Horowitz partner and co-founder of Assurance IQ, bootstrapped and sold to Prudential for $2.35bn in 2019.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ideMX8AzDM4)

**Takeaway:** Paulus bootstrapped Assurance IQ because life insurance pays commissions upfront: good unit economics should fund growth, and bets that economics improve with scale usually fail. At PCM Encore he adapts endowment-style portfolios for tax and uses West Coast apartments as a bond replacement that doubles as a cheap option on falling rates (May 2026).

- Addepar (after 2008): wealth managers had moved into hedge funds, private equity, real estate and venture, which looked diversifying on paper but were not tracked after purchase. From '06 to '08 "these were all going up together", "And then it turned out that they all crashed together". Clients wanted "a real understanding of the underlying exposures of the funds".
- VC vs founder: a VC sees "dozens and dozens of really smart teams attacking the same space" and thinks statistically; a founder needs "that naive belief that you're going to make it happen". Marc Andreessen hunted grand slams; Paulus, "a little too skeptical", looks for practical problems where he can differentiate.
- Bootstrap test: pick a market whose cash flow allows it. Life insurance pays "up front commissions for virtually the entire value", so "What would be the reason to raise?" "if you can't make it work, there's probably something at the core of your business that's wrong." Many insurtechs assumed economics improve with scale: "They don't. Google just gets more expensive".
- Focus without VCs: Assurance moved to Bellevue, Washington, "out of sight, out of mind", and simply followed customers: "if customers did better with an agent, we'd put them on an agent". If $100,000 spent on Google in a month didn't come back, "the business is done", so it tracked "the day, the hour, the minute": "There was no such thing as a quarter for us." It grew to hundreds of millions of dollars of revenue.
- Why sell to Prudential: Medicare was growing fast, but its cash flow is "the opposite" of life insurance: costly to acquire, and "It's illegal for carriers to advance you commission", which meant hundreds of millions of dollars of debt financing. As a broker, "a small change in the Swiss Re algorithm, and our economics would be underwater"; direct sellers like Geico and Progressive own their underwriting, and Assurance's volume needed "basically a trillion-dollar balance sheet".
- PCM Encore began as his own family office. Managing his father-in-law's money (a Logitech co-founder) showed how differently goals and taxes shape a portfolio, and in 2024 it opened to other families. Even "a billion-dollar-plus single-family office" is "subscale" and "can't get the best talent".
- Tax: endowments combine "seven to nine asset classes" with partly uncorrelated returns, but for a taxable investor paying 45–50% (New York, California) the style is "pretty darn inefficient": "You're mostly throwing off gains." PCM, a fiduciary with its own tax practice, harvests losses through direct indexing and real assets; the biggest tax wins often lie outside the money it manages.
- Effort goes where returns can differ: "uncommon investment opportunities" in a lower-middle-market private equity strategy and in real estate. In commodified parts of the portfolio it is "very fee-focused", or pays exceptional managers who are "worth it".
- Real estate: "95% of real estate deals" he wouldn't touch. Hotels and offices carry "equity-like risk" tied to credit and economic cycles, where a plain equity portfolio likely out-returns them after tax. He wants real estate in the bond slot instead: "if you owned a bond since 2020, you've lost 25-30% of that just to inflation".
- West Coast multifamily: in recessions "people are forced back into apartments", and "rents are the single biggest component to inflation". He expects "low teens returns" (development "well into the 20s"), tax write-offs of 130–140% of every dollar invested under "the new big beautiful bill", and returns often unrelated to stocks.
- Rates as an option (May 2026): deals underwritten at today's rates, with no rent growth assumed, should pencil to mid-teens or low-20s returns, and the "worst-case scenario is they probably stay about where they are". A 100bp cut, "very feasible" with a new Fed chair, means cap rates "20% lower" and "40, 50% IRR deals". Five years ago prices assumed "perfection" and the upside was hard to see.
- Supply cliff (May 2026): COVID-era zero rates brought many starts, then "really nothing has started in the past 3 or 4 years". Double-digit Bay Area rent increases are "the canary in the coal mine", with growing tech-forward cities such as Bellevue next. Opportunity-zone deals can earn IRRs "in the 20s or 30s just from the tax benefits".
- AI barbell: own what AI won't touch (apartments; pest control, "the last thing" a machine will do; franchise restaurants) or sit in the middle of it, where value is created, "but you better be ready to dance". Sourcing uncommon deals may be the last job AI takes, because "the best data on uncommon investments is not anywhere on the internet".
- Judgment: read banking history (Morgan, Rothschild, Warburg) because it "tends to really repeat itself", stay out of "the Silicon Valley New York echo chambers", and argue both sides of a question. Prize people "willing to come out of a conversation with a different viewpoint"; success risks "a gilded cage" where everyone says yes.
