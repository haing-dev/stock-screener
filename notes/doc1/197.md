### 197. Gavin Baker — Fidelity lessons, surviving drawdowns and why AI isn't a bubble yet (Generating Alpha Podcast, Jul 2026)

*Managing partner and CIO of Atreides Management; spent nearly two decades at Fidelity, where he ran the $17bn OTC portfolio and helped make Fidelity a large early holder of Nvidia and Tesla.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FmLGYLQ6DFY)

**Takeaway:** Every revolutionary technology brings a bubble, but Baker (Jul 2026) sees no AI valuation bubble: tech trades at a discount to staples, AI spending has paid off so far, and he hopes shortages of "watts and wafers" plus the scars of 2000 will prevent overbuilding. Investing well takes a process that fits your temperament, work others won't do, and surviving the drawdowns that come every few years.

- Find "an investment philosophy and process that fits with your own unique emotional makeup", so you can "make good high quality decisions when you're wrong". Most newcomers think value investing is a discovery, but "everybody on planet Earth has read all that stuff many times"; at Fidelity many styles succeed.
- Start in a commodity industry: on cement and aggregates he learned that "ultimately everything comes down to supply and demand", and to model price and volume separately, because a price change falls "all the way down to the pre-tax income line".
- Feb 2000, six weeks into covering semis: customers' "days inventory were at all-time highs", finished goods too, and chipmakers' own inventories were at records, while stocks traded at "multiples no one had ever seen before". His note said this "is not consistent with the demand environment"; the senior tech analyst downgraded his stocks and took him to see every PM.
- His two picks were Nvidia and Integrated Circuit Systems, run by Hock Tan. Dell's model is to find a rich profit pool and drain it; Hock's is "to find a profit pool that has been drained and there's no competition left. Enter that and jack prices up". Jensen, Hock and Lisa retain exceptional engineers; "Most other semiconductor companies fail to retain exceptional executives."
- At 25 he got the large-cap pharma "sector call wrong" and fell from top-ranked analyst to the bottom. Public equities are "the biggest competitive set on planet Earth", so "no matter how good you are, you're going to have really hard times", and "You cannot succeed as an investor if you don't love the game".
- Tobacco: three court cases were "existential risks to the stocks", and he believed missing one meant being fired. He went to "every single trial, hearing", found former clerks of each judge to interpret them and read tens of thousands of pages. He called all three: "you can for sure get an edge in litigation if you do that kind of work", even though outcomes are binary.
- Retail: every retailer said it disliked e-commerce margins. A Fidelity colleague (Tim Cohen \[?\]) showed him why e-commerce began in deflationary goods, where "the working capital cycle really worked for you", unlike Home Depot's. From 2007 to 2021 he was asked about a bubble "in 90% of client meetings", and performance "really came down to we overweight or underweight the internet".
- Bubbles (Carlota Perez): "anytime you get a truly revolutionary new technology, you always get a bubble". Markets rightly spot the revolution, then a "breakdown of diversity in opinion" (Mauboussin) fuels overbuilding and a crash. For AI the key is whether the build-out is "funded out of debt or cash flows".
- Valuation (Jul 2026): "tech's at the same multiple um it was at five or six years ago", multiples "have compressed since the beginning of 25" and tech is "at a discount to staples, which happens very rarely". The real question is a capex bubble, but "the ROI on all this spend thus far has been really positive", with "a little divot" now as Blackwell spend goes to training.
- Why he hopes for no bubble: the scars of 2000 (down "80 or 85" then vs 60–65 in 2009) "puts a lid on tech valuations", and "we're fundamentally short watts and wafers". TSMC's executives, who "dismissed" Sam Altman "as a podcast bro", won't "expand capacity as fast as the world wants them to", and a chipmaking lead is hard to close because the recipe, not the ASML tools, differs.
- "as AI eats the world, silicon is going to eat the world": AI is "probabilistic and it is recomputing the answer each time", far more compute-hungry than human-written software, so "watts and wafers are everything". He values Michael Burry's Substack: "We want a really smart person credibly banging a bearish drum."
- Video games: AI world models will "lower the cost of developing a game by 90%", which "might end up being really bad" for game makers (more competition) but good for platforms (an "explosion of content"). AI replacing local GPU rendering within 5–7 years "is ridiculous": rendering Monopoly Go with a video model at list prices would cost "more than two orders of magnitude" more than its revenue.
- Tesla: just before its lockup ended, at a \~$1.5bn market cap, few came to hear Musk's first-principles case. EVs were "the only car or type of transportation where the core inputs were deflationary" (battery energy density compounding mid-to-high single digits a year), and handle, accelerate and protect better. He has read every Tesla and Nvidia transcript: "when you identify an exceptional company, you always stay close to what's happening".
- Drawdowns: "if you're in a drawdown, you were wrong" (Fidelity's George Vanderheiden: "being early is the same thing as being wrong"). A mentor's rule: "you either have to panic early or double down late. And essentially, no one does both"; he is "a double down late person". Expect "a really tough time every two, three, or four years"; keep touchstones (old drawdown notes, A Wizard of Earthsea on pride) and a hobby with "a number that you can make improve".
