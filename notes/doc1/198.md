### 198. Steve Cohen — Probability bets, managing risk and running Point72 (Generating Alpha Podcast, Jan 2025)

*Founder and CEO of Point72 Asset Management, successor to the hedge fund he started in 1992, and majority owner of the New York Mets; a short interview with two teenage hosts.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ThAOHHXqZmE)

**Takeaway:** Investing is making good probability bets: know why your view isn't already in the price, keep processing information, and add to a loser only after rechecking the work. At Point72's size (\~$37 billion, Jan 2025) his job is managing risk-takers rather than a book, and passion and conviction mattered more to him than mentors.

- Career: he joined Gruntal & Co. at 21, learned the options business, stayed 14 years and outgrew it because it couldn't provide enough capital. At 36 he started his own hedge fund with $20 million in 1992; its successor, Point72, runs about $37 billion with 3,200 employees (Jan 2025).
- He played poker every day in high school (once at 5:30 a.m. on the day of his SATs). With little money he "had to win", which taught him risk under pressure and when to play, fold or raise; it stopped being about money and became "making good probability bets which is sort of what I do in the markets". A crowded house of eight children "probably helped me deal with chaos in the markets".
- Learn from the tape: at 12–13 he watched stock prices on TV, and at 14 worked at a clothing store next to a brokerage so he could check quotes. At Penn he skipped class to stand outside a local brokerage watching the tape until they invited him in: "I got a better education there than I did in school".
- Decisions: "investing is really about probabilities", measuring risk and reward and feeling confident in the work; Point72 is heavily fundamental. When pitched an idea he asks many questions to "try to understand why this is a view that's not already reflected in the market price".
- "you can't ignore the macro": the US and world economy ultimately drive company earnings and stock prices. Recessions, expansions, new trends, or Russia invading Ukraine and moving gas and oil prices all have to be monitored.
- Losing positions: a stock sits in a long/short portfolio, where shorts may be protecting you. When one goes against you, recheck the work, make sure you're not missing something and find out why; "you may want to add if you've done all the work" and still see a great opportunity.
- Horizon and efficiency: quants trade second by second, but his fundamental discretionary equity teams don't trade minute by minute, because "your transaction costs would kill you". Asked whether efficient markets leave fewer opportunities: "we have had a phenomenal year last year", and "I think the quality of work is getting better" (Jan 2025).
- Running a multi-strategy firm (macro, quant, equities, credit): he recently stopped trading his own book because, at 3,200 employees, "it's better for the firm that I am actively managing the firm and not a book". Each strategy has its own nuances (he had just interviewed a European gas trader), but "in all cases you're taking risk and then the question is how do you manage that risk".
- Hiring portfolio managers and senior analysts: track record, passion, experience managing people, and "a specialty or expertise in something as opposed to being a generalist"; "we like sector expertise". The Point72 Academy hired for 40 positions from 30,000 applicants worldwide this year.
- Culture: collaborative and transparent, and "it's not like I give them Capital they give me performance I feel like I owe them too", so he helps people develop their careers and keeps his door open.
- AI: automating manual work frees people for higher-value work, and Point72 creates "a lot of unique data" that AI tools could harness to "find new ways to be profitable".
- The Mets (bought in 2020, per the host) are a "civic responsibility" he is "willing to lose money to support". Like a hedge fund, a team runs on analytics and on evaluating and developing talent (the farm system); he wanted his quants to help with baseball analytics, but "they didn't really bite on that one".
- Build your own path: someone taught him options, but he created his own style, which others later copied "because it was pretty profitable". As he grew, people said he didn't need to be bigger: "people just do not have the imagination", and "it's very easy to get talked out of something you feel passionate about". His advice: "if you love what you do it's not work".
