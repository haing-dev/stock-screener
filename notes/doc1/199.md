### 199. Alan Patricof — From the family-office file drawer to Apax, Greycroft and Primetime (Generating Alpha Podcast, Feb 2025)

*Venture pioneer who founded Alan Patricof Associates (later Apax Partners), Greycroft (2006) and Primetime Partners (investing for an ageing population), interviewed at 90 by two high-school hosts.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X4DBaWiM9XM)

**Takeaway:** Back leaders first and economics second; early-stage investing is "an art not a science", and a few winners pay for many mistakes. He has seen euphoric overpricing \~10 times and sees it again in AI (Feb 2025).

- The original idea: at a family office everyone watched the public portfolio, while small private deals ($100,000 or $500,000) went into a "file drawer" and were forgotten because private companies didn't report.
- There he backed three early-stage companies (New York Magazine, which he chaired, a medical-electronics company and a cable, TV and radio business). All three succeeded, which "doesn't usually work that way".
- His first firm managed family offices' private deals "so they don't have to even think about it". It added an SBIC and then institutional money, and he "probably was the first person to open up an operation in Europe". At \~12 countries APA "added an X" and became Apax.
- Why Europe lagged: in England, if your company failed "you probably couldn't get another job"; founders gave up employment contracts, a car and driver and a pension; and there were no public markets.
- Style drift: Apax's European partners leaned to later-stage deals "which were safer and where it was more based on numbers than on technology or extrapolation of Market opportunities". Apax became almost entirely private equity, and he left in 2001, "after the blood bath of the bubble", walking away from "frankly a gold mine".
- The same drift at Greycroft (internet and media): it "has started to slide into the private Equity business from Venture", though it still does early-stage deals, now in AI. He puts Greycroft at about $3 billion under management and Apax at about $83 billion.
- Primetime Partners (started \~4 years ago) invests in products, services and technologies for older people: "there'll be more people over 60 in 10 years than there will be under 18".
- Euphoria (Feb 2025): "we're right now in a euphoric moment for AI companies I've seen this play 10 times already" (PCs, cell phones, storage, the internet). There are always winners "but there are also a lot of losers", and after "extreme valuations in the last year" "not everybody's going to come out of this with a smile on their face".
- Apple: he bought into the second round after one phone call with Steve Jobs, who "didn't try very hard to sell me", investing $315,000 from a modest fund. His book values it at $7 billion today; a newer computation says $10 billion. He calls it luck, though he had seen the PC revolution coming through Commodore and Texas Instruments.
- The catch: you had to "sit with it for 40 years", and "the Venture business is not meant for holding things for 40 years your investors want you to give liquidity". The fund distributed the stock in the 1980s and "did not realize anything like that return".
- What he looks for: leadership first. "If you've got a great leader they can have a second rate product but they'll do something to fix it"; the best product with a "second rate leader" won't reach its potential. Second, the economics: what it takes to make a profit and how much capital that needs.
- Partnerships: share the economics and make partners feel part of "a total effort" rather than "a oneman band"; "I've seen more firms fall apart because of the politics". Apax is in its 55th year and its third generation of management.
- Advice: "everything in life is cumulative", including reputation. Strangers stop him almost daily with "you don't remember me but" stories, and they are "almost consistently positive".
