### 200. Michael Mauboussin — Expectations investing, cash flow vs earnings, and skill vs luck (Generating Alpha Podcast, Feb 2025)

*Head of Consilient Research at Counterpoint Global (Morgan Stanley Investment Management) and adjunct professor at Columbia Business School since 1993; a career interview on the Generating Alpha podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Wu7q2IhYI4s)

**Takeaway:** Every asset is worth the present value of its future cash flows, so ask what the price implies and whether the company will beat it. Investment creates value only when it earns above the cost of capital, which EPS, multiples and even free cash flow can hide; where luck is large, as in investing, process matters more than outcomes.

- Three lessons from Al Rappaport's *Creating Shareholder Value*: "it's not about accounting numbers but about economic value"; valuation and strategy must be "joined at the hip"; and its stock-market-signals chapter asks "what do I have to believe to justify today's stock price".
- Expectations investing in three steps: work out what's priced in; use strategic and financial analysis to judge whether the company will meet, exceed or fall short of it; then buy, sell or hold. In his metaphor, step one is how high the bar is set for the high jumper and step two is how high the company will jump.
- Any asset is worth the present value of its future cash flows, so "everything's a DCF model", whether venture capital, buyouts, public equities or bonds. P/E and EV/EBITDA are shorthands that save time but carry shortcomings and biases.
- Base rates: a paper on "lifetime earnings" studied \~16,000 IPOs since 1975. About 20% are still around, 40% merged or were bought, and 40% went defunct or were delisted.
- Free cash flow = net operating profit after taxes minus investment in future growth, and "can be itself quite misleading". The guiding mantra is to "make investments that earn above the opportunity cost of capital".
- Walmart had negative free cash flow "for the first 10 years that it was public but it was spectacular": it invested more than it earned, at very high returns. Negative FCF is neither right nor wrong; what matters is the return on the investment.
- Multiples ignore how much investment the earnings needed. A company can "increase earnings per share but to destroy value" by earning below the cost of capital on incremental investment; the same goes for EV/EBITDA.
- M&A shows the gap: releases trumpet accretion to adjusted EPS plus a synergy number, yet the most common outcome is accretive to EPS, value-destroying, and the buyer's stock falls. The market is saying "we don't think you're going to earn your cost of capital".
- Skill vs luck runs from all luck (roulette, lotteries) to all skill (running races, chess). The more luck, the more process matters, because "you need to repeat a small advantage over time" for skill to surface; luck-heavy measures need bigger samples.
- Humility: "forecasting is inherently extremely difficult to do", and "the market is smarter than you are". Anyone who thinks they've "got it licked" hasn't been around long; the market humbles everybody sooner or later.
- Intuition works in stable, linear systems like chess but serves you poorly in unstable, nonlinear ones such as markets and economies. Kahneman and Gary Klein's adversarial collaboration found it "very domain specific"; people overextend it, and success stories hide the failures.
- Skills he stresses: numeracy; accounting, which lags the shift from factories to intangibles such as software; seeing "everything is an expected value bet"; and, when you think you see an inefficiency, asking "who's on the other side of my trade". Great investors share "a deep sense of curiosity" and read avidly.
- Lesson from his car-dealer father: "your livelihood is the cash that's generated by the business".
- Screen implication: ROIC rules fit his framework better than cash-conversion rules, since a high-return reinvestor can show negative FCF for years. The price-implied expectations (a reverse DCF) are worth showing next to valuation multiples.
