### 201. Nick Del Deo — Inside the data center boom: who earns the returns, and how to value CoreWeave (The Real Eisman Playbook, Oct 2025)

*Nick Del Deo, digital infrastructure analyst at MoffettNathanson (covering Equinix, Digital Realty, CoreWeave, Lumen and Cogent), walks Steve Eisman through the data-center industry, its returns and its power constraints.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G121VNcu0dg)

**Takeaway:** AI demand turned the commodity end of data centers, which used to earn about its cost of capital, into a low-teens-return business because power and capacity are scarce, and private capital is doing most of the building. Del Deo values CoreWeave on the net present value earned per dollar of GPU capex rather than on EBITDA multiples, and expects returns to compress as chips and power loosen.

- Who owns the boom: Digital Realty and Equinix are about the only listed data-center landlords left. Others were taken private (QTS by Blackstone, CyrusOne by KKR and GIP) because "The sheer volume of capital required" and years of negative cash flow put public investors off. Private operators run up to twice the leverage ("it could be twice as high"), use structures like ABS, and QTS could be twice Digital Realty's size by capacity in a few years. Equinix (\~$90–100bn EV) and DLR (\~$75bn) are too big to take private; QTS and CyrusOne were \~$20bn.
- REIT trade-off: paying out most cash flow forces REITs back to the capital markets to grow, "one of the sore points among public investors". But REIT status removes corporate income tax: "you're basically trading off not paying corporate income taxes for maybe having to issue equity over time". Equinix, like the tower companies, converted only once its taxable income turned positive.
- Costs: a data center costs "low teens, millions of dollars per megawatt", so a 100 MW building is "$1.2 billion or so", and the equipment inside costs "three to five times the cost of the building". Most of the hyperscalers' \~$350bn of 2025 capex goes on servers and network gear; leased space shows up as leases, not capex.
- Three kinds of data center: hyperscale (one or a few huge tenants; "a very competitive niche" with little to differentiate), enterprise (internal systems for the likes of JPMorgan or Walmart), and interconnection hubs where networks swap traffic. Equinix built neutral hubs, "a literal network effect" where everyone comes because everyone is there, "akin to the Chicago Merc or eBay".
- Returns: before AI, hyperscale and plain enterprise data centers earned ROIC that "wasn't terribly different than their cost of capital" (\~7–8%), so their AFFO per share and stocks struggled, while interconnection players (Equinix, CoreSite) earned low-to-mid-teens ROIC. AI created real supply constraints: large-footprint pricing, which used to fall each year, has "probably doubled", lifting hyperscale returns to "low teens" with far more capital to deploy. But "lifting supply constraints really helps from a volume perspective but it hurts returns": if power catches up, returns revert toward the cost of capital.
- Location: AI training "is not latency sensitive" and needs cheap power and land, so it goes to Texas, North Dakota, Wyoming and Iowa rather than tier-one metros. Third-party developers avoid these "oddball markets" for fear they can't re-lease them when leases end in 10–15 years. That leaves them to hyperscalers, aggressive private players and Bitcoin miners repurposing their power for AI (CoreWeave is buying Core Scientific).
- Why Equinix and DLR fell in 2025 while AI infrastructure soared: Equinix's June analyst day laid out heavy capex, and new retail-style facilities take time to fill. AFFO per share growth, \~10% a year recently, dips to mid-single digits next year before returning toward 10% "if the plan works". Eisman: had Equinix been private, the plan "would be approved by in a heartbeat"; Del Deo agreed that is why so many operators went private. DLR fell in sympathy after running from \~$80 to \~$180.
- Valuation flip: Equinix used to trade at a clear premium; now DLR trades at "a premium of a few turns" on AFFO or EBITDA. Adjust for Equinix's higher stock-based comp. DLR's longer hyperscale leases, whose rents have "skyrocketed", give it more re-leasing upside and a proportionally bigger development pipeline. Equinix earns better returns per dollar, but its niche limits how much it can invest.
- Equinix and AI: it hosts "a little under 40%" of the world's cloud on-ramps, a hub for multi-cloud enterprises; hyperscalers are its largest customers but a much smaller share of revenue than at peers. It should gain as latency-sensitive inference (gaming, real-time ads) and private AI deployments grow (Bristol Myers put a private GPU deployment in an Equinix site), but "It's never going to lead to the sort of like rocket ship results" seen elsewhere.
- CoreWeave (2025): a GPU cloud that leases its buildings and owns the GPUs; Microsoft is \~70% of revenue, set to shift as OpenAI deals ramp. Revenue went from "$16 million" in 2022 to $5.3bn in 2025 and may reach the mid-$20s bn by 2028, on $21–23bn of capex this year. An OpenAI contract worth \~$14–15bn over 5 years might need "40 billion or so in equipment", putting its capital budget "in the same league as AT&T, Walmart, Exxon Mobile".
- Valuing CoreWeave: Del Deo rejects peers' habit of putting data-center or Microsoft/Google multiples on its EBITDA or operating income ("which I would not endorse"); \~65% EBITDA margins ignore the GPU spend, "totally the wrong way to look at this business". He models each contract's unit economics (upfront cash and capex, 4–5 years of locked-in revenue, rent and power, some tail value) and finds "something like 15 to 20 cents" of NPV per dollar of capex. Value = that spread × the capex CoreWeave can deploy until it reaches a steady state. His target was $65 with the stock \~$90 (2025).
- CoreWeave risks: a GPU's economic life is unknown (CoreWeave depreciates over six years; OpenAI signed 5 years plus two one-year options; Nvidia is speeding up releases). The "big battleship" cloud providers have "essentially limitless resources", and easing chip supply should weigh on pricing and returns. CoreWeave is "a legitimately good operator" of failure-prone GPU clusters, but holders must "believe in OpenAI", which is very cash-flow negative, exposed to fast followers like DeepSeek and, if dominant, able to squeeze suppliers.
- Power (2025): US data-center capacity has grown \~15% a year and \~20% is a reasonable forecast; installed capacity is \~25 GW against \~1,250 GW of US generating capacity. PJM's capacity-auction prices are "screaming for people to come in and build generation", and operators buy gas turbines to self-generate until they get a grid connection. Forecasts of hundreds of gigawatts carry "a lot of double counting" (one customer shopping five developers yields five utility requests), so utilities now make applicants put "skin in the game".
- Fiber: in the telecom bubble Level 3 laid 10–12 conduits per route but used one, and the spares "sat unused for two and a half decades" until hyperscalers needed long-haul fiber. Lumen (stock \~$1 a year earlier, \~$4 in 2025) signed \~$8–9bn of contracts with all four hyperscalers to fill them, but Del Deo is "a little skeptical": "growth will improve but not as much as the company articulates". He likes Cogent (\~$1.5bn market cap), which is converting the old Sprint network into a third wavelength supplier against Lumen and Zayo, though "I've been wrong the last couple years".
