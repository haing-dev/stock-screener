### 203. Tom Gayner — Mistakes of omission, hot stocks and 35 years of Markel's portfolio (Library of Mistakes, Nov 2025)

*CEO of Markel Group, who has managed its equity portfolio since joining in December 1990; an evening conversation and Q&A at the Library of Mistakes, a financial-history library in Edinburgh.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uR3N1jfeOew)

**Takeaway:** Gayner's costliest errors are things he didn't do, above all passing on Berkshire in 1984 because "no stock could possibly be worth that much". His edge is temperament: avoid hot stocks, hold trusted businesses for decades so taxes stay deferred, and let insurance cash flow buy in every quarter.

- Record (host Peter Hollis \[?\]): since Dec 1990 Markel's share price and NAV per share are each up over 140x, \~15% a year for 35 years. The equity portfolio, now $13bn, returned almost 13% a year: 1.5 points a year above the US market and 2.5 above global equities.
- Underwriting first: in the high-rate 1980s insurers commonly paid out $1.03–1.08 per premium dollar and lived on interest income. Markel wrote coverage that is hard to get, demanded a combined ratio of 99 or better, and invested the "penny or two of underwriting profits" in equities, the formula Buffett used to build Berkshire.
- RJR Nabisco, Feb 1990: Drexel Burnham's bankruptcy collapsed the junk-bond market. He judged RJR the most creditworthy LBO and bought its zero-coupon bonds at \~30 cents against a rough \~70 value in bankruptcy, "the fulcrum security". Tax rules taxed the accreted interest, so few holders could own them; Steve Markel, with non-deductible interest expense, could. They were paid off at par 10 months later, and Gayner joined Markel that December.
- Errors of commission are small and self-limiting, and come with a feedback loop to learn from. Errors of omission are "way worse": "you don't have to tell anybody", and that "takes away your ability to learn".
- His biggest mistake: after Carol Loomis's 1984 Fortune article on Buffett, Berkshire's S&P tear sheet showed great numbers, "But I thought no stock could possibly be worth that much" (now $740,000 a share, Nov 2025). Berkshire was Markel's first purchase in 1990, after Steve Markel "twisted my arm". He added nothing until the late 1990s; it has been \~10–15% of the portfolio since.
- Late 1990s: he "couldn't pencil out numbers" for the dot-coms, and the portfolio was flat or down over two years while the S&P rose over 50% (host's figures). "I am in a race between being right and being fired." Steve Markel's monthly review covered the top 5 holdings and the top 5 news stocks they didn't own. For \~18 months it ended: "I understand what you are doing and why you're doing it. And I understand what you are not doing it and why you're not doing it. See you next month."
- "I have never been a cool kid. I've never owned a hot stock." He skipped bank takeover plays, the drug stocks (Merck, Pfizer) and the dot-coms, and distrusts parties where "you and your wallet are cordially invited". Nvidia's market cap equals Germany's GDP; he would rather own Germany, and "I'm never going to own Nvidia". Markel has held five of the Magnificent Seven "for a decade plus"; Tesla and Palantir are among what it doesn't own (Nov 2025).
- Deferred tax as free funding: the $13bn equity portfolio cost \~$4bn, so its \~$9bn gain at a \~25% tax rate is over $2bn of "in essence zero cost financing" (Nov 2025). Turnover runs 5–10% a year.
- Portfolio shape: over 100 stocks, many of them "library cards" (tiny stakes that make him think about a company). The bottom 40 don't move returns; the top 20 are \~60–70%. Diageo, Brown-Forman and Pernod count as "one and a half" positions. Diversification matters more now, since fewer outcomes fit his 90% confidence intervals, and small stakes that keep rising ("Why? Why?") often grow into large ones.
- Insurance is the engine: in \~140 quarters at Markel, all but perhaps one (9/11 or a 2005 hurricane) brought new money into the investment account. Steady inflows make "a below average investor look like an above average investor" through dollar-cost averaging. Starting over, he would build the insurance business first: "that's really where the permanent capital comes from".
- Banks: he lost money in First Pennsylvania, Continental Illinois and First Republic of Texas. Now, when tempted, "I sort of lay down until the fever passes". Banks face "risk of ruin" from runs; insurance money is stickier because a claim needs an event ("Your building had to burn"), so 95% of his work in financials is insurance. He owns "some" bank stocks.
- People: "you can't do a good deal with a bad person" (his father). Liking a manager is "a good signal. Not a perfect signal"; "Not liking somebody is an incredibly powerful signal". He passed on an acquisition whose CEO was "not as smart as what he thinks he is and that's dangerous".
- Idea flow: he tells able shareholders "You are smarter than I am, but I am managing money for you", so after acting for their own clients they should tell him what to buy or sell. Twenty years ago he knew 14 of Markel's top 20 holders and 4 were computers; now he knows 7 and 9 are computers.
- AI as a bubble: "I don't know." Read Trollope's The Way We Live Now and swap "railroads" for "AI". America marries engineers, visionaries and "financial scamsters": much capital is lost, but society keeps the railroads.
