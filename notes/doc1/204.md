### 204. Ryan Tunis — P&C stocks worth owning as the soft market begins (The Real Eisman Playbook, Sep 2026)

*Property and casualty (P&C) insurance analyst at Cantor, interviewed by Steve Eisman (Ep 74), who adds his own views on AIG (he owns it), Trupanion (he is short) and Kinsale.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4m6174aphVA)

**Takeaway:** P&C insurance runs on its own pricing cycle, not the economy's, and a soft market began about a year ago after a seven-year hard market (Sep 2026). Tunis prefers well-reserved, middle-market Travelers, "self-help" AIG and the brokers, and has never been more negative on personal lines.

- Why P&C is different (Eisman): "it's not cyclical with the economy. Its pricing cycles go according to its own rhythm", so investors own it for stability, not growth. Tunis: insurers know their revenue but not their cost of goods (losses); if inflation lifts claims above the $700,000 expected on $1m of premium, margins shrink, and that makes the cycle.
- The cycle (Sep 2026): hard markets, when carriers can raise price at will, usually last two or three years out of 10, but the last one ran seven. Softening began about a year ago in "capacity" lines such as large-ticket property, where "You need capital and a checkbook", and is leaking into casualty.
- Soft-market playbook: favour Main Street, agent-based middle-market business (Travelers' footprint), which is hard to "burn your way into"; large accounts (more of Chubb's book) face the most competition. Soft markets suit stock picking, because business mix and "how conservatively are you reserved" decide who absorbs rate cuts. Travelers' reserves impress him most.
- He recommends Travelers and AIG but not Chubb, which Eisman calls probably the best commercial P&C company (Tunis agrees). Travelers and Chubb trade at \~12–12.5x earnings ("a little bit too expensive") and \~2.4x \[?\] and \~2x book.
- AIG (Buy): a "self-help story" at around book value with an ROE of "reliably 11%", now pure-play P&C after shedding the last of Corebridge. Its large-account mix suits a hard market, but the 2027 targets, endorsed by the incoming CEO, rest on expense savings and reinsurance synergies rather than the cycle.
- AIG history: Eisman heard that Hank Greenberg's decentralized style left AIG with 200 operating systems, which took \~10 years to work through; Tunis adds that fixing its underwriting culture also took a long time. Eisman is "very confident" the stock won't regain its pre-crisis peak of 1,200 in his lifetime.
- Personal lines (Tunis has never been more negative; neutral on Progressive, which Eisman calls arguably "the best insurance company in the world"): auto rates rose \~60% in three or four years when \~40% was needed, because insurers overestimated inflation. Everyone is over-earning and piling back in to grow: a "once in a generation" soft market with rate cuts, last seen in 2006, likely to run "through the end of 2027".
- Structural threat: driver-assist systems (ADAS) are on \~40% of new vehicles vs 15% three or four years ago, and collision frequency has fallen sharply. The \~$350bn auto premium pool is a measure of exposure units, and "Fewer exposure units means you just don't have that TAM."
- Distribution: personal lines are \~half a trillion dollars of North American premium (\~80% auto, 20% home). Direct writers GEICO and Progressive have taken \~1.5 points of share a year for \~15 years, to a little over 30%, because auto is an "involuntary product"; homeowners has been "pretty impenetrable" to direct sellers, since bundles need agents.
- Brokers vs AI: carriers' \~30% expense ratios (costs plus commissions over premium) don't bother shareholders, so going direct gains little and alienates brokers. "A third of all claims that are filed are initially denied", so clients want Marsh or Aon, not "an AI bot"; broker pay has been flat for two decades. Eisman notes an AI scare once knocked brokers down 10% in a day, "and then nothing basically happened".
- Brokers are his top subgroup: retention plus clients buying more cover as they grow makes growth "sort of automatic". Broker organic growth seems to have bottomed at \~4–5%, above the 3% of the last soft market, at multiples in line with that market. The real AI risk is in HR and benefits consulting.
- Reinsurers (Eisman: "It's just a balance sheet"): consolidation (Munich Re, Everest, Hannover Re, Swiss Re, RenRe, Arch) makes them more reliable long-term capital partners. But the industry has accepted "returns that are unacceptable" before, so discipline is unproven; much negativity is priced in.
- Trupanion (Eisman short for years; Tunis neutral): a \~$1bn pet insurer that has never shown acceptable unit economics and targets free cash flow of 2% of revenue (\~$30–40m). The policy costs \~$1,000, and it has 20% of a \~$6bn market and is losing share; past M&A bids near these levels stop Tunis getting "way too negative".
- Kinsale (non-admitted, E&S insurer of small firms): Eisman relays a claim that it refuses to pay claims; Tunis calls it a "savvy underwriter" with E&S-typical mid-50s loss ratios and low costs, partly from paying brokers less. In a soft market small accounts flow back to the standard market, and "They've never really traded in a soft market before."
