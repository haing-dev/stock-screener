### 207. Ken Fisher — Top-down investing, the Great Humiliator and late-cycle leadership (The Intrinsic Value Podcast, Aug 2019)

*Founder and chairman of Fisher Investments, then managing over $100bn; interviewed by Preston Pysh and Stig Brodersen on The Investor's Podcast (TIP255).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xra37vXQxlA)

**Takeaway:** Markets pre-price everything widely known and humble everyone, so even the best investor is right only \~70% of the time. Decide top-down which categories should lead now, pick stocks within them, always hold a hedge against being wrong, and stay bullish unless something big, bad and not yet priced appears.

- Humility: "if you're the greatest in the world you're still probably right no more than 70 percent of the time", so get comfortable being wrong. You must be able to take "a financial emotional punch" and learn who you are, because "capital markets are a very expensive place to learn that".
- The market is "the great Humiliator", out to humiliate as many people as possible for as many dollars as possible for as long as possible, and most of all the biggest and most successful. As a baby brother he learned to want what his "superior direct competition" (most big players) didn't, because head-on he'd lose.
- No category is better: over very long periods growth and value, big and small, and sectors such as tech and healthcare end up with "nearly identical returns". Favoring one is "ridiculous": "I don't think anything's better". Small-cap value is "an insurance policy" against big-cap growth, and the US against the rest of the world.
- Top-down process: split the world into countries, sectors, growth vs value and big vs small, decide which should do best now, then pick stocks within those. The first job is "trying to get rid of most of the haystack even though you get rid of some needles with it"; hunting needles mostly gets you "a lot of hay stuck in your clothes".
- Example (Aug 2019): the US beat non-US markets, but strip out technology and America did "pretty much exactly the same" as Europe over the last few years, "down to a rounding error". The gap is American tech's dominance, so going US-only is a concentrated tech bet: "if you're wrong you'd be dead".
- Always own a counterweight: "I always want to buy some stuff that does badly", because if it lags, the main bets worked. Never bet too heavily: "if you bet too heavy and you're wrong you die".
- As a bull market ages, breadth narrows, so go where "80% of the fish are over in one part of the lake". Late buyers are people "too afraid to own anything before", who buy big, high-quality growth names they know. In 2019 that was big tech, as in 1998–99, when the mega caps were "fundamentally sound companies" yet tech lagged from 2000 to 2009 "because it got too carried away before".
- Small caps have beaten large since measured data began, "but it's done it in these little bursts": the first 20% of five bull markets coming off bear-market bottoms. Take those out and big caps did better. In the next bear market, "the categories that have led the most toward the end of this bull market will lag badly".
- Markets pre-price all widely known information, so if you agree with the consensus "you can't possibly succeed"; reflexive contrarianism is also "kind of stupid". Clock face: if everyone expects noon, 11:30–12:30 is priced in, but 3 or 9 could happen while a contrarian bets on 6. Use what's priced to rule out part of the possibility spectrum, then bet on the likeliest of the rest.
- His bias: "you should always be bullish unless you've got a good reason to be bearish", and good reasons are rare. World GDP of $80tn+ growing \~4% nominal adds \~$3tn a year, so a recession needs bad news bigger than that. Taking a bull market down needs something "big bad ugly and not pre-priced", not a stray $100bn or a Fed misstep.
- Biggest miss, 2007–08: he blames FASB and IASB mark-to-market rules, which wrote \~$2tn off global bank balance sheets in 12 months. The increase in mortgage defaults was under 10% of those writedowns, yet banks had to restrict lending, and he missed it completely.
- Consensus errors (Aug 2019): since \~2000 the news business has grown "stubborn to hold on to false stories", which makes betting against them "an arbitrage opportunity". Every year polls expect long-term rates to rise and every year they're wrong; with globalism added, Milton Friedman "described exactly how to think about this".
- Bubble call (Aug 2019): "a bubble building that nobody sees" in ESG, infrastructure investing and impact investing. It isn't about to burst, but the next downturn will pop it and make things worse by "about another trillion bucks", a round-number guess.
- Screen implication: bottom-up screens don't ask which size, style, sector or country should lead now. Fisher would; small-cap screens pay mainly early in bull markets.
