### 208. Tom Gayner — From value spotter to value creator: the four lenses (Talks at Google, Jun 2015)

*Then chief investment officer of Markel Corp (later its CEO), at Google on how his approach moved from statistical bargains to compounding businesses.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2sG91e1Wh4I)

**Takeaway:** Buying statistical bargains and waiting for the price gap to close stopped working for him, so he now looks for businesses that keep creating value. Of his four lenses, reinvestment matters most, and the costliest valuation error is never buying a compounder because it never looked cheap enough.

- Graham's quantitative bargains suited the post-Depression 1930s, but "that pond has gotten a little overfished". A price gap is a snapshot, and the gaps did not close after he bought. So he moved to spotting value creation, "instead of a picture how about a movie": what will the business be worth next year and "the decade after that"?
- Lens 1 is a profitable business with good returns on capital and not too much leverage; each of those words "came about because I made a mistake". He wants "a demonstrated record of profitability", not descriptions of what will happen someday.
- Leverage: in 2008–09 he took "tough losses" in businesses where he "did not really appreciate how much leverage was inherent". Even a good business may have to refinance "at a time when the markets just don't want to give it to you", like having a good hand ripped away mid-game.
- Lens 2, judging management from outside: as in dating, look for overlapping values. He spends "a lot more time reading about people" (annual reports, proxy statements, magazine articles) than meeting them, because "there's a good paper trail". Judgment improves by "trial and error with the emphasis on error".
- Lens 3, reinvestment: a chef-owned gourmet restaurant can be superb "but it's not replicable"; McDonald's opening store after store is where "that reinvestment Dynamic kicks in". Ask "how big can this be how scalable is it how replicable is it".
- The spectrum: best is a business with very good returns that can reinvest at "the same sort of rate year after year" (rare, and "very richly priced when you see it"). Worst earns poor returns yet "seems to need gobs of it all the time"; he used to joke about airlines. Second best earns high returns it can't reinvest, run by managers "intellectually honest" enough to buy businesses, pay dividends or buy back stock, as Buffett did with Berkshire's textile cash.
- Reinvestment is the lens he weighs most: will the business get better or worse over time? Such businesses are hard to find, so when you find one, "don't be a penny pincher".
- Lens 4 has two errors. Overpaying is "an error that you can recover from". The bigger, hidden cost is a business that "did compound and you never bought it because it never met your test of valuation"; people remember what they did, not what they failed to do.
- Even Graham made more from GEICO, a growth company, than from all his other investments combined, and needed his partner Jerome Newman to close the deal. Gayner recommends the first edition of Security Analysis, where Graham says growth is "extremely important it's just hard to calculate".
- Read the new-highs list first. He used to start with the Wall Street Journal's new-lows list, looking for bargains. Now he reads new highs first and asks "what does the market know that I don't know". This "very gross distinction" proved "tremendously effective in pushing me more towards better quality companies".
- Google (Jun 2015): he owns some, not a lot, and feels "stupid" about missing it. His sticking point is "how the compensation at senior levels works" and what Google intends to accomplish for its shareholders.
- Markel's engine (Jun 2015): in 1990 he got $2M to manage out of a \~$50M pot; he now oversees \~$20bn-plus, \~$4.5bn of it in equities. The stock has gone from $8 to \~$800, roughly half from cumulative insurance profits and half from investing them.
- Individuals can copy the structure: live on less than you earn and you have cash flow to invest and no clients to pull money at bad times. Fund investors earn "a fraction" of fund returns by buying after rises and selling after falls. His formula: "live on less of what you make invest it reasonably and live a long time".
- Screen implication: his "second best business" (high returns, little to reinvest, cash returned by honest managers) fails a revenue-growth gate such as Core's 5% minimum, and he finds the new-highs list a better source of quality ideas than the new-lows list.
