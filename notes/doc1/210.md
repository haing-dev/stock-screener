### 210. Tom Gayner — What changed in his four tests: incremental returns, EBITDA and five-year pay (The Intrinsic Value Podcast, Dec 2020)

*Then co-CEO of Markel Corporation, interviewed on The Investor's Podcast (TIP) about how his investment tests have evolved over 30 years.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z52LyI9j1rI)

**Takeaway:** His four tests have held for decades, but the price test changed most: from accountant-style bargains below net working capital to qualitative judgments made "with trepidation and humility". What counts is the next dollar: "your returns as an investor will converge towards what the incremental returns on the incremental dollars are over time".

- Leverage is the constant: levering okay returns on capital into higher returns on equity "introduces a degree of fragility". With ultra-low rates some debt is rational ("not too much of it"), and structure matters: overnight credit "that reprices regularly" is "fundamentally different" from 30-year fixed-rate financing. He urged his children to take 30-year fixed mortgages that cost less than rent (Dec 2020).
- The names of good businesses change. Some "were fine businesses 5 10 20 30 years ago that aren't anymore", and "returns on capital typically don't straight line": they are either improving or deteriorating.
- A record of reinvesting says little about "the next dollar": "it's not about the past it's about the future". Ask whether incremental dollars will earn better or worse returns than past ones.
- Price changed most. Graham-era buying below net working capital relied on "external" numbers. Qualitative judgments "you can't put them in a spreadsheet"; a young investor making them may feel confident, but "you're probably wrong". Older investors make them anyway, "with trepidation and humility".
- Screener-style quantitative work is "necessary but i don't think it is sufficient". Use it to try to disprove a qualitative thesis: the data "doesn't tell you you're right", but it can show you are probably wrong.
- EBITDA, which his 2019 letter called "the least worst proxy" for Markel Ventures (as the host read it): it misleads for a steel mill depreciated on historical cost that will cost far more to replace ("you're kidding people you're probably kidding yourself"), but fits a radio station with little capex. The bigger the D, the less EBITDA tells you; Ventures is "much more radio station like then steel mill like".
- Integrity tells: years of good results are "a very good tell"; a great company does things "for their customers rather than to their customers". Beyond that, "trust your gut a little bit".
- Debt as a character test, from Shelby Davis in Omaha as Markel began buying whole businesses: to avoid crooks, "buy one where they don't have any debt", because "crooks don't want to steal their own money". An owner who loses his own equity loses everything; a manager of a debt-financed business "can move on".
- Five-year pay: Markel pays its managers on a rolling five-year average: "if things go well for markel i get paid if things don't i don't get paid". If fired, he jokes, he'd make 12-year-old Scotch, so it would be "11 and three quarters years" before anyone knew if he was any good. Long-cycle work needs a culture where people aren't "second guessed or micromanaged or pulled off the task midstream".
- 2020: insured losses in Q1 and "unprecedented business conditions" at Markel Ventures, but enough margin of safety that "we've lived to fight another day". The three-engine strategy (insurance, industrial and service businesses, investments) "hasn't changed one iota". In March–May he "chose to err on the side of caution" and expects to be "justly and appropriately criticized" for it. Survival comes first: "compounding cannot take place if the chain is interrupted".
- Persistence beats yield: in Buffett's example of an 8% current-coupon bond against a 6% zero, falling reinvestment rates made the 6% zero, which "compounded for the next 30 years", the better buy. Hares are "way better traders" than tortoises but often worse investors.
- Macro is important but unknowable, so he makes sure what he owns won't be disrupted by it. Of some holdings he joked, "if those stocks were countries they'd be switzerland": "they don't win wars but they don't lose wars". An 1820 investor couldn't foresee the macro but could judge that Johnnie Walker, a trusted pharmacist, had integrity; Diageo, a long-term Markel holding, still sells the brand. Back him and 15 others, lose 14, and the compounder "swamps the amount of money you lost".
- Index funds: "a great idea gets done to the point where it gets overdone and ultimately becomes a bad idea"; much money is now invested "without a fulsome understanding" of what it owns (Dec 2020).
- Screen implication: check the return on incremental capital and its direction, not just a long-run median ROIC, and distrust EBITDA multiples where depreciation is large.
