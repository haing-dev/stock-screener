### 211. Bob Brackett — Why energy stocks fell as oil rose, and how to buy commodity cycles (The Real Eisman Playbook, May 2026)

*Bernstein's oil, gas and mining analyst for \~17 years (PhD in geology; previously a Hess executive and a management consultant), interviewed by Steve Eisman (Ep 60) after Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dFGIXqpyVzA)

**Takeaway:** Commodity investing is "a bit of a boring cyclical strategy": buy commodities trading below mid-cycle, in markets too big to be flooded, and read the cycle from EBITDA margins. In May 2026 energy stocks fell despite \~$100 oil because investors treat the backwardated forward curve as a forecast, yet refilling \~1 billion barrels of drained inventories and a new fear premium argue for a higher mid-cycle price.

- Who sets prices: growth mandates hold almost no energy, so value investors do, buying when oil is below mid-cycle (Brent has averaged \~75 in real terms since 2000). In 2025 oil "stunk" (glut, OPEC spare capacity, mid-50s), but the equities "never dropped to those equivalent levels". His sentiment gauge: if people at a party perk up when he says what he covers, "we're late cycle and there's probably nothing to be made there". Eisman: these are "more trading vehicles than long-term growth stocks".
- The 2026 shock: Iran showed it can close the Strait of Hormuz, which carried 20% of world oil and natural gas flows, half of seaborne sulfur and 7% of aluminium. With \~20 of \~100 million barrels a day cut off, commercial and now government inventories are draining "at a rate we haven't really seen before": \~1 billion barrels so far (May 2026). Yet oil sits slightly below 100 (peak \~115, per Eisman), under the 120 reached when Russia's \~7 million barrels a day of exports came off the market. Brackett blames "market hope": the market "simply believes that this situation will be fixed", and energy is only \~4% of the S&P.
- Why Exxon fell: early in 2026 generalists rotated out of software (AI disruption) into Exxon for upward revisions with oil at $60, a "beautiful anti-correlation between Microsoft and ExxonMobil". Since the attacks 2026 EBITDA is being revised up: with \~50% EBITDA margins, oil at 90 instead of 60 lifts revenue 50% and EBITDA \~100% ("That's the flex"). But oil investors look 1–2 years out, and the backwardated forward curve falls toward \~$75 a year out. The curve was "never built to be a predictor"; it is "almost pricing an insurance policy", yet the market treats it as a forecast.
- Why producers are worth more anyway: windfall cash cuts net debt, so "enterprise value will transfer to market cap", and funds buybacks. Refilling \~1 billion barrels over years adds demand comparable to oil's \~1% annual growth, so "we should have a higher mid-cycle price", plus a lasting "fear premium" because Iran could close the strait again. The UAE's 2026 exit from OPEC is "a little worse" for prices but not "cataclysmic": Qatar left years ago without flooding the market.
- Gas is his growth story: US electricity demand, flat for 15 years, now grows 3–4% a year on electrification and AI, and a 1 GW data centre needs the power of a city of a million people. Wind and solar go only so far, nuclear is slow and coal is retiring, so "the call on more electricity is really a call on gas". Theme: "long whatever AI needs, short whatever AI can do". Among large caps, the pure-play gas producers that "rise to the top" are Expand Energy (EXE, the biggest US gas producer) and EQT.
- Gas discipline: Permian gas that comes with oil is now priced negative because the pipelines are full: "It is a waste product." Prices are set in the Haynesville and Appalachia, where producers for 10–15 years added rigs whenever Henry Hub covered costs and earned only a marginal return. Then investors said: "Give me half the cash flow, you can use the other half." With power and LNG demand rising and supply disciplined, "you can finally earn a reasonable through cycle return on natural gas".
- Reading the cycle: miners are "mean reverting stocks", and EBITDA margins show where you are. A zero margin (price equals extraction cost) is the bottom; "astronomic" margins are "too good to be true" and get reinvested in new mines. Decades of data link the entry margin to forward returns: buying at the bottom (GFC, COVID, the end of the China supercycle) gives "great forward returns".
- His screen: which commodities trade below mid-cycle ("I want some beta to that"), in markets "big enough that can't be disturbed too quickly". Only iron ore, copper, gold and aluminium are $100-billion-a-year markets. Lithium grows double digits but is "a bit of the siren song": one billion-dollar project from a big miner can flood it, and brine from oil and Appalachian gas wells (starting in Arkansas) could add low-cost supply that "should scare lithium investors".
- Copper: a long-term bull case. About 30 million tons a year is mined and recycled, demand grows \~1 million tons a year, existing mines lose 1 million tons plus a year, and "you can't frack for copper". But tariff threats (50% on steel and aluminium, a threatened 50% on copper) built the largest US copper stockpiles in decades, which could be dumped if tariffs don't come. His large-cap pick if positive: Freeport. His job is not finding great companies: "It's identifying great entry points."
- Shale's lesson: post-GFC capital funded shale growth, "Not profitably", and until 2014–15 the best stocks were the fastest growers. A learning curve, wells drilled in a week instead of five-year offshore projects, "infinite capital", $10 million units of capex and an OPEC price war collapsed prices. In Jan–Feb 2016 rating agencies cut their oil decks from $70 to $50, debt markets shut, and companies cut dividends and raised equity with "huge dilution". Today's mantra is "a dollar to reinvest a dollar back to you", with FCF yields of 6–7% for the best and low teens for those with more debt and less credibility.
- Majors: no one has a data edge on Exxon (unlike shale, where every US and Canadian well is public and hedge funds can make a generalist "somebody else's exit liquidity"), so grade management on "what did you tell me a quarter ago, did you deliver it". Majors are "extremely well-run": they deploy \~$25 billion a year on time and on budget, earn mid-teens returns on capital through the cycle, and their \~3% dividends are "untouchable". Exxon, Chevron and ConocoPhillips paid through COVID; European integrateds cut. Exxon pays \~$20 billion of dividends plus 20–25 billion of buybacks a year. Compare such yields with TIPS, not Treasuries, since they are inflation-protected.
- Picks (May 2026): ExxonMobil, whose larger refining arm lowers beta and helps as oil comes off a peak, since product prices fall slower than crude. Diamondback (FANG), a single-basin (Midland) "basin master" that levered up for acquisitions and lacks EOG's track record, is attractive; such companies eventually get bought (Pioneer by Exxon, Hess by Chevron, Concho by ConocoPhillips). EOG is "so transparently great that it's typically an expensive stock", and its decentralised multi-basin model is hard for command-and-control majors to digest (Exxon's XTO was "a bad acquisition").
- Miners and LNG: after the same "path of punishment", miners return "oodles" of cash (2–3% dividends plus 2–3% buybacks), at lower FCF yields than oil companies, which must yield "hundreds of basis points" more because oil may have no terminal value. Cheniere is a toll booth: \~95% of capacity under take-or-pay contracts averaging 20 years (blended \~17) at about $2.50 per MCF, revenue booked at the dock, with no price or geopolitical risk. It trades near an 8% FCF yield vs \~5% for gas midstream peers in the S&P 500, which he expects it to join in 2026.
- Screen implication: his method matches the cyclicals and trough-cash screens: margins near the bottom of their own range, a commodity below mid-cycle, and supply discipline rather than growth capex. Add a manual check that the commodity market is big enough (iron ore, copper, gold, aluminium) that one new project can't flood it.
