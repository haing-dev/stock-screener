### 220. Bill Gurley & Brad Gerstner — AI winners and losers in big tech, Nvidia and the chip buildout (BG2 Pod, Feb 2024)

*Episode 3 of BG2: Bill Gurley (Benchmark) and Brad Gerstner (Altimeter) sort the large tech companies into AI winners and losers, then debate the compute buildout, Nvidia and new chip ventures, the day before Nvidia's earnings.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-i9AGk3DJ90)

**Takeaway:** Ask of each company whether AI enhances or attacks its existing business, then whether the price already reflects that. Gerstner sees Google search as the textbook innovator's dilemma (already discounted at the lowest P/E of its peers) and expects compute demand to beat consensus; Gurley doubts that new chip and fab ventures can catch Nvidia and TSMC.

- Gerstner's "private equity approach to the public markets": get the phase shifts and super cycles right, not the quarter. Priceline went from a billion-dollar company to 120 billion, "A 120 bagger", while analysts fixated on hotels added per quarter instead of how much travel would move online. On Nvidia's quarter: "we have no edge".
- Gurley borrows Michael Mauboussin and Paul Johnson's competitive advantage period (CAP): back into how many years of free cash flow the market cap assumes. Coca-Cola can grow 3% yet trade at 40–50x earnings because its advantage is durable. When disruption closes the CAP, the multiple collapses: BlackBerry fooled people "into thinking it was a value play cuz it was trading at 10 times earnings", and Yahoo the same. Multiples reflect growth plus durability (the discount rate).
- The test (Gerstner): is the existing business "enhanced or attacked" by AI, and what new businesses open up? And is "the juice worth the squeeze" for customers: if Copilot makes engineers 30–50% more productive, yes. Gurley's clear winner is Microsoft (Office, developer tools); Gerstner adds its early OpenAI deal.
- Google search math (Gerstner, Feb 2024): \~10 billion queries a day growing \~4% a year, with 13% more ads on the page, gives a 17% search CAGR. Ten blue links cost \~1/3 of a penny per query; a 750-token answer costs \~4 cents, 10x more, and with query refinement up to 50x. Revenue per search likely falls too, so a \~95% margin at 99% share might net out at 50–60% with lower share. "Google's margin is their opportunity" for Perplexity, Microsoft and Meta.
- Search and YouTube make over 80% of Google's profits, so this is "case exhibit number one" of the innovator's dilemma: Google knows it and is trying to "thread the needle". The first queries lost are low-value, Wikipedia-like ones, so revenue won't break at once, but "the horse is out of the barn on answers". Gerstner: challengers only need to cover costs, and he would sell answers without ads.
- Margins mean-revert: the hosts cite Jeremy Grantham's line, "Profit margins are probably the most mean reverting series in finance". Gerstner quotes Satya Nadella: "all the money is made in the two to three years around a phase shift".
- Is it priced in? Consensus 2023–25 growth (Feb 2024): Microsoft 14%, Amazon 12%, Nvidia 42%, Google 11%. Google trades at the lowest P/E (21x 2024), and Nvidia is cheaper than its P/E suggests on a PEG ratio. Gerstner: Google's headwinds are "already discounted in the stock"; if it threads the needle, "I'll climb on board that bus".
- Next 10x moments: memory and actions. Gurley: true memory is hard because the model "would literally have to retrain every night on each every individual user", and memory adds switching costs, "which means higher multiple". Gerstner: Apple could run a small on-device model that knows the user and routes hard questions to bigger models; Rabbit's "large action model" booked an Uber in a demo.
- Meta (Gerstner): AI targeting improved engagement across Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp and backfilled the ad losses from Apple's tracking changes, when the stock was at "90 bucks". New lines: AI agents for WhatsApp businesses, shopping agents and AI glasses.
- Apple (Gurley): it owns the best asset, the phone, but has never been good at internet services, and Siri has "been terrible". Until Tim Cook commits to a "180 degrees" pivot, "it's a doubters camp". Gerstner: running a 5–10 billion parameter model on the phone, with memory and actions, could unlock a big device cycle.
- Amazon: "We've seen almost no share shift from AWS to Azure" because of "data gravity" (Gerstner). Gurley cites Andy Jassy: training costs are "tiny compared to the inference costs", so inference should favour lower-power, lower-cost chips such as Amazon's own.
- Nvidia (Gerstner): Jensen Huang sees a $1 trillion data-center installed base becoming "two trillion" in four to five years, all accelerated. Consensus at $700 a share implies Nvidia's share falls from 55% to 26% by 2028. A B100 costs more than an H100 but does far more work, which protects margins for now. He expects a "zone of disillusionment" and will use it "as a buying opportunity". Gurley: "the worry's more on pricing than volume".
- Gurley's chip skepticism: Altman's reported $7 trillion and Masayoshi Son's $100 billion plans target the same Middle East investors, a "smell of loose money". Servers "depreciate a bit like fish". Taking on Nvidia and TSMC at once with a 20% chance against each leaves \~4%; first silicon can cost $50–100 million and yields start "crappy". AMD is worth more than Intel "because they got out of the fab business".
- Morris Chang (as the hosts relay his MIT talk): Texas fab operator churn was 12% in a recession and 25% in good times, and "you just can't run a fab plant with 25% churn". Vietnam or India is likelier to challenge Taiwan; Gurley is "somewhat skeptical" of the CHIPS Act. Gerstner wants four or five load-balancing locations, not US onshoring.
