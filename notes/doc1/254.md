### 254. Ester Baiget — Novonesis: enzymes worth 1–5% of a customer's costs, volume-led growth and repricing (NBIM, Nov 2025)

*President and CEO of Novonesis, the enzyme and microbe maker formed by merging Novozymes and Chr. Hansen (main shareholder the Novo Nordisk Foundation); a chemical engineer who spent 25 years at Dow; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UPtPobAYCcM)

**Takeaway:** Novonesis sells biological ingredients that are a small share of customers' costs but decide how their products perform, so customers rarely leave and the company can grow two to four times faster than its 1–2% end markets. A new pricing discipline turned annual price erosion into a source of growth.

- What it is: "the leading bio solutions company", whose enzymes and microbes replace chemicals and "silently touch" "more than two billion people every day". NBIM owns 2.5%, worth more than $700 million (Tangen).
- How it works: Novonesis engineers and screens a library of 100,000 microbial strains, ferments at scale (Kalundborg's fermenters hold 300 cubic metres, as tall as a building, in one- to two-day batches), then separates and formats the product. It sells tailor-made solutions, often "cocktails" of enzymes and yeasts.
- End markets: dairy (yogurt taste and texture, high-protein), baking (freshness, clean label), biofuels and detergents (washing at low temperatures, biodegradable), across "more than 30 different markets".
- Market: the biosolutions market is "60 billion" including amino acids and vitamins, but she measures the opportunity against the "trillion dollars" specialty-chemicals market. Asked for market share, she would only say "it is strong".
- Growth: "high single digit", mainly from volume ("a little bit by price"), in food and household-care markets growing 1–2%, so "we outgrow two, three, four times the markets". R&D is 10% of revenue.
- Why customers pay: its products are "1 to 5% of the cost of goods sold" of a yogurt, bread or detergent, yet set its performance: "You cannot make low sugar added with cheaper sugar."
- Stickiness: customers switch "only if you mess up", so reliable supply matters most. Productivity adds 2–5% more volume a year, and capacity is built beyond that. Once in, "we stay", starting small and adding products (in dairy: cultures, then texture enzymes, then natural preservatives).
- Margins and pricing: "you don't have 25% EBIT margin or 37% EBIT the margin if you don't understand the value" (25% EBIT at legacy Novozymes; Novonesis reports a \~37% EBITDA margin). Annual price talks and a "fair share of value" at contract renewal moved pricing from "1 to 2% erosion" at Novozymes to a "1 to 2% contributor of revenue growth".
- Regulation is the bottleneck, because biological products are assessed like non-biodegradable chemicals. A microbe to replace a fertilizer takes six to eight years to register in Europe and two in Brazil; a novel protein up to 5 years in Europe and six months in Singapore.
- The prize, in her numbers: using today's biosolutions to their full potential would cut global CO2 emissions by 8% and unlock 800 billion of economic profit by 2035. It is "homegrown", needing no chips or imported naphtha.
- AI: predicting a protein's shape after one amino-acid change once took a year of lab data; it now takes less than a minute, because the strain library is documented and linked to field performance.
- Pipeline timing (carbon capture, enzymes that recycle PET plastic, specialized proteins, enzymes for biopharma productivity) depends on regulation and the rest of the value chain, so "we only invest further when the market is ready". Turning biomass into bioethanol took 10 years to reach commercial plants.
- Merger integration: a year between signing and closing let it ask all 10,000 employees what to keep and what to let go; the new culture is neither legacy one. The biggest threat is failing to prioritize, so "a yes and also needs to mean a no or yes later". From Dow she brought the habit of watching "all the lines of the P&L" and cash flow.
