### 257. Thomas Schinecker — Roche: pharma as a 13-year portfolio bet, AI targets, China's rise and the pricing bargain (NBIM, Nov 2024)

*CEO of Roche for just over a year and at the company for 21 years (previously head of Diagnostics); a molecular biologist who grew up in Singapore; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LQ6lAvNMjPE)

**Takeaway:** Drug development is a 13-year, 1-in-10 portfolio bet that must be paid back in about 10 years of exclusivity. Roche manages it like an investment portfolio, uses diagnostics plus pharma to move toward prevention, and is setting AI targets to make R&D 40% faster and 20% cheaper, while warning that China is catching up fast and Europe has grown "too comfortable".

- Position (Nov 2024): Roche is the second-largest healthcare company by revenue, with pharma and diagnostics "Under One Roof". It leads in diagnostics, neuroscience and hemophilia, is becoming the leader in ophthalmology and is top three in oncology. NBIM owns 2.3%, worth more than $6bn.
- Oncology: Roche has been one of the leaders for two decades, since buying Genentech and its antibody know-how, and puts "50% of 13 billion" of R&D into oncology. Sequencing a genome cost billions and took more than 10 years in 2003; now it takes days and costs hundreds of francs. Next: personalized cancer vaccines, and drug combinations to stop resistance, as with HIV.
- Launch math: a new medicine takes \~13 years and only "one out of 10" candidates makes it, so launching about two new medicines a year for 10–15 years is "Quite a feat". Roche aims for 20 this decade, and lifting success "from 10% to 20%" would double the medicines reaching patients.
- R&D as a portfolio: Roche spends 13 billion Swiss francs a year, "the highest spender" in the industry yet a fraction of global R&D, so about 50% of its launched medicines came from outside. Each project gets an NPV and a "probability of success". High-risk, high-reward bets (such as Parkinson's) are mixed with surer ones, because Roche must "earn back our R&D money".
- Obesity miss: Roche had an incretin in its pipeline in 2010–11 (and Chugai an oral one) but left the field: "we should have stayed in this space". Obesity is linked to cancer, kidney and eye disease and possibly Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, so treating it is prevention.
- Brain shuttle: less than half a percent of an injected medicine reaches the brain. Attaching a shuttle protein carries antibodies across the blood-brain barrier; an Alzheimer's drug using it could launch in the "28 29 time frame".
- Diagnostics plus pharma: health systems want better outcomes at lower cost, which means prevention, slowing progression and cures, and all three need diagnostics to find high-risk patients and causes. Data businesses (Foundation Medicine, Flatiron) feed both drug design and treatment choices.
- Innovation culture: "very low turnover" keeps people on long projects; follow the science with a "long breath" rather than changing course. Three separate early-research engines (Chugai, Genentech's gRED and Roche's pRED) give "three unique shots at goal".
- AI: let AI design thousands of molecules and test only a handful in the lab. Roche targets 40% faster R&D (where it thinks the industry will be by the end of the decade) and 20% lower cost, and wants AI to draft the "800 900 pages" of regulatory submissions.
- Europe vs China: Tangen notes Europe's share of clinical trials has halved in 10 years. The US wins on venture capital; in Europe, getting a trial approved can take a year, and Europe may have "become too comfortable". China's patents are rising "exponentially" and its talent is returning; Roche in-licenses Chinese molecules ("the science is great"). The US still leads, but China is "catching up very quickly".
- Pricing bargain: after 13 years and 1-in-10 odds, a drug has "around 10 years", shortening in some places, to earn back the failures before generics and biosimilars arrive. As he puts it, "our industry is actually the pipeline of the generics and biosimilar Industry"; 44 Roche medicines are on the WHO essential-medicines list.
- Broken incentives: there have been no new antibiotics for 50 years, because a new one would be held in reserve, so for biotechs "the business model doesn't work". Differential pricing is a contract between rich and poor countries; leaders demanding the "same price as in some country in Africa" would stop launches in poorer markets.
- Long view, short-term delivery: Tangen notes the last three CEOs averaged 15 years in the job against 5 for the S&P 500, and that the founding families hold "the majority of the Voting Rights"; Schinecker says this structure supports long-term thinking. Yet recent quarters showed "9% each quarter" growth. His advice: "there are no shortcuts in life".
