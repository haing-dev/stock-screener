### 259. Ajay Banga — The World Bank's AAA funding model, debt workouts and lessons from Mastercard (NBIM, Oct 2023)

*President of the World Bank and former CEO of Mastercard (12 years), after Nestlé and PepsiCo in India and 13–14 years at Citibank; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's In Good Company while in Oslo to meet Norway's government and the Nordic-Baltic governors.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vMkli_QhMJ4)

**Takeaway:** The World Bank turns donor capital into cheap loans by issuing AAA bonds, but even a bigger bank can't fund the world's "intertwined" crises without private capital, and debt workouts start with transparency. From Mastercard he brings urgency, thoughtful risk-taking and broad employee share ownership.

- Funding model: donor countries' capital plus retained earnings, levered up through bonds of the kind Norway and NBIM buy. The AAA rating lets them borrow "at a relatively good price"; IDA, the concessional arm, gives grants and "basically zero cost financing" to the poorest countries.
- Ownership of the IBRD lending arm: US 16–17%, Japan \~7%, China \~5%. The last capital raise (2018) trimmed the US a little and lifted Europe and others; more re-weighting is possible ("absolutely") but only at the next capital increase, timing "no idea".
- China and the BRICS: the problems are "way too big for us to get caught up in competitive issues on development". He wants more lenders pushing, but not "fragmented shoulders because scale does matter"; example, a joint deal with the Inter-American Development Bank on the Amazon, Caribbean climate and digital learning.
- Debt workouts start with the data: poor countries borrow from China, other governments, multilaterals and private lenders on dissimilar covenants, and some lenders asked borrowers "to not disclose the terms". Zambia's restructuring came once all creditors put their numbers on the table, and "people have to take a haircut including the commercial creditors".
- G20 in India (2023): focus on multilateral development bank (MDB) reform; the G20 backs a better World Bank becoming "a bigger bank". But "there's not enough money in government or mdbs alone", so private capital must come in "in a way that makes sense for the private sector".
- Norway helps through the bank's general capital, IDA and "new instruments like hybrid Capital" under discussion (Oct 2023).
- AI democratizes knowledge, as Google made the encyclopedia free, but poorer countries lack the infrastructure to benefit in the short term. Both AI and incentive schemes need "guide rails".
- Digital ID before payments: "cash is the friend of the rich person not the poor person", since people without ID in a cash economy "don't get counted" and get no credit or insurance. A digital ID is "foundational", if it protects privacy and is cyber-safe.
- Mastercard under Banga: the stock rose from $20 to 360 (\~18x). He joined a year to a year and a half after the IPO, when it had 4,000 people and 20 billion of market cap.
- Employee ownership: staff in share plans grew from "the tens of people" to "the tens of thousands", creating "quite a few thousands" of millionaires. He wants long-term shareholders to push for such plans so staff feel "the pain and the pleasure", without incentives "to do shortterm things to Goose prices".
- Leverage lesson: paid partly in Citi stock, his holding fell "from the tens of millions to the single digit" in the financial crisis; "fortunately I had no loans and no debt". "life is 50% luck the other 50% is what you do with the luck".
- Mastercard's three values: a sense of urgency, "thoughtful risk taking" and accepting mistakes, "because without risk there is no reward". At the World Bank, projects take "interminably long": "development delayed is development denied". His desk notes: "done is better than perfect", "fail smart and fail often", "take the risk".
- Emerging-market consumers buy on unit price: many won't buy a 500 g Nescafé pack but buy a 100 g refill five times, paying more per gram. "it's not just volumes it's volumes at the right unit price that matter"; run global practices with local execution.
- From his first boss's boss at Nestlé India, Barry Ryan: "never take no for an answer". If something failed 10 years ago, check whether the assumptions have changed or it was simply badly executed.
