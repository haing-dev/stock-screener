### 260. Børge Brende — Between two world orders: decoupling, record debt and AI-led growth (NBIM, Jun 2025)

*President and CEO of the World Economic Forum (the Davos meeting) and a former Norwegian foreign minister; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R-ul-WN1zRU)

**Takeaway:** The post-Cold War win-win order is giving way to hard-power competition, but leaders who need growth will stop short of full decoupling. With trend growth down from close to 4% to about 3% and global debt the highest since 1945, Brende counts on new technologies, not fiscal muscle, to keep growth up (Jun 2025).

- World order: from post-1989 "win-win thinking" to a more competitive, mercantilist approach, "from soft power to hard power". Rivals can still agree where interests overlap: cybercrime costs "four five trillion US dollars a year", 3–4% of global GDP.
- Limits to decoupling: leaders need a good economy to be re-elected, and nearshoring, friendshoring and trading only with friends instead of buying the best and cheapest inputs costs growth. He expects "a securing of the global value chains", but "I don't think there will be a full decoupling".
- Growth: trend growth was "close to 4%" for decades and is now "closer to 3%", too low to cut poverty as before. Trade once grew 2–3x faster than trend; now new technologies could lift productivity "maybe with 10% in the coming decade", so no repeat of 1970s stagnation.
- Why the economy held up (Jun 2025): productivity gains and "huge investments" from new technologies, especially in the US and partly India; protectionist measures not yet severe enough to dent growth; and the post-COVID stimulus.
- Debt: "the highest global debt since 1945" leaves "not that much of fiscal muscles" for the next recession, and some countries are more indebted than at any time since the Napoleonic wars. The "private sector is still very vibrant" and has "a lot of money to invest", so problems need public-private work.
- US (Jun 2025): 27% of the global economy with 5% of the population and almost 50% of global military power. Paying more for imports and a tighter labor market from immigration curbs "can lead to higher inflation", but he expects a soft landing rather than a hard one.
- Europe: after the Davos "gloom and doom" (Jan 2025) he is "more optimistic on Europe now". Spain, with 40% youth unemployment \~15 years ago, is the fastest-growing OECD economy (US included); the euro, once written off, is "still the second most important currency in the world". Drags: red tape, slow follow-up of the Draghi report, domestic politics.
- US–China, the "G2": the US is almost 30% of global GDP and China almost 20%. China's exports to the US fell from 20% of its exports in 2013 to \~11–12%, but demand for manufactured goods "has peaked" as it shifts to services and digital trade; China also faces property prices and a shrinking workforce.
- Energy: "politics maybe have changed but science hasn't", but climate policy must be weighed against access (700 million people lack electricity) and security; basing almost half of Europe's gas on Russia was a bad idea. China is "the first electrostate", balanced with coal, LNG and nuclear; AI might bring commercial fusion in a decade instead of three or four.
- Buyers can move markets: a WEF idea from COP26 (Glasgow) has big companies green their supply chains, e.g. Apple says "25% of their aluminium needs to be green", which "creates also a big demand".
- AI: DeepSeek built a leading model for a third to a quarter of others' cost, so "we're maybe a little bit closer to the software phase where we can do applications" rather than a hardware phase needing trillions. Back-office jobs will go, but people move up the value chain: in Switzerland, he says, 95% of the population farmed 100 years ago vs \~2% now.
- Power and turnover: whichever of the US and China leads new technologies "will probably emerge as the most powerful nation also of this century". Yet many of today's 10 largest companies by market cap did not exist two or three decades ago, so the leaders of a few decades from now may not be founded yet. "Data is the new oxygen in this economy."
- Emerging markets: the platform economy was winner-takes-all, creating "private superpowers", but AI opens chances for young, fast-growing economies (Nigeria, Indonesia, India, Bangladesh, Vietnam). China has \~130 unicorns and adds about 10 a quarter; "the jury is out" on whether trillion-dollar investments are needed.
- Short tenures: CEOs now last "three four years" and voters punish incumbents, so at Davos "the name tags are changing much more frequently". He reads this as short-term thinking, since some reforms take a decade to show results, and says a medium- to long-term view should be rewarded.
