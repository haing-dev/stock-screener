### 261. Christine Lagarde — 1920s echoes, an oil shock, ECB independence and the dollar's reserve role (NBIM, Mar 2026)

*President of the European Central Bank, formerly France's finance minister, head of the IMF for a decade and head of a global law firm; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen in Frankfurt for NBIM's In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SgawP7BdFew)

**Takeaway:** AI plus geopolitical fragmentation make today look like the 1920s, and Europe, open and short of fossil fuels, is the most exposed to trade and energy shocks such as the Middle East war (Mar 2026). The ECB keeps its 2% target and its independence; Lagarde's main regret from the post-COVID inflation is feeling bound by forward guidance.

- 1920s analogy: then, the combustion engine and the manufacturing line came as the first globalization gave way to fragmentation, and it ended in financial crisis, European bank failures and a world war. Today it is AI plus "challenges to the international world order". Her lesson: diplomacy, sound public budgets and "being attentive to the real transformation and not so much hype".
- Europe's exposure: "Europe is of all advanced economies probably the most open", and apart from renewables its own energy sources are rare, so trade and energy shocks hit it harder than others.
- Middle East war (Mar 2026): shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, \~20–25% of oil traffic, is threatened, with "unprecedented" volatility: "the price of oil can go up by 30% and go down by 30% in a matter of one day". Shipping and insurance costs rise and energy prices have "ripple effects in a matter of months". Short term, reopen the strait; long term, speed up the green transition, with nuclear also growing faster.
- Social model vs defense: Europe's social benefits are "to be assessed, determined, and possibly modified" as societies age, and if securing democracy needs military spending, "reduced benefits in some areas" should be on the table.
- Why central banks need independence: a single price-stability mandate with accountability, and timing. Rate decisions take "6, 9, 12, sometimes more months" to work, while politicians think about public opinion and the next election. ECB independence is "engraved in the treaty"; she backed the US Fed chair as "a beacon of integrity".
- Post-COVID inflation: she concedes the ECB was "a bit slow". One person warned her, "but I did not pay enough attention to that person"; her regret is "to have felt completely bound by previous forward guidance". The ECB now has "better sensors and better analytical tools", and "deviation from 2% will come after my time".
- Productivity: Europe's gap with the US began when "we in Europe have missed the internet revolution". The US will lead AI's pioneering phase (chips, data, energy prices), but in diffusion "Europe is actually a little bit ahead of the US in terms of penetration of AI in the manufacturing process". AI spreads in \~3 years vs 30 for electricity, so gains may come "a lot faster than what we think".
- AI and rates (Mar 2026): AI is not yet deflationary. A frontier model costs close to "1 billion dollars" to train and uses masses of energy, and fragmentation may block the global scale needed to amortize it. She doubts the case that a new Fed chair can front-load rate cuts on AI productivity, as in the Greenspan era: energy is being repriced and "we're not in the same situation as in the Greenspan's days" on public debt.
- Draghi report: progress is real but "not fast enough"; simplification and capital markets union are under way. The brake is 27 sovereign states and national "gold plating": each supervisor adds "another little ratio here, a little buffer there", ending in "27 different regimes". Her fix: EU regulations rather than directives.
- Labor: Europe's workforce is starting to shrink and "we've exhausted the women forces", so expect longer working lives, pension and retirement-age fights and immigration; AI won't replace everything. Reform sticks when inclusive: a Baltic prime minister's IMF program got 80% of its aims, with consent, by including unions "from the get-go".
- Dollar: "about 60% of central bank reserves", \~40% of international trade invoicing and still the crisis destination for capital. Confidence is "probably" slightly undermined, but such shifts take time; the ECB recently widened its euro repo lines to national central banks around the world.
- Digital euro: pilot in 2027, subject to parliament, and full rollout in 2029. Few young people carry banknotes, central bank money anchors the financial system, and the rails for digital assets are "a public good".
- Stablecoins: "I'm not convinced of the business case of stable coins". Their real use is fixing cross-border payments, which banks and a digital euro could take away; otherwise they bridge crypto and fiat. They grew strongly "up until last October. Since last October it's flat" (i.e. since Oct 2025).
- Leadership: the danger is "the corridor thinking, the group think" of people from the same schools and models. She always tries "to have one outlier in the group": "we have to welcome that irritation".
