### 264. Kenneth Rogoff — A 1970s replay: tariffs, the dollar's peak, debt and the next inflation burst (NBIM, Apr 2025)

*Economist, former IMF chief economist and chess grandmaster; co-author with Carmen Reinhart of "This Time Is Different" (2009), with a new book on the dollar due; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zAkj8tOq3kQ)

**Takeaway:** Rogoff reads Trump's tariffs and the pressure on the Fed as a 1970s, Nixon-style replay. He expects another post-pandemic-size inflation burst within 5–7 years, US debt partly inflated away with much higher rates, and a dollar whose global footprint peaked in 2015 and will slowly shrink (Apr 2025).

- Setting (Apr 2025): "it feels a little like the 70s" and "Trump actually feels a little like Nixon". US exceptionalism is "dented"; his biggest worry is damage to the institutions behind it: rule of law, keeping its word, paying its debts.
- Tariffs: a flat 20% "would be very bad, like a big tax hike" but "would not be the end of the world" alongside deregulation; "the real problem is the randomness" and what it signals for central bank independence. A year out he guesses US tariffs on Europe of "at least 10 you know 15% 20%", with retaliation. No Trump put: "I think he has conviction".
- Tariff inflation is "not a big deal": a short-term price rise plus lower quality, paid by US consumers, as every study shows (2018 included). The real cost is slower innovation and growth. Recession odds "have gone up sharply", and it would be "a loss of confidence, not an oil shock, not a pandemic, not a global financial crisis": Democrat consumers, about half, see disaster, and Republicans won't spend enough to offset them.
- Dollar: its footprint (reserves, transactions, pricing) peaked around 2015. It has been lucky in its rivals: the euro is "a regional currency", the yen hardly used, the renminbi even less. Reserve currencies fade slowly (the Spanish peso over \~100 years), and the dollar is "late middleaged": it "is going to stay first, but gradually its place is going to come down", in value too.
- The dollar's main threats are domestic: "the unwillingness to confront debt" and doubts about central bank independence. China, scared by the Russia sanctions and by dollar clearing that lets the US "see everything", is pushing more pricing and loans into renminbi and building its own payment rails.
- China: "if your prices are going down continually and you're telling me you have 5% growth, I don't believe you"; he guesses 2–3% for the next 2–3 years. Savers have lost confidence, rates are falling, there is deflation, and "China's Sweden time is over", a longer Japan-style slump. Housing is the core problem; the state crushed the private sector (Jack Ma, party officials on boards).
- China spillovers: a slowdown hits energy and commodity exporters (Norway, the US, Brazil, Argentina). "China plus one" investment has helped Japan, Korea, Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia and India; India now grows faster than China but is hard to get "euphoric" about.
- Inflation was "in remission and not dead". Central banks in the low-inflation era were good but lucky (globalization, China, little populism), and that tailwind is gone: "inflation's a political economy problem". When a shock hits (cyber war, pandemic, climate, debt) and central banks fold to politics, he expects "another big burst of inflation" within 5–7 years, maybe sooner, and inflation "a problem for the next several decades".
- Fed independence: the Fed "is not the Supreme Court", lacks the ECB's treaty status, and its independence "could disappear in a week", under pressure from left and right. Once tax cuts and stimulus stoke inflation, he expects "Nixon like price controls" and financial repression, with banks forced to hold more government debt at low returns, before Trump's fourth year. Left alone the Fed would hike, setting up "a showdown".
- Post-COVID inflation "was a central bank mistake": if supply chains were to blame, "Why didn't the price of paper towels come down?" Rates should have risen sooner. Forward guidance and the dot plot "got overdone": "markets don't believe it" and central banks tie their own hands.
- US debt: on its current path it gets "resolved by inflation", and next time "interest rates are going to go up a lot more" because investors won't forgive twice. The deficit is \~7% of GDP this year and debt is $36 trillion, heading to $65 trillion in 10 years per the CBO. No sudden crisis or default: "we have the inflation card to play", unlike dollar-borrowing emerging markets or euro-borrowing Greece.
- Rates: the 10-year inflation-indexed Treasury yield averaged zero in the decade after Summers' secular-stagnation speech, hit minus one and is now "around two", "a 3% swing"; US interest payments rose from 250 billion to nearly a trillion. Judge a debt level by its rate: "tell me what interest rate you're paying". Deficits are fine; a big debt is not, because it stops you running them.
- Europe: high debt weighed on Italy, Greece and France, and Europe "wildly underperformed the last 10 years"; Germany can act now because it saved "for a rainy day". US defense spending fell from \~8% of GDP late in the Cold War to 3.5%, and the US can't fight in two regions at once, so Europe must go well past 2% to make up "decades of underinvestment".
- AI may be deflationary like globalization, but he rejects "don't worry about inflation" because political pressure on central banks dominates; his worry is that AI moves faster than people can adjust. Tangen: AI gave NBIM \~15% efficiency gains last year and he expects 20% this year. On crises: "look for it coming out of crypto rather than necessarily high valuations of AI firms".
