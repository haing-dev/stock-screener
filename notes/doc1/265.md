### 265. Cullen Roche — Macro from first principles: money, bonds, time horizons and home bias (The Investor's Podcast, Oct 2022)

*Money manager, author of Pragmatic Capitalism (second edition just out) and the pragcap.com blog, on his 10th visit to The Investor's Podcast with Stig Brodersen.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BnYibUdBTxE)

**Takeaway:** Roche argues that every investor now needs macro: first how money and flows move through balance sheets, then how people react. In practice, plan around time horizons rather than return per unit of risk. After 2022's rate rise, bonds and T-bills became good value again for near-term money (Oct 2022), and home bias is a risk.

- Macro first: picking stocks is like finding "the Michael Phelps" in a river, but "it's equally important to understand the dynamics of the river". He sees passive investing as "really a macro investing approach". Rumours about Deutsche Bank and Credit Suisse (Oct 2022) could pull in the Fed because banks are interconnected.
- Method: model the economy as flows through balance sheets ("income statements reflect the rivers and balance sheets are reflected by the lakes"). Then add psychology: "you can't just get the economics right, You also have to get the response mechanism, right". Investors may buy junk bonds after QE for no economic reason.
- Buying stocks and bonds is "reallocating our savings", not investment in the economic sense of spending "for future production", like a firm building a factory. That kind of investment is high-risk: "over the course of a 10 year period, 90% of corporations will fail".
- All Duration Investing (his 2022 paper): manage risk across time, not just return per unit of risk. He gives every asset a duration, and "the stock market is an 18 year type of bond like instrument inside of my model". Keep near-term needs in cash and short bonds so you are never "a forced seller of stocks into a downturn", "the worst behavioral mistake".
- He loves bond ladders: put 10% of a $100,000 bond allocation ($10,000) in each maturity from one to 10 years and roll each one as it matures, whatever rates do. The two big biases are "being too short term and being too long term".
- Housing: a home is a "deteriorating asset on valuable scarce land" with a high "expense ratio" (upkeep, taxes, time). Net of those and inflation, real estate has "only generated about a one, maybe 2% real return"; buying in 2012 or 2006 made the difference.
- Stocks: Buffett's letters suggest assuming the market makes "four to 5%" after inflation, about half the commonly quoted 10%, before taxes and fees. Control taxes, fees and behaviour; "investing in yourself is the absolute best" investment.
- Home bias: a Londoner in 1800 who never looked abroad missed the US boom, and a Japanese investor who owned only Japan in 1990 suffered. "The reserve currencies come and they go". International beat the US in the 2000s, the US has won for 15–20 years, and that "could be changing right now" (Oct 2022). Owning only the S&P 500 implies "a certain arrogance".
- Bonds (Oct 2022): a five-year bond fell \~15% this year as rates went from zero to 3%, but held to maturity it returns par plus its coupon. Six- and 12-month bills pay 4%, "a world that hasn't existed for 10 to 15 years". At a 5-year duration a 1-point rise costs \~5%, about a year's coupon at 5%, so higher yields cushion further rises: bonds are "a much far superior value today".
- Bonds are "principle protection instruments" for horizons of \~5 years or less, not real-return assets, so he is not a fan of TIPS or 30-year Treasuries. A 4% bill won't beat inflation, but it beats 0% in the bank for six-month money.
- Diversification means "learning to hate some of your portfolio all the time" (his book, quoted by Brodersen). In 2022 stocks were down \~25%, bonds \~11% and gold \~10% while commodities rose and cash was flat in nominal terms. Value "smoked growth" over the past 18 months after a decade of lagging.
- Money: banks are the main money issuers ("loans create deposits"), driven by loan demand and "credit worthy borrowers", not reserves. QE reserves are not really money printing; deficits are closer, since the state sells a bond to a richer saver and spends the cash on people more likely to spend it.
- Rates: the Fed sets the price of short rates but lets the long end float like a dog on a leash. QE controls the quantity of bonds, not their price, so it is weaker than people think. With the 10-year below the 2-year (Oct 2022), the market says the Fed is too tight, which is why the curve is "a pretty good predictor of recessions".
- The Fed's third mandate is financial stability: as a clearing house it keeps payments flowing in panics (the reason it was created after 1907; it worked in 2008). He calls QE "a bad policy" and wants rate-setting more systematic than people "putting their fingers in the air".
