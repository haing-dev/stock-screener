### 266. Margrethe Vestager — Competition policy, big-tech rules and China's industrial playbook (NBIM, Sep 2024)

*EU Commissioner for Competition, also coordinating Europe's digital policy, who brought antitrust cases against Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n-PLrOZUGGs)

**Takeaway:** Vestager sees regulation as keeping markets contestable rather than blocking scale: merger bans should be rare, breakups a last resort, and Europe needs true single markets (capital, telecoms) to grow bigger firms. The larger threats are a slow erosion of competitiveness and China's playbook, which took solar and is now aimed at wind (Sep 2024).

- Rules as market hygiene: the AI Act targets risky uses (a municipality deciding social benefits needs "a human in the loop"), the Digital Services Act makes platforms assess harms such as addictive design, and the Digital Markets Act keeps markets "open and contestable" so a start-up's success "doesn't depend on some gatekeeper opening the gate".
- The Digital Services Act in practice: TikTok wanted to pay users points, usable as shopping vouchers, for liking, watching, sharing and commenting. It couldn't show it had assessed the addiction risk, so the product didn't launch in Europe.
- Siemens–Alstom revisited: the EU blocked the rail merger five or six years ago over signalling and very-high-speed trains, despite warnings that China would take the world market. On review, the Chinese "are very big in China but not in many in any other markets of significance", while Siemens and Alstom have full order books.
- Merger remedies can fail: a divested unit may look fine, but "two years down the road three four years down the road" the buyer "cannot make it". The Commission prefers a buyer already in that market, e.g. a chemicals firm for a chemicals unit, rather than "a venture fund".
- Scale where it helps: Airbus was a pro-competitive combination (otherwise one widebody maker), and there is "room for consolidation of a European Defense industry" as budgets rise and most is spent outside Europe. What blocks cross-border deals: "we really miss a European Capital Market we really miss a European Telco market".
- Telecoms: Tangen notes the US has three operators while Europe has three per country, \~30 in all. Vestager says Europe's four biggest serve \~60% of customers, but cross-border operators still face national rules, "we do not even have a European prefix"; a true single market would let telcos grow organically.
- Banks: one capital market would make cross-border bank mergers easier and worthwhile, and big corporates need banks with scale and global services. Tangen: Credit Suisse had to fail before UBS could buy it, and Europe's biggest bank ranks \~20th worldwide; she can't recall the Commission assessing a bank merger, and says European banks recovered from the financial crisis more slowly than US ones.
- AI: regulating the technology itself is "overly ambitious", and research is untouched; the rules bite on use cases. A diagnostic AI must spot heart-attack symptoms in women as well as men, and Europe's welfare states can be the home market for "trustworthy AI". With the FTC, DOJ and UK CMA she expects AI to speed up market dynamics, as digitization did.
- Breakups are a last resort, but Google's ad-tech case is the exception she flags: Google owns the buy side, the sell side and the marketplace, a conflict of interest the Commission found hard to solve without a third party running part of it. Corporate culture shows in architecture: Apple's gated oval HQ mirrors its walled garden; Google's open campus mirrors Android on others' phones.
- Competitiveness: Europe moves fast in acute crises (pandemic, war, energy, inflation), but its competitiveness crisis is slow and needs market access, safe supply chains, fast permitting, research and better access to capital. She wants a biotech act to cut time to market, since biotech gets regulated like an old-school novel chemical.
- Energy: Europe leaned on Gazprom "because it was cheap" and paid with its security. Supply now spans pipeline gas and LNG from "10 12" prime suppliers, built within a few years; gas stays a transition fuel.
- China's playbook: solar moved to China through joint ventures, takeovers by the Chinese side and a closed home market used as a base, then huge low-cost output "making it very difficult if not impossible for European producers" to compete. Wind faces the same playbook, "luckily not so advanced yet"; the Foreign Subsidies Regulation lets the EU probe subsidized tenders.
- Economic security: of the EU's China triad (climate partner, economic competitor, systemic rival), competition and rivalry have merged, so the focus is now export controls and research security (checking PhD students for espionage) in AI, semiconductors and biotech.
