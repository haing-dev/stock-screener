### 272. Kristalina Georgieva — The IMF view: a smaller-than-feared tariff shock, debt with no buffers, and AI's jobs tsunami (NBIM, Dec 2025)

*Managing Director of the IMF, six years in and starting her second term; Bulgarian, formerly at the World Bank and EU humanitarian commissioner for 5 years. Interviewed by Nicolai Tangen at IMF headquarters in Washington for NBIM's In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M6Z5cO0WLN4)

**Takeaway:** The world economy is "Better than we feared worse than it needs to be": growth above 3% held up thanks to stronger institutions, an agile private sector and a tariff shock smaller than feared. But high debt leaves no buffers for the next shock, inflation isn't beaten yet, and AI may hit labor markets "like a tsunami" (Dec 2025).

- What the IMF does: surveillance of all 191 members (the Article IV "vital signs"), lending for balance-of-payments crises from "lending capacity of $1 trillion", and institution building. It is funded "like a savings bank": members commit resources that are lent and repaid with interest. The US holds "over 17%" of quotas and major decisions need 85%, so it alone has a veto.
- Bulgaria's cure: late-1990s hyperinflation of "over 8,000%" ended with an IMF-backed currency board and fiscal discipline; today it has 24% debt to GDP, a 3% deficit and joins the euro zone on January 1 (2026). Argentina has had many programs and "none of them got the country completely out of trouble"; she hopes its reform-minded president ends the borrowing.
- Outlook (Dec 2025): "over 3% growth this year and next", but the pre-COVID average was 3.7% and growth now "lingers around three", too slow for people's hopes. Resilience has "not been really fully tested" against geopolitics, technology, demography and climate.
- Why the economy held up: post-crisis institutions (independent central banks; fiscal rules, "You cannot spend money you don't have"); a private sector agile enough to stock up before tariffs hit; and a smaller trade shock. Tariffs announced on April 2 were \~23%, the level is now 17–18%, and only \~8–9% is collected. Above all, "that tit for tat did not happen"; hitting back would have done far more damage.
- Debt: with rates "no more zero", debt service crowds out infrastructure, AI readiness and education, and high debt means "no buffers". COVID-era fiscal and monetary space is gone ("Today not possible"), and support programs are hard to withdraw. Her line: "fiscal consolidation do it slowly do it gradually but get your house in good order for the next shock". US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent agrees on cutting the deficit to 3%.
- Inflation: disinflation continues and Europe is at target, but US tariff pressure was muted only because goods were stocked in advance and profitable companies absorbed some of the cost. "we are not quite yet ready to say victory."
- Climate is a financial risk: assets exposed to climate shocks or policy shifts can suffer "unexpected significant losses", and hits to vulnerable economies (Philippines, Vietnam) become financial risks. AI's biggest side effect may be "a departure from a path towards decarbonization".
- AI readiness: an IMF index ranks 174 countries on digital infrastructure, labor-market flexibility and skills, innovation diffusion, and regulation and ethics (the hardest). Singapore, Denmark and the US lead; most developing economies are not ready and will miss the productivity gains.
- AI and growth: the IMF sits between the optimists and pessimists at "between 0.1 and 0.8%" of additional growth, large when growth is slow. But AI will affect 60% of jobs in rich countries, \~40% globally and 26% in low-income countries, "like a tsunami hitting the labor market", risking divergence within and across countries.
- AI bubble (Dec 2025): "companies are profitable. they are making money on AI", so the investment is backed by profits. Some correction is likely because "enthusiasm has pushed valuations that are now so stretched", but she doubts it ends badly, since AI products are "being sold for profit".
- Cooperation: confrontation has made members value cooperation more, and one called the IMF an island of cooperation. Her rule at meetings: "you leave your trade war, your cold war, your hot war outside of this room". In COVID the IMF deployed about a trillion dollars of lending plus a 650 billion SDR allocation, with a first emergency loan (Kyrgyz Republic) within a week.
- On US claims of mission drift, she accepts the need for focus: "we are about 4,000 people". Her proudest achievement is keeping the membership together, e.g. a Ukraine program backed by every member except Russia.
