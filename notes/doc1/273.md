### 273. Adam Grant — Givers and takers, thinking like a scientist and the "I'm not biased" bias (NBIM, Aug 2023)

*Wharton organizational psychologist and author of Give and Take and Think Again; Nicolai Tangen asks him about generosity at work, changing your mind, culture, work hours and his book Hidden Potential (out Oct 2023).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V3Cne8u985o)

**Takeaway:** Judge people by how they make others better, not by visible individual results, because takers are better at being seen. Treat your views as hypotheses to test, and start by admitting your own bias: the best reasoners are the worst at seeing the flaws in their own reasoning.

- Givers, takers and matchers: on sales revenue, medical-school grades and engineering productivity, "the givers outperform the takers". They also risk underperforming, by burning out or being exploited by takers. Smart givers who manage their boundaries gain purpose, trust and learning, and helping others "actually makes you better at solving your organization's problems".
- Why takers still rise: they claim credit, so they are more visible than givers, and "we confuse their confidence with competence". Promoting on individual results rewards them; ask instead "do you make other people better". Corning Fellows get "a job for life and a lab for Life" only if they lead a patent worth at least $100 million and are supporting authors on other people's patents. Few takers fake help for years to become "author 34 on your patent".
- Think like a scientist, not a preacher, prosecutor or politician: opinions are "hypotheses waiting to be tested" and decisions are "experiments", so pilot or A/B test the alternatives. Leaders resist because they were promoted for being right. Tangen: good investors are "pretty stubborn" but "able to change their mind when the facts change", an agility he sees in Stan Druckenmiller.
- The root bias is the "I'm not biased bias". The higher you score on IQ tests, the more likely you are to fall for it: "the very people who are the best at reasoning are the worst at seeing the flaws in their own reasoning". The fix is confident humility: "I don't know yet but I believe I can figure it out".
- Read the data before judging: Amy Edmondson found that more psychologically safe hospital teams had "higher error rates". It was a reporting bias: those teams admitted mistakes, so they could detect and prevent them.
- Culture runs on stories. Pixar still retells how, in the 1980s, the board demanded two names to lay off by the next morning and co-founders Ed Catmull and Alvy Ray Smith wrote their own. The best stories show junior people upholding values "when senior leaders are not looking". In a crisis leaders should be "carriers of calm" (Brené Brown's point), and their jokes "self-deprecating not other deprecating".
- Work habits: he works \~60 hours a week, down from \~100 a decade ago, when he was "less creative". Returns diminish as hours push toward 50–70, and working nights and weekends lowers intrinsic motivation. Teams together "at least half the week" show higher productivity, satisfaction and retention; fully remote makes culture and tacit knowledge hard to build.
- Judge potential by distance travelled, not starting ability: we over-admire prodigies and underrate late bloomers. Grant, a diver with no natural talent, made the Junior Olympic Nationals twice; his coach said "you got further with less Talent than any diver I've ever coached". His advice: "don't judge your potential by your initial abilities".
