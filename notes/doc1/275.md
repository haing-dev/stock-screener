### 275. Adena Friedman — Nasdaq from market operator to fintech provider: listings, dark pools and anti-fraud data (NBIM, Dec 2024)

*CEO of Nasdaq since 2017 (joined as an intern 31 years earlier; CFO of Carlyle for three years through its IPO); interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hRC8SZ2n4Jc)

**Takeaway:** Nasdaq is turning from an exchange into a technology partner to banks, brokers and other exchanges, built on liquidity, transparency and integrity. It scaled by buying best-in-class specialists such as Verafin and selling them into its tier-1 bank relationships.

- Strategy: Nasdaq is "going from a market operator to a technology partner", aiming to be "the trusted fabric to the world's Financial system". Tangen notes the stock exchange is now "the division where you make the least money".
- Footprint: it runs the Nordic and Baltic exchanges (all but Norway, lost to a rival that took "a less friendly approach") and supplies trading technology to 130 markets from Sweden.
- The 2017 strategy review asked three things: which technology trends will reshape the industry over a decade, which trends will reshape clients, and what clients think Nasdaq is good and bad at. Banks named regulatory risk, capital risk and "managing criminals out of our networks".
- How it bought Verafin: Nasdaq screened every anti-financial-crime vendor and "came out with one name". Verafin was not for sale, grew "20 plus percent a year" and had \~2,200 clients, but no tier-1 or tier-2 banks, which is where Nasdaq was strong. The Adenza deal added Calypso (risk management) and AxiomSL (regulatory reporting).
- The data moat: US law lets banks share some data with a "neutral third party" but not with each other. Nasdaq's consortium covers 2,500 banks and about a billion transactions a week, and AI on that pool gives "fewer false positives".
- Size of the problem: \~$3tn is laundered and half a trillion dollars lost to fraud globally each year; US fraud losses of $150bn a year cost 50 basis points of GDP growth.
- Why listings move to the US: "it's not so much the ADR that's coming but the primary listing is moving". The draw is the most liquid market, "the most Diversified investor base" (retail, institutions, hedge funds) and deep research coverage. Sweden is Europe's exception: 37% of people hold domestic equities directly, helped by pensions and tax-advantaged savings accounts.
- Nasdaq (founded 1971) let young companies list "before they were profitable" when NYSE rules kept them out. Its first class (Comcast, Applied Materials, Intel, Paccar) is still in the Nasdaq-100. Companies listed small and stayed, so its listed market cap is now "neck and neck" with NYSE ("we're 30 trillion"). Amazon listed at a $300 million value.
- Public vs private: cyclically, the question is whether public investors are "ready to underwrite risk" as rates change; structurally, it is "the burden of being a public company". Nasdaq Private Market gives private companies periodic liquidity in their shares, open only to qualified or institutional buyers.
- Market structure: high-frequency traders are "the modern market makers", keeping a bid-offer spread available at all times. About 50% of European and 45% of US trading happens in dark pools and internalizers. Nasdaq competes with them for order flow but also sells and operates their trading systems (coopetition).
- Concentration in a few mega caps is "the natural progression" of a tech-driven economy, and 5,000 companies are still listed on Nasdaq.
- Leading at scale: the move from manager to leader is from directing people to empowering them to be better at their jobs than you. The CEO sets the vision and capital allocation and picks "a few projects" to join personally. Nasdaq has \~8,500 staff, about half technologists.
- Hiring screen: confidence with humility, curiosity, expertise as "table Stakes", and kindness, because "relationships break ties" even in routing decisions. From taekwondo: "every offensive move requires a defensive move".
