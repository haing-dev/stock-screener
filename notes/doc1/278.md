### 278. Annie Duke — When to quit, kill criteria and judging decisions rather than outcomes (NBIM, Feb 2024)

*Former professional poker player (retired 2012), trained in cognitive science and author of the book Quit, in a bonus episode with Nicolai Tangen.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zgQmaScqFiY)

**Takeaway:** People quit too late because culture and a stack of biases (sunk cost, endowment) all push toward continuing. The fixes: treat holding as buying, commit to kill criteria before you start, and judge decisions by expected value rather than outcomes; in teams, make intuition explicit and collect views independently before discussing them.

- Only continue what you would start fresh today. If a stock bought at 50 now trades at 40 and you wouldn't buy it at 40, sell it, because "holding is the same as buying". Waste is a forward-looking question (is the next dollar positive expected value?), not a look back at what was spent.
- Failure is either stopping something still worth doing because it is hard, or "failing to stop something that is no longer worthwhile". The real failure with the stock is holding it instead of moving the $40 into something that will win.
- Kill criteria: when a thesis's signals fail to appear, investors often add ("now it's really cheap"). Before starting, write down the signals and commit to actions. Example: if you bought Bitcoin in 2021 as a hedge, decide in advance what correlation with a falling market, over what period, makes you sell. Everest climbers turn around at 1 p.m. wherever they are on the mountain.
- Resulting (outcome bias): Pete Carroll's pass call in the 2015 Super Bowl was intercepted and is called one of the worst ever, yet on base rates "it's not even close": it was the better choice. One losing stock says little; judging someone's decisions needs enough data points, such as a diversified portfolio over a durable period.
- Make intuition explicit: a VC who says "I know a good founder when I see one" should write down what a good founder and a good investment look like. The rubric cuts noise and bias, trains the team faster, and records what was thought at the time, so results can be judged fairly later.
- Collect judgments independently, in a "nominal group" (rubric scores, forecasts) before any discussion: "probably the single biggest thing you can do in order to improve decision-making". The accuracy gains sit in the gaps between people's models of the same facts. When asking advice, don't reveal your view or the outcome.
- Poker's lesson: luck breaks the feedback loop, so players blame losses on luck and credit wins to skill, because people want a positive self-narrative now. Sort results into earned reward, bad luck, dumb luck and "what you deserve", and examine decisions win or lose.
- Model facts, don't just check them: "58% of the people who died of covid were vaccinated" (one August) survives a fact check, but with over 80% of the population vaccinated and age matched, vaccination was five times better. Her advice: only luck and decision quality shape a life, and only the second is yours to control.
