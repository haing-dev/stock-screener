### 279. Anthony Bolton — Popularity is risk: contrarian investing, sentiment signals and a bet on China (NBIM, Feb 2025)

*Former Fidelity fund manager who ran UK Special Situations and spent four years in Hong Kong running a China fund; Nicolai Tangen calls him "the father of contrarian investing".* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37txX2TANKQ)

**Takeaway:** Look where others aren't: judge what the price already discounts, and read sentiment (shareholder lists, insider trades, charts) as well as fundamentals. In Feb 2025 his contrarian trade was to buy China and sell America.

- "popularity is risk and conversely unpopularity is opportunity". He felt uncomfortable owning what everyone owned, and the best opportunities were the uncomfortable ones: "by the time it was comfortable it was late". It takes conviction, patience and a temper that takes success and failure the same.
- Don't ask whether the outlook is bright or poor. Ask "how your view of the future Compares with what's discounted in the stock market", and don't underestimate the market's ability to discount.
- He looks at the chart first: it shows whether he is early or late, and a long-running winner usually means late. He screened for stocks that hadn't done well, especially where management, the portfolio of businesses or the environment was changing for the better. Some were just "dogs".
- Shareholder lists (UK small and mid caps): if many funds he rated already owned a stock, he was late. None could mean a great find or a terrible business. Best was one rated fund already in, leaving room for others to come later.
- Weighing vs voting: most investors only weigh the fundamentals. He spent his effort on the voting, measuring sentiment through ownership and insider dealing; insider trades such as CEO buying could confirm or conflict with his view.
- Charts confirm or veto: a confirming chart could justify a bigger position, and one that "started to roll down" was a reason to cut. He built positions in steps, starting at 0.5% and going to 1%, 2% and, for a few, 4%.
- He liked asymmetric returns: downside protected by "a very strong balance sheet or a business with very good cash flows", upside from something like a drug discovery or an oil well that could transform the valuation.
- Seek the counterview: if you're long, find out why the shorts are short. In one meeting with a hedge fund short of an oil company he owned, "after 10 minutes it just hit me like a flash that they were right and we were wrong".
- Being right 55% of the time is great. What separates good managers from bad is "not having the losers", so cut them. A record under three years may be luck. He had three bad years in a row (\~1989–91) and Fidelity stuck with him.
- Holdings rose in bull markets; in bear markets he pruned and concentrated. Spend at least a quarter of your time on new ideas, since big funds drift into defending what they own. UK Special Situations was once five times the size of the next-biggest UK mutual fund, so he had to buy while prices were still low, before the turn.
- Passive and trend-following money make trends last longer, but "dumb money" still creates big anomalies: "the correction comes later but when it comes it's bigger". Committees rarely help, because "by the time everyone agrees" you catch the tail end.
- Feb 2025: the most contrarian trade was to buy China, near its low after a three-year bear market, and sell America, loved and expensive after \~20% returns two years running. "divide the journey from the arrival": Trump's inauguration ended the US journey, and the day the tariffs came could be China's turning point.
- China case: the authorities want the market higher, bond yields are record low, property is out and gold has already run, leaving "really one other place they can go to": equities. He doubts AI's gains will stay with a handful of US stocks, and sees crypto as "one of the signs of excess".
