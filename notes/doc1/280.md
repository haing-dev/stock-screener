### 280. Arvind Krishna — IBM's turnaround, the AI capex math, software after agents and quantum by 2029 (NBIM, May 2026)

*Chairman and CEO of IBM (CEO since 2020; 35+ years at the company), interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on IBM's reinvention, its AI and quantum bets and his leadership.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IxqrSXiRja4)

**Takeaway:** Krishna rebuilt IBM around growth: he skipped a costly catch-up in public cloud, bought Red Hat, spun off a shrinking services arm and kept the mainframe growing. He thinks the AI data-center build-out is ahead of the revenue it needs, that the largest models will be commodities, and that AI will cut the value of front-end, seat-based software while systems of record keep theirs.

- Mix (2026): hybrid cloud and AI software is almost half of revenue, consulting about a third and hardware about 20%. His diagnosis on taking over: IBM was trusted but seen as "part of the past, not necessarily the future", so he backed what becomes big revenue over many years, not in a month, a quarter or a year.
- Build vs buy (2017): catching up in public cloud would have cost "5 to 10 billion a year", and "if you think at the end of 5 years you're still going to be number five, that doesn't seem like a worthwhile investment". He partnered with all the big clouds and bought Red Hat, judging software M&A "probably a better return". His test: technology trends, rivals' technical strength, whether the capex earns its ROI, and whether another use of capital pays more.
- Spinning off IT services (now Kyndryl, a third of the workforce): with growth essential, a unit "declining at 5%" meant "everything else would have to grow at 10, not at five". He called it "fundamentally deflationary" and decent "but not high margin", a business where clients "look for stability, not innovation", that could "unlock more value for its investors" on its own.
- Acquisition playbook (Red Hat, Confluent): leave engineering free to build, fully integrate go-to-market to sell through IBM's wider country footprint, and fold in back-office functions (HR, legal, contracts, treasury) ideally "on day minus one". Red Hat keeps its own engineering because of its open-source scale; Confluent's Kafka backbone makes data real-time for AI.
- Culture: his best move was making IBM willing to take risk. In decline, people survive by "not raising my head", so he tells staff "Don't give me a 90% confidence. Give me a 50% confidence." and builds in a buffer; a 10–15% yearly staff refresh is healthy. COVID let him "do in 1 year what may have taken 3 to 4 otherwise". His self-named mistake: slow expansion beyond large clients ("B to large B").
- AI capex math (2026): \~1 GW of data center needs $60–80bn of chips, so the over 100 GW committed is a $6–8tn build-out. On a 5–7-year payback that needs an extra $1–2tn of revenue a year, and "that much incremental revenue I don't believe is there". Half today's spending "completely makes sense"; at double, some won't earn "a great return". "Some will disappoint. Many will thrive."
- Models: the largest will be "a commodity" with "low switching cost", able to earn a margin but not one "with a massive moat"; perhaps two or three builders survive, not "a half dozen to a dozen". In consumer AI, firms whose existing distribution is aligned to AI should win; enterprise is "wide open".
- Adoption speed: computers took \~20 years to be embraced (1950s–70s), PCs \~10 (from \~1980) and the internet \~5 (1995–2000). Each wave was faster, but AI still takes years: "It's not yet months". He puts AI in its "second innings" of nine, 3–4 years from wide use, "bigger than mobile and it's bigger than cloud" and in the internet's category, not the "10 times the internet" some claim.
- Watson lesson: winning Jeopardy (2011) proved the technology, but IBM built monolithic applications in "the hardest" vertical, health; starting with customer care and enterprise documents, "we would have been 5 years ahead". That AI was "bespoke" (one use case, one corpus); today's models handle many uses and absorb new data without relearning.
- IBM's AI stance: it rents chips from the big clouds and sources frontier models rather than building them, since switching costs will be low. Its small open-weight Granite models (none over 100bn parameters) are cheaper to run and can sit on premises for data-sovereignty needs; the target is enterprise work such as procurement and accounts payable, for clients that will be "multi-model".
- Software sell-off (2026): Tangen noted IBM fell 13% when Anthropic's Claude Code ("cloud code" in captions) was released; many software stocks were down 40–60% and IBM \~25%. Krishna: fewer workers shrink "seat-based software" and AI agents "will replace some of the front end of much software", but the system of record holding the business logic stays valuable. He guesses a fourth of firms (value mainly in the front end) face long-term harm and maybe half neither help nor hurt; unable to tell which, investors "take the sector down" until reported numbers sort them out.
- Mainframe: it shrank in the 10 years before he became CEO and has grown every year for the 6 since. Its systems-of-record workloads (retail banking, card authorizations, airline reservations) need "six to nine nines" of availability, and the cloud would cost "three times as much". IBM put AI on it in 2021; the z17 runs fraud models in line, up to 450 billion inferences a day with its Spyre card, and has post-quantum cryptography.
- Quantum: he is "100" confident of quantum computers by 2029 (useful in production \~2028–30), needing 10x more scale and 10x better error correction. First uses: materials (coatings, drugs, fertilizer, magnets), pricing complex instruments in milliseconds, and routing (30% of truck miles and containers run empty). IBM is "a couple of years ahead" of IonQ, Quantinuum and Pasqal, and quantum could be worth "Hundreds of billions" to it: it is where GPUs were \~2015, \~7 years before demand outran supply.
- Security and geopolitics: AI lets attackers exploit flaws in seconds rather than months, and "some of the smaller vendors may not have the capability to defend". He doubts AI technology can be regulated across borders, only its uses. China's top-down quantum push will "catch up or be in the race", and Shor's algorithm (breaking most of today's encryption) makes quantum a national-security issue.
