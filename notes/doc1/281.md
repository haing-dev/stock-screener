### 281. Bruce Flatt — Brookfield's three D's and why private ownership wins (NBIM, Dec 2023)

*CEO of Brookfield, an alternative asset manager with $850bn under management, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vTd02-0BiOM)

**Takeaway:** Flatt invests behind three megatrends (decarbonization, deglobalization, digitalization) through private funds that own infrastructure, power and property. He argues private owners can ignore quarterly earnings, and that the best vintages come when banks are constrained and money is scarce, as he saw it in Dec 2023.

- Decarbonization runs on cost, not virtue: wind and solar are now "the lowest cost energy" in almost every country. Ten years ago most of Brookfield's renewable output went to the grid; now most goes to corporates, much of it to tech companies.
- Brookfield calls itself the world's largest builder of solar plants and owns Westinghouse; Flatt sees no full transition without nuclear. Its first transition fund raised $15bn, the second will be larger, and it "targets 15% returns" compounding over 10–15 years.
- Infrastructure was 1–2% (or zero) of institutional portfolios 25 years ago, is \~10% today and should reach 15–20%: long-lived, low-risk assets that compound. With $1m as an individual he'd buy infrastructure and "compound for the next 25 years at 14%".
- Inflation hedge: infrastructure income "gets adjusted by CPI", so with fixed-rate financing revenue rises while interest costs stay flat. Many of Brookfield's businesses are therefore "positively disposed to inflation".
- Property is where higher rates bite, but much of it carries long-term mortgages, and "it's the coupon that's that's relevant not the treasury rate". Spreads were very high at the bottom and have narrowed, so the all-in rate is "not that far off" (Dec 2023).
- "good real estate today is really great and bad real estate is terrible": top space is sought after at high rents (Brookfield has no spare room for its own office among 30m sq ft in New York), while poor space in the wrong cities is "very bad".
- Deglobalization is supply-chain diversification. Top-end chips and vaccines move back to the US (Brookfield is building an Intel fab in Arizona with Intel), the rest shifts to Vietnam and above all India. On balance it adds "a little more inflation".
- Europe is "a value market it's not a growth Market" (low population growth, little immigration, harsher bankruptcy laws), so know your entry point and don't overpay.
- Rates (Dec 2023): "interest rates are still very low"; they look high only against two years earlier, which was the anomaly, and may come down a little. Brookfield's private-market returns run from 10% at the low end to 25% at the high end.
- Private beats public for long projects. Brookfield plans to take private an Australian power distributor (2 million homes, coal and gas generation) and spend $20bn over 10 years building renewables and shutting the coal and gas plants. A $6bn market cap company could not do "a$2 billion Equity issue" every year for 10 years.
- Alignment: a fund is typically 20% Brookfield balance-sheet money and 80% partners'. Investors get an 8% hurdle return before Brookfield shares in the upside. Co-investments carry much less or no fee: in a recent payments deal, 30% came from the fund and 70% as co-investment.
- Timing: 2021 was the wrong time because "money was free". With banks constrained, deal activity low and fundraising hard, "the vintages starting right now for the next 24 months will be excellent vintages" (Dec 2023). Negative headlines are good news.
- On buying cheaply: "you're never sure what's cheap", so Brookfield keeps gathering information on whether it is "different this time". Once in, keep conviction: "often the best investments are ones where they continue to go down".
- Screen implication: for hard-asset owners, check for CPI-linked revenue and fixed-rate debt; for property below book, location and quality decide whether the discount is a real floor.
