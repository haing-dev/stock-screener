### 282. Dev Kantesaria — Compounding machines, no healthcare, and free cash flow yield against the 10-year (The Intrinsic Value Podcast, Oct 2021)

*Managing partner of Valley Forge Capital Management (\~$2.7bn, Oct 2021), a Harvard Medical School graduate who spent \~18 years in venture capital before 14 years of concentrated public-equity investing; interviewed by Trey Lockerbie (TIP389).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=csn-II3Xg9c)

**Takeaway:** Own monopoly and duopoly "compounding machines" with pricing power and low capital needs, keep a current intrinsic value on each, and buy fast when one drops. Value them by the free cash flow yield they deserve, after the cost of stock pay, against the 10-year Treasury.

- One playbook: monopolies or duopolies with pricing power, capital efficiency and margins that rise over time, held for years. He says 98% of value peers chase cigar butts, like a washing-machine maker at a PE of 12 that may be losing money three years later. Energy, financials, heavy industrials, chemicals and materials are "for the most part value traps".
- "stock screens just don't work" for him because the advantages are "subtle". His holdings share a high return on tangible assets, very low capex and long records of pricing power, but screening for high operating margins and return on tangible assets only narrows the list. 95% of his time goes on reading 10-Ks and 10-Qs.
- He updates an intrinsic value (plus or minus $5 a share) on favourites every quarter, so he can act within days. Monster Beverage fell 40% in a few days on a health scare: "Great businesses don't go on sale very often". Market neglect, a stock trailing the market, is the other entry. He wants one to three great ideas a year.
- He buys at 30–60% below value, then keeps adding: some holdings were bought on 10 separate occasions over a decade. Cash was once as high as 20% and is now in the low single digits (Oct 2021): "there really is no bad time to buy a compounding machine".
- 2020 was the first real test. He added no new names and sold nothing (he bought more of existing holdings), and several holdings reported record margins in "the worst GDP downdraft since the great depression". Of the fund's 27% gain, 6 points came from a large COVID short in March 2020, one of \~5 shorts in 14 years, built on what doctor friends told him.
- No healthcare at all, despite medical school and 18 years of biotech VC. Pharma, biotech, devices, hospitals and healthcare IT lack "a very predictable path to organic growth and higher free cash flow" over 5–10 years. He also avoids hardware tech, mines (a $10bn potash mine built without knowing the potash price when it opens five years later), and businesses tied to commodity prices or interest rates or needing heavy capital.
- He wants essential products, a long record of pricing power, a dominant position unlikely to be disrupted, rising industry volumes and "many different levers to win". Such companies "take many years to get off the rails", so there are no 90% collapses and there is time to switch.
- Moody's and S&P Global, held 10+ years and up \~20–25x from their lows in 12 years, are a "natural duopoly". 90% of the world's debt carries both ratings, and an issuer that uses Kroll, Morningstar or Egan-Jones pays 30–50bp more, so rivals can't win even for free. They price at inflation plus several percent, and costly regulation has solidified the duopoly.
- Valuation is a DCF, but the edge is the discount rate, not an extra 2–3% of growth. In practice he ranks companies by the free cash flow yield they deserve, then compares it with today's yield and the 10-year Treasury. Moody's has traded at 5–6% (perhaps 10% 10–12 years earlier) when he thought it deserved \~3%.
- Stock pay: issuing 1–2% of the company in stock and buying back 1–2% is "just running in place". He computes free cash flow yields after the after-tax cost of buying back the shares issued as pay.
- His error was shunning the FANGs for poor capital allocation, excessive pay, pet projects and dual-class shares: "it's been a mistake not to own these companies", because organic growth "more than covered for these sins". He owns Amazon (Oct 2021).
- Record: 39 winners and six losers (positions over $10,000, 14 years). The worst, in 2010, cost \~4% of gross performance: American Dairy, a US-listed Chinese infant-formula maker bought at a single-digit multiple after the melamine scandal. Lesson: like the US's 500 car makers that became three, he "got involved in that battle too early".
- He sells when a stock reaches 100% of intrinsic value and can't justify its place, when quality or competition changes, or when management misuses cash or takes on excessive debt. Most often he simply finds something better, and sold names (Monster, Nike) kept rising.
- Macro (Oct 2021): ignore historical P/Es and follow the S&P 500 earnings yield (\~5% for next year) against the 10-year Treasury (1.4%). He expected Japan- or Europe-style 1–2% GDP growth, a 1–3% 10-year yield, and automation and AI pressuring jobs, making predictable growers "prized assets".
