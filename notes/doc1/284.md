### 284. Rob Vinall — Business Owner Fund investor meeting: people first, then runway, then price (Fully Allocated, Jan 2017)

*Founder and sole manager of RV Capital's Business Owner Fund, taking investor questions at the fund's 2017 meeting in Engelberg, Switzerland, after a short talk by the CFO of holding Bergbahnen Engelberg-Trübsee-Titlis (Titlis cable cars).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdA3BMtB6OU)

**Takeaway:** Vinall buys trustworthy people and a long growth runway first and price last: "the multiple per se is not what's providing the margin of safety", the quality of people and business is. He tries to disprove each idea, sells on disconfirming evidence rather than on price, and keeps a concentrated, unlevered fund.

- Four criteria: understand the business (a quick filter), honest and talented management, a great business earning good returns for a long time, and an attractive price. His starting hypothesis is "yes" on each, and the work is to disprove it, e.g. how management behaved and communicated in 2009. A CEO with only a couple of years in the job leaves too little data, unless picked by a long-serving predecessor.
- Price: "if you're wrong you're wrong", and a low-PE mistake is no less painful; a low-quality company's earnings can vanish, and leverage deepens the loss. He would rather pay a little more for something he is sure of, because certainty lets him buy when everyone panics.
- Facebook (bought a few months before, \~25x earnings): a small premium to the market on this year's earnings, with WhatsApp and partly Instagram not yet monetized and VR and AI spending depressing profits. "you don't want to take a negative view of a company because it's investing for long term".
- Valuation is relative, not an absolute cap: few stocks he follows trade much below 20x earnings, and most of those grow at GDP at best or are cyclical (Jan 2017). Cash is not risk-free (he was in India in Nov 2016 when 500 and 1,000 rupee notes became worthless), and top-rated long government bonds yield close to zero.
- Trupanion (US pet medical insurance): \~1% of US pets are insured vs closer to 30% in the UK or Sweden. Bought over a year earlier at about half the price ($7 vs $16), it is his highest-upside, lowest-certainty holding. If it fails, he will take it as a sign to wait for more mature companies.
- Novo Nordisk was \~13–14% of the fund at the start of 2016 and nearly halved in a year the market rose \~10%, yet the fund was about flat. With no leverage and no redemptions he rode it out and added substantially in 2016; he won't add more now because he doesn't want to be "too concentrated".
- Novo's moat: insulin is a large molecule, so copies are biosimilars rather than identical generics. Switching needs a doctor, a new prescription and slow titration, so it is "much stickier" and more "forgiving" of a failure to innovate. The case rests on whether its insulins and GLP-1s stay better, not on US pricing questions.
- Capital allocation: Novo returns \~100% of its cash, half as dividends and half through buybacks a mandated bank runs daily with no management timing. Credit Acceptance is better: "they wait wait and wait and when the share price is cheap they retire huge amounts of stock".
- Selling: mainly when evidence disproves the thesis; he sold Baidu in 2016 over how it handled its medical search business. He won't sell a winner just because it got expensive ("I'll take that problem"), since on good businesses earnings estimates tend to be "very wrong in the right direction".
- Moats: a widening moat matters "way more" than a wide one, because one eroding 5% a year is largely gone in \~10 years. Few unchanging businesses remain (Titlis, \~3% owned for \~5 years, is one), and avoiding fast-growing tech makes beating the market harder because share prices follow earnings.
- Size: Google grew \~20% a year when he bought it \~5 years earlier and has not slowed. Among German small caps he "can scarcely think of" any growing sustainably above 15% a year except Grenke Leasing (15–20%).
- Management "dirty secret": face to face with thousands of CEOs he would do no better than 50-50, so he limits himself to obvious cases: founders and decades-long tenures with a clean record (Wolfgang Grenke). Best owner: an owner-operator who shields the company from market short-termism; second best: a strong outside owner such as the Novo Foundation.
- Red flag: Robin Li's bid, about a year earlier, to buy Baidu's video business himself. If the price is low he short-changes shareholders; if high, "the guy's an idiot to be buying it". He got comfortable because it looked born of frustration, and the bid was withdrawn.
- Crises: he expects one without knowing when, but holding all cash would be "unbelievably stupid" and gold is a poor long-term store. He owns firms with no debt (industrials) or far less than peers (financials) that can "Hoover up market share"; in 2009 German companies cut print and TV ads but spent more on search.
