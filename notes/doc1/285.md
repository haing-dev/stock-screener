### 285. Cal Newport — Deep work, the "hyperactive hive mind" and slow productivity (NBIM, May 2024)

*Georgetown computer-science professor and author of Deep Work, Digital Minimalism and Slow Productivity; Nicolai Tangen asks him how to protect focus, tame email and manage workloads.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PCDgLqd53ew)

**Takeaway:** Valuable knowledge work needs long stretches of undistracted focus, and the main enemy at work is the "hyperactive hive mind" of constant ad hoc messaging. Fix the system, not the schedule: fewer projects at once, transparent workloads, scheduled communication and practised concentration.

- Deep work, full concentration on a hard task with no distraction, is "the number one way you produce valuable things using the human brain". The brain needs 10–15 minutes to lock in (later he says "10 to 20 minutes"), which is why hard work feels hard until "a new gear kicks in". Book it on the calendar like a meeting; mornings work best for most people, so he would push meetings to the afternoon.
- Quick checks are almost as bad as multitasking. One glance at an inbox sets off the brain's alarm that the tribe needs you, leaving "partial continuous attention" (Linda Stone's term). By the afternoon people only answer easy emails, "your brain crying Uncle"; the exhaustion plus the sense of not doing real work adds up to burnout.
- The root cause is the "hyperactive hive mind": seven projects each generating unscheduled back-and-forth messages force constant checking. An instant Slack question or CC-all is "useful to you in the moment but it creates a macrocognitive environment that is intolerable for the human brain". Fix it at the source: a separate channel and a set time for each type of communication (e.g. supply-chain issues with international distributors), not one mixed inbox per person.
- Slow productivity replaces "pseudo productivity", activity as a proxy for useful effort. Every project brings its own emails and meetings: "if I say yes to 10 things I have 10 projects worth of administrative overhead". Work on two at a time, finish them fast and well, then pull in the next.
- For managers, "workload management has to be transparent": one visible list of the team's work, explicit assignments, a cap per person (two at once in his example) and visible status. A four-day week or a hybrid policy treats the symptoms, "not getting to the underlying disease" of overload. Add "small seasonality": no meetings on Fridays, or a lighter week after a hard month.
- Focus is trained like a muscle. His "productive meditation": walk with one professional problem and pull your attention back each time it wanders. He did it daily as an MIT postdoc: "it really was like a superpower". Bill Gates's think weeks work the same way; one in the 1990s produced his Internet Tidal Wave memo, when he saw Microsoft was about to miss the biggest trend since the PC.
- Spotting deep thinkers: their hallmark is "originality and Clarity of thinking", seeing how pieces fit and finding the interesting angle. Tangen's test: a dinner neighbour with clear, different ideas has probably done the thinking alone. Advice to the young: "respect your attention" rather than renting it to apps.
