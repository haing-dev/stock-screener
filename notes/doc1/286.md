### 286. Christel Heydemann — Orange: Europe's fragmented telecoms, deflationary prices and the 10% ceiling on diversification (NBIM, Aug 2025)

*Chief executive of Orange (23% owned by the French state); an engineer whose career ran through BCG, Alcatel-Lucent (15 years) and Schneider; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m22U6mS4oCs)

**Takeaway:** European telecom is a capital-intensive scale business split into national markets of three operators each, so cost per customer is high, prices keep falling and regulation caps returns. Heydemann wants consolidation, is trimming investment, and sees diversification beyond connectivity stuck at \~10% of revenue.

- The core problem: telecom created huge value while mobile spread, but "demography in western world is uh flat if not declining". The question is how to keep investing in a capital-intensive network "while creating value in mature markets".
- Fragmentation: Europe has roughly 27 countries with three players each, versus three in the much bigger US. Orange has \~20 million customers in most of its countries and many operators have fewer than 5 million, against China Mobile's 1 billion. China Mobile buys mobile sites by the million, Orange by the thousand, so "the cost of running the network per customer is actually more expensive in Europe". 5G rolls out faster in the US and China.
- Pricing: France has "the cheapest mobile data packages" in Europe, "much cheaper" than the US. Energy, water and food prices rose in recent years, but "That's not the case in telecom", where prices are "frankly deflationary".
- Stress signs: some operators sell their fixed or mobile networks in "a kind of sell and lease back model because of the pressure on their cash flow" and their debt. The long-term risks are under-investment and less resilient networks.
- Regulation was built to open the market to competition. Even wholesale infrastructure prices are regulated, using a regulatory cost of capital that differs from what investors require ("a parallel world of regulation"). A single EU telecom market, as in the Draghi report, is unlikely because states won't give up control of spectrum or security.
- Big tech: platforms monetize data, so their model needs more traffic, and "more traffic on our networks means more investment" for operators. Orange's answer: "we're trying to reduce investments" without lowering quality.
- Capex cycle: "I don't think we're going to see 6G". Instead come 5.5 and 5.6, a "progressive upgrade" of existing 5G through software rather than a big-bang hardware renewal.
- AI traffic: AI keeps lifting traffic volumes, and Orange helped create a GSMA "observatory of AI traffic". Real-time voice translation (demoed at Mobile World Congress) needs very low latency; at scale it will take "a few years but it's not decades".
- Vendors: some European countries bar Chinese 5G equipment, and Orange uses Chinese vendors more in Africa. From her years at Alcatel-Lucent, Chinese kit is "actually cheaper" and "more efficient from an energy standpoint"; European vendors lack China's home-market scale. With one Chinese and two European mobile vendors, avoiding single-sourcing is hard.
- Digital sovereignty means "strategic autonomy" built on four Cs: control (know your supply chain), choice (including reversibility, which is hard with cloud and AI tools), competence (skills) and scale.
- Diversification: Orange tried to create a bank and pulled back; it prefers to distribute others' products. It keeps infrastructure many telcos sold (submarine cables, cable-repair vessels) and sells cybersecurity, home security, energy and insurance. Even at the most advanced telcos diversification "limits to 10% of our total" turnover; B2B cloud, AI and cybersecurity are the main bets.
- AI inside Orange: 70,000 employees use AI tools every day. Adoption must be "very decentralized", in the hands of managers, and is slowed by data quality, silos and technical debt.
- Culture: the civil-servant heritage brings devotion (staff in Mayotte repaired the network after losing their homes) but a habit to "manage people more equally". She tells leaders "don't tell me what you think I want to hear", pushes "boldness" and open talk about failures, and keeps Fridays free of meetings.
