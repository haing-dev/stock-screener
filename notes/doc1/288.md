### 288. Daniel Pink — Regret, motivation and timing: judge the decision, not the outcome (NBIM, Dec 2024)

*Author of seven New York Times bestsellers, including The Power of Regret, Drive and When; Nicolai Tangen asks him about regret, motivation and pay, hiring and the science of timing.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qvy9oAldcxY)

**Takeaway:** Judge decisions on what you knew at the time, and use regret instead of banishing it: people mostly regret the chances they didn't take. Motivate with autonomy and progress rather than complex if-then rewards, hire on evidence rather than unstructured interviews, and plan the day by time of day.

- Judge a decision on what you knew at the time, not on how it turned out: a $2 million bet on a crypto fund that worked may still have been a bad decision, and a smart one may not have paid off yet. "you have to scrutinize the decision itself rather than the outcome". Tangen links this to Howard Marks; Pink to Annie Duke.
- His regret survey covers \~26,000 people in 134 countries, who share four core regrets. Foundation: small early choices that compound ("I spent too much and saved too little") and arrive "gradually then suddenly". Boldness: "overwhelmingly people regret not taking the chance", far more than those who took one and failed; his own law degree was a "quintessential Act of timidity". Moral: doing the wrong thing. Connection: relationships that drift apart without drama.
- Keep a "portfolio theory of emotions": mostly positive, but not only. To CEOs who claim to have no fear gene: "you want a little fear". Upward counterfactuals ("if only", the silver medallist) feel worse but help you do better; downward ones ("at least", the bronze medallist) console. Writing or talking about a regret turns a "Blobby" feeling into words you can analyse, and admitting mistakes often makes people "actually think more of us".
- Motivation: if-then rewards work for simple, short-horizon tasks but less well for creative, long-horizon ones. The biggest day-to-day motivator is "making progress" in meaningful work (Teresa Amabile, Harvard Business School). Control makes people "comply or they defy". Tangen: control for five years and "you got the wrong people left in The Firm"; Pink: the rest leave, so you get "an army of compliant people". Be a macromanager instead (set direction, hold people accountable, give them tools); the billable hour is "absolutely destructive of autonomy".
- Pay in finance: hire great people, pay "a healthy base salary", and add bonuses on simple, straightforward criteria that are hard to game. Many schemes are so complex they need "an entire administrative apparatus" plus a "Judicial arm" for disputes. Keep bonuses "simple not high stakes and connected to measures that matter", but if "your portfolio goes up you deserve a piece of the action".
- Hiring: "we're pretty bad at hiring across the board", and there is no evidence that unstructured interviews predict anything. Structured interviews, past performance and test assignments that match the real job have some predictive value. CEOs may rise for being better at politics, tall, male or conformist rather than for stronger drive, and founders and 35-year insiders are "different species".
- Timing: "time of day explains 20% of the variance in performance", and unlike talent or social advantage it is easy to fix. For the 80% who are not night owls: analytic work early, admin in the early-afternoon trough, brainstorming late in the day, when mood rises and vigilance falls. In the trough, anaesthesia errors are "four times more likely" and doctors prescribe more unnecessary antibiotics and opioids; no one in his family may have a discretionary afternoon procedure.
- Breaks are part of the work: "something is better than nothing", "social is better than solo", moving beats sitting, outside beats inside, and leave the phone behind. The best is a 15–20 minute afternoon walk with someone you like, talking about anything but work. Naps under 20 minutes restore without grogginess; his nappuccino is a coffee, then a 25-minute alarm, because caffeine takes about 25 minutes to reach the bloodstream.
