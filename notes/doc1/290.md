### 290. David Epstein — Range: why generalists win in "wicked" environments (NBIM, Jul 2024)

*Author of Range and The Sports Gene and a former science writer at Sports Illustrated; Nicolai Tangen asks him about generalists and specialists, learning, careers, teams and AI.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c8mG6Tsk1fk)

**Takeaway:** Most work now happens in "wicked" environments, where narrow experience breeds confidence more than skill. Broad training, a spell of exploring before exploiting and diverse teams adapt better, and slow, frustrating progress is often a sign of real learning.

- Kind vs wicked learning environments (psychologist Robin Hogarth): where goals are clear, rules fixed, patterns repeat and feedback is quick and accurate, narrow experience reliably makes people better. Where rules change, feedback is delayed or wrong and "work next year may not look like work last year", people "get more confident but not better".
- "breadth of training predicts breadth of transfer", the ability to apply skills to a new problem. Narrow repetition breeds the Einstellung effect: you keep using a solution after it stops working. Specialised surgeons have fewer complications even after adjusting for volume, but are "much more likely to do those procedures when they're unwarranted"; the surgeons he told agreed, but "they all felt it wasn't them".
- Struggle signals learning: "if you're not struggling that's definitely a sign that you're not learning". In a California middle-school trial, mixed (interleaved) maths practice felt slower and students sometimes rated the teacher lower, but on the test it was like "moving the 50th percentile kid to the 80th percentile". In sport, national pipelines show an "inverse relationship" between optimising junior and senior performance.
- Explore before you exploit: Northwestern's Dashun Wang studied (as he recalls) 26,000 careers and found that best work clusters in a "hot streak", and "reliably before a hot streak people spend time in this period of experimentation". Great creators "just create more stuff", and introspection "has been oversold as a method for learning about yourself".
- Founders are older than the myth: research by MIT, the US Census Bureau and Northwestern puts the average founder of a fast-growing tech startup at 45 on the day of founding. Mark Zuckerberg said "young people are just smarter" when he was 22.
- Diverse teams solve unfamiliar problems better because they bring more and broader analogies. Even AI bots "programmed to behave randomly" improved remote teams by knocking people off their first, anchored answer, which is usually not their best; good ideas tend to come later than we think (the creative-cliff illusion).
- Hiring curious people: Baillie Gifford, in his view Scotland's most successful investment company, "would not hire anyone with an MBA" and taught the finance instead ("a little overboard", he says). Serial innovators (Abbie Griffin's research) read widely, connect domains and "often appear to flit among ideas". Managers should "underwrite the risk" of their experiments (3M's and Google's 20% time), and ask, as NASA's first chief knowledge officer Ed Hoffman does, "what's something you're good at that we're not using".
- AI: he uses Claude, Perplexity and ChatGPT daily and finds them "very often wrong and yet very useful". The ATM precedent: when it arrived in the US in the early 1970s, coverage expected the 300,000 bank tellers to be out of work overnight, but cheaper branches meant banks opened more, so over the next 40 years there were more tellers, doing broader work such as customer service and financial advice.
