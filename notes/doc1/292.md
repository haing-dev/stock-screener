### 292. Dev Kantesaria — A 50-company fishing pond: pricing power, no serial acquirers, and playing offense in 2022 (Good Investing Talks, Oct 2022)

*Managing partner of Valley Forge Capital, which he started 15 years earlier with $300,000 and runs with a two-person investment team; interviewed by Tilman Versch on how he defines and buys "compounding machines".* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JCAIHYRNbhw)

**Takeaway:** Only \~50 companies worldwide meet his bar: pricing power, volume growth, rising margins, little capital and R&D risk, and sound capital allocation. He owns 8–12 of them, buys on regulatory or legal scares, and in Oct 2022 was fully invested and "playing offense".

- His edge is temperament ("excruciating patience", which he thinks is partly "genetic and can't be taught") plus judging future risk, which really means choosing the discount rate. Volatility is a poor guide to future risk: Coca-Cola's last 50 years don't guarantee its next 10.
- The "fishing pond" holds only \~50 companies in the world. He owns 8–12 (14 is fine, mid-teens is the most you'll ever see) and is reluctant to buy his 17th-best idea. He is a "historian": he wants decades of proven quality and does not try to predict step changes.
- The checklist: a monopoly or oligopoly with secular volume growth, margins that rise over time even at $50–100bn market caps, light capital needs, and no R&D risk such as spending a billion dollars to find the next blockbuster drug. "pricing power is the hallmark of a great business", meaning prices raised above inflation consistently.
- A quality business needs both volume growth and pricing growth; with only one it can be an okay investment but not a great one. The common error is trading quality for price, like calling a washing-machine maker at a PE of 8 a quality situation.
- Pricing power is rarely disclosed, so he builds the case from indirect hints over many years. The ideal is a cheap, essential must-have that customers grumble about but keep buying, even when prices rise 10% with inflation at 2%.
- One bad trait can make a fine business uninvestable, most often capital allocation. A company planning to grow by buying five companies a year won't end well (he cites Valeant). He likes "offensive acquisitions", penalizes defensive ones, and nets stock pay out of his free cash flow models.
- Entry points come from a bear case: a regulatory issue, a legal threat, a short-term competitive threat or a bad earnings release. The Durbin amendment on debit-card pricing made Visa and Mastercard "tank", and that was his entry. He followed FICO, "the monopoly business in consumer credit scores", for seven years before buying.
- Biggest lesson of 15 years: foreign stocks, even bought at reasonable valuations, were the most work for the least gain. Low-probability but "outrageous" risks hit: a CEO self-dealing to a family entity, new foreign-ownership laws, sudden tax changes. He stays US-centric.
- No pharma, biotech or devices: a drug can be pulled for side effects, and rivals are hard to judge because "a lot of the clinical trial data is private".
- None of his \~50 companies wins on scale economies. He would not own Amazon's low-margin e-commerce (or Walmart) alone. He owns Amazon for AWS, advertising and Prime pricing power, and for the option to become a $10 trillion company. He expected an operating-earnings inflection, \~18 months late (Oct 2022), and never puts optionality in his base case.
- Management matters mainly for capital allocation (excessive stock pay, defensive or overpriced deals), and more in young companies; he wants businesses "your idiot nephew could run". Meeting CEOs is "a double-edged sword", since polished executives bias you upward.
- Margin of safety shows in the win rate. A typical manager has 60% winners and 40% losers; he wants 90%+ of ideas to work, and a concentrated book can't hold stocks that can fall 90%. If you worry about the next earnings release (or a beverage maker's Nielsen volume reports), the business isn't predictable enough.
- Money-losing IPOs and SPACs down 60–80% "are not coming back". The idea that a stock down 80% must bounce is wrong; many of them will never be profitable.
- Oct 2022: under 1% cash, the same 10 holdings as in Jan 2020, "playing offense". The COVID rebound flattered cyclicals and low-quality firms; he expects a "judgment day" as global GDP growth reverts to 0–2%. A 5–7% 10-year yield for a decade would be bearish, but he expects low rates. 2008–09 was "the luckiest thing" of his career, and in a crash "sometimes the best thing to do is to do nothing".
