### 293. Dmitry Balyasny — Inside the multi-strategy, multi-PM platform at BAM (Capital Allocators, Oct 2023)

*Founder, managing partner and CIO of Balyasny Asset Management (BAM), a \~$20bn multi-strategy hedge fund with almost 2,000 staff in over 15 offices; a long interview with Ted Seides on how the platform hires, sizes and allocates.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w6Ay1GWKX3A)

**Takeaway:** BAM hands out capital as volatility budgets with a drawdown plan agreed on day one, then moves risk between strategies as their opportunity sets change. Diversification across strategies and long-dated investor capital, not any single trade, is what carries the firm through crowding unwinds.

- Learn in a small box: his prop-trading training (zero base pay) limited instruments, holding periods and risk, and widened the box only after consistent success. BAM uses the same rule for new PMs and new businesses: "Whenever we start new businesses, we start them small. We figure we're going to be the worst at them when we start."
- Mix (Oct 2023): fundamental long/short equity is still the largest strategy but now "about 45 to 50% of the risk", down from 100% early on and \~85% five years ago. The rest is macro, commodities, equity arbitrage, credit (a couple percent of AUM) and quant, still being built.
- New businesses are blueprinted first. For commodities BAM studied prop firms, merchants, CTAs and hedge funds, skipped physical trading (slow to build) and long-term CTA trading (unattractive Sharpe ratios), then hired into the plan. Hard-to-build areas are a moat: few firms have good commodity businesses "Because it's freaking hard."
- Risk box: capital is allocated in "volatility dollars"; vol dollars × the PM's expected Sharpe = expected P&L. Every PM signs a multi-step drawdown framework on day one. Equity books get idio vs factor limits (e.g. "at least an 80% idio, 20% factor portfolio"); macro and commodity books get historical stress limits.
- Firm level: BAM has averaged \~4–4.5% volatility for about five years, and equity leverage averages around 4x.
- Alpha in long/short comes from earnings and multiples. Aim for estimates that are "30% differentiated for the quarter" and maybe 60% for next year, then judge sentiment and positioning for the multiple. "I got the numbers right, but the stock got killed because the multiple contracted by 50%" is no excuse: "The best investments are ones where you get both of those right."
- Allocation flexes within limits: a PM with a vol allocation of 50 can run 40–60 and can ask for flex capital (e.g. 50 to 75 for three months after a dislocation). Management raised macro from 15% to 25% over the last couple of years but would not take it to 60%, because teams need time to absorb capital.
- Mean reversion vs momentum: in relative-value books (fixed income RV, merger arbitrage) step up when stop-outs blow spreads out, provided you navigated the drawdown well enough "to where you have the ammunition to step up". Growth equity sectors and directional macro are "momentumy": a good environment feeds a good environment.
- Read the environment: the best time is when generalist money flows into a strategy; the worst is when it flows out and specialists are underwater, which brings "cascading drawdown type of periods". Sub-strategy risk can move up or down 50% between the two.
- Specialists rarely get hurt in their own area but by something "rolling through the markets that might have started somewhere else". So equity teams pass on how often companies talk about inflation, and macro teams interpret Fed language for equity teams.
- Contagion fears are overdone: long-biased money (mutual funds, ETFs, long tech funds in 2022) is the bigger correlated risk in dollars. In Q1 2020 RV and merger-arb spreads blew out, yet BAM was positive on the month at the depths of the third week of March: "we never want to be in a position of relying on government intervention to save us."
- Judge PMs against their opportunity set: the best directional macro trader would have struggled 5–7 years ago with zero volatility and zero rates, then made a fortune once rates moved. BAM hires slowly and tells PMs to do the same (often 30–70 interviews, case studies): "if you're going to take 3 days to hire somebody, they'll probably take 3 days to fire somebody."
- Capital stability: early hot money took assets from 300 to 600 and back to 300, and in 2008 from $2.5bn to $1.25bn although BAM made money and did not gate. Capital is now "all two and three-year capital" vs monthly and quarterly five years ago, which lets BAM hire quant PMs with two-year sit-outs. Record: 20 positive years out of 21 with zero correlation.
- Mistake: in 2008 BAM passed on the subprime short (no mortgage or credit traders, worry about investors' reaction) although he and staff had money in Paulson's mortgage short fund. A loss would have cost the fund 1–2%. Lesson: size the downside and take the bet; his pet peeve is analysis paralysis, since "by the time it becomes obvious, usually the opportunity is gone."
