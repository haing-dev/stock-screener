### 294. Eliot Higgins — How Bellingcat finds the truth in public data (NBIM, Jul 2026)

*Founder of Bellingcat, the open-source investigative collective, and a self-taught investigator who dropped out of college; recorded with Nicolai Tangen before nearly 200 NBIM staff.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N8x_BNqwF8k)

**Takeaway:** Bellingcat's edge is method, not secret access: mine public data others ignore, verify before forming a view, and publish fast, because once people think they know the truth they rarely change their minds. Disinformation runs on demand in engagement-driven platforms, and AI now lets people dismiss any inconvenient image as fake.

- Open-source investigation uses public data: social media posts, satellite imagery, marine-traffic and flight data. Bellingcat has \~35 staff (mostly researchers and trainers), \~200 trained volunteers and \~43,000 people on its Discord, with teams on financial investigations, online ideologies and conflict. It has trained investment firms, earns \~30% of its income from workshops, and says US aid cuts wiped out 25–40% of global funding for investigative-journalism NGOs overnight (it took none).
- The core value: "you never prejudge a situation. Just because you feel something should be the truth doesn't mean it actually is the truth." The order is "you do the investigation, you verify information, and then you have your opinion". In 2012 he began geolocating Syrian war videos with satellite imagery to win online arguments; he catalogued the YouTube channels of media centres and armed groups and checked hundreds of videos every day: "no one else had figured out you could do that".
- He launched Bellingcat in July 2014, and "3 days later MH17 was shot down". Volunteers combing every scrap of information found where the missile launcher came from and where it fired, helped by Russian soldiers' own social-media posts; Russia later banned soldiers from posting.
- Skripal and Navalny: the suspects' passport numbers were "just a few digits apart, which is very suspicious", and "In Russia, basically all government data is for sale". Bought passport forms (stamped with the Russian MOD's phone number) and phone records (every cell tower, so agents could be tracked "minute by minute") unmasked them. Navalny, calling from a spoofed FSB number, got an agent to describe the poisoning, and the hour-long recording came out the Monday after Putin denied involvement. Bellingcat no longer buys such data; it did then because people were being killed with chemical weapons.
- Niche communities are sources: plane spotters exposed the rendition-flight network of the early 2000s, and the WikiFeet foot-fetish community debunked a fake Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez photo. "people with really really specific interests and hobbies can actually be a really useful source of information". Sources shift: a monthly Planet Labs subscription gives daily imagery and satellite tasking within days, Ukraine moved to Telegram from 2022, criminals post on TikTok, and infrared imagery mapped damage during Iran's internet shutdown.
- Speed matters because "once people feel they know the truth about a subject, they'll not change their mind about it". For events like the Minnesota shootings there are "at best two or three hours". In the Iran conflict Bellingcat published video of Tomahawk missiles striking near a bombed girls' school within about 48 hours; asked about it by journalists, Donald Trump replied "Well, the Iranians have Tomahawk missiles as well."
- Disinformation is a demand problem: "for disinformation to work, you need people who want to engage with it", so Russia's hundreds of fake videos on Armenia got no engagement. Treating it as a supply-side issue is "complete nonsense"; distrust pushes people to platforms that reward "engagement over the truth", where populist and conspiratorial claims beat slow verification. For a long-term investor, when reality is contested (the Iran story changes daily), "it's very hard to make any real, you know, tactical strategic decisions".
- AI "gives people a permission structure to deny reality": anything unwelcome can be called AI. It has not yet changed open-source investigation much (Jul 2026), but it helps at the margin: Claude wrote him a Python script that "within 10 minutes it done a job that I'd been putting off for 8 years", and models can spot clues, such as a tree that grows only in one region, that still need checking.
