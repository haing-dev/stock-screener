### 296. Erin Meyer — The culture map: feedback, trust and decisions across countries (NBIM, Aug 2024)

*INSEAD professor, author of The Culture Map and co-author, with Reed Hastings, of No Rules Rules; Nicolai Tangen asks her how to lead a fund with offices in Norway, London, New York and Singapore that it tends to treat as one culture.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4XGiNB00wIs)

**Takeaway:** Culture shapes how people communicate, give feedback, build trust and decide, so a multi-country firm can't run as one culture. Calibrate feedback to the listener, build personal trust before being direct, and match decision style to the work: consensus where mistakes are costly, egalitarian and fast where creativity matters.

- Her culture map places countries on eight behavioural scales, built from \~180,000 interviews in \~62 countries. The US is maybe the most low-context (explicit) culture: "tell them what I'm going to tell them and then tell them and then tell them what I've told them". In high-context France, where she has lived for 23 years, good communicators pass far more between the lines; in immigrant New York, "if you don't say it it was uncommunicated".
- Read faces, not just hands: in Japan, where people make less direct eye contact, a colleague spotted who had questions by how bright their eyes were and drew out four questions after she had got none. Back at INSEAD she saw the same signals in her classes. Likewise, a quiet colleague from China in a virtual team may not lack ideas: "don't underestimate how much culture impacts our communication".
- Feedback is the scale most likely to break a relationship. Americans learn to give "three positives with every negative"; the French give less praise and stronger criticism. A Frenchwoman who moved to Chicago heard her American boss's opening praise as "the best performance review I've ever received", and "by the time he gets to the real message she isn't even listening anymore". Too direct for the culture breaks the relationship; too indirect and the message is missed, so with outsiders start less direct and ease in.
- Her four A's of feedback: give it only to help ("aim to assist"), make it actionable, receive it with appreciation (thank, don't defend), and let each person accept or decline it. With that culture in place, end meetings with one thing each person did well and one to improve; Tangen tried it after a presentation and found it "really low stress".
- Trust comes from the head (cognitive: you are reliable and do good work) or the heart (affective: a personal bond). The US and Norway are task-oriented and keep the two apart; "every Emerging Market country in the world" weaves them together at work, so invest in the relationship first. With trust, "if you speak too directly to me I will forgive you"; without it, that may be the last time they want to work with you.
- Hierarchy and consensus are separate scales. Denmark is the most egalitarian country on her map; Japan is hierarchical yet "the most consensual culture in the world". Consensual cultures decide slowly and then stick to the decision, while in the US "the boss decides". Every country is "generationally becoming a little bit more egalitarian", which she credits to the internet.
- Fit the culture to the work: consensus suits products where mistakes are costly (Japanese cars), while "egalitarianism generally leads to a lot of creativity". For an IT start-up she would combine "the egalitarianism of Scandinavia", "super fast and flexible decisions like in India" and the feedback style of "the most direct cultures in the world the Netherlands and Israel", plus time spent on personal relationships.
- Netflix (from No Rules Rules): swap the pyramid for a tree, with the leader at the roots "leading with context not control" and junior staff often making the biggest, most expensive decisions ("don't seek to please your boss seek to do what's best for the company"). In that model a leader should spend "50% of his or her time" coaching people on how the organisation makes decisions.
