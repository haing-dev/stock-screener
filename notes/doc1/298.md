### 298. Dev Kantesaria — Growth plus predictability: toll collectors, pricing power and near-zero cash (Grant's Interest Rate Observer, Nov 2024)

*Founder of Valley Forge Capital, a concentrated (\~8 stocks), unhedged fund, and a former venture capitalist of \~18 years; interviewed by Jim Grant and Evan Lorenz on Grant's Current Yield podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IchsreKkNYc)

**Takeaway:** Kantesaria owns a handful of "toll collector" businesses at the intersection of growth and predictability, mostly bought when the market neglects them, and stays fully invested. He distrusts forward multiples, scale-based moats and big tech's capital allocation, and expects AI to be commoditized while lifting his holdings' margins in the near term.

- Few, big decisions (Buffett's punch card: "You have to make 20 decisions in a lifetime"). Positions are not static: a stock bought 17 years ago may be added to "13, 14, 15 times along the way" when the market misjudges intrinsic value.
- Markets are "generally" efficient and everyone has the same information, so the edge is interpretation. From venture capital he learned to boil many risks into one decision, focused on "earnings power 10 plus years out".
- Sometimes he buys after a 40–50% fall on a regulatory, legal or competitive scare, but "two thirds of the time, we're buying into these great businesses because of what I call market neglect".
- His "fishing pond is about 40 to 50 companies", and a couple of names join every few years. Quality is "the perfect intersection between growth and predictability": Colgate is very predictable but has low organic growth, and young fast growers aren't predictable. None of the \~2,000 IPO and SPAC-era listings "hold a candle to a model like FICO".
- ASML over TSMC: ASML is a monopoly "10 to 15 years ahead" and all leading-edge AI chip demand flows through it, "the most predictable way to participate in AI". TSMC is very capital-intensive and its edge is scale, "not an advantage that we prefer".
- Ratings and credit scores (Moody's, S&P Global, FICO) are toll collectors: cheap next to the debt or loan decisions they support, with a network effect a better model can't break. A free rival can't undercut them: an issuer like IBM would pay 30–50bp more a year on $5bn of debt with a lesser agency's rating.
- In early 2009 he bought S&P Global at $17.50 (on his honeymoon) and Moody's at $16.50; both were \~$500 (Nov 2024). The feared risks were "overblown, misunderstood": no legislative, regulatory or legal route could break the "natural duopoly".
- Beware forward multiples: FICO's 100% price rise on its mortgage score (a one-year rise he had never seen) and a mortgage recovery as rates fall are not in them. Picking stocks off Bloomberg forward P/Es for 2025–26 means "making a lot of mistakes".
- Pricing power is his hedge against inflation or deflation: "If inflation is 10%, we want companies that can raise their prices 13 or 14%". A weaker consumer hurts Visa and Mastercard, but the shift from cash, B2B payments and latent pricing power should compensate.
- Cash: he once held up to 20%, but in a portfolio of compounders "Our returns would be far better if we held zero cash along the way". Cash is \~1% now and will likely stay at 0–5%. His worry at Berkshire is who would deploy its $325 billion if something happened to Buffett.
- Public over private: venture capital (one investment sold for \~$2.7 billion) relies on one or two home runs among losers, while public markets offer a meritocracy, transparency and daily liquidity. Endowments favour private equity and credit because without daily marks problems "can be hidden for long periods of time".
- Big tech: avoided for 17 years (bar a little Amazon) over capital allocation, dual-class shares and founders' pet projects; Google rises 10% after hours when it announces a buyback. AI makes these firms capital-intensive with unclear returns, and he expects "an off the shelf AI program" to handle 95% of today's human tasks within a few years.
- AI: he expects "massive unemployment" (he sketches a 20% scenario), but over the next five years margins at firms whose largest cost is people, most of his holdings, should be "far greater than we could ever imagine". Investing is a relative game: if the S&P returns 4% a year for a decade, the job is still to beat it.
