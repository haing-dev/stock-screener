### 302. Hans Ulrich Obrist — The curator as "junction maker": serendipity, unrealized projects and reinvention (NBIM, Apr 2026)

*Artistic director of the Serpentine Galleries in London, often called the world's leading art curator, talking with Nicolai Tangen (CEO of NBIM) about creativity, relationships and what business can learn from the art world.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=heyZKDmCrtg)

**Takeaway:** A curator's job is to listen and make "unexpected connections". Obrist leaves room for serendipity, asks everyone about their unrealized projects, and keeps the institution fresh by adding new layers (technology, ecology, partners like Fortnite) on top of formats that still work.

- J.G. Ballard called him "a junction maker. You create unexpected connections." The method starts with listening: a studio visit a day since 1986, asking artists what the art world has not let them do, then "enabling" it rather than squeezing them into a framework.
- Leave room for serendipity. A checklist fixed early makes a show "very limited"; good projects grow as "a chain reaction". His "do it" show (artists write instructions anyone can carry out) has run for 33 years and "keeps learning, it keeps evolving", a counter to "short-termist deadlines".
- His standing question is about the "unrealized project": too big, too time-consuming, utopian, censored or self-censored (Doris Lessing: self-censorship is "not to be underestimated"). Projects outside people's daily routine "are very often the most interesting". Few fields publish them (architects do, through competitions); Tangen asked what could be learned by asking CEOs the same. His other fixed questions: advice to a young practitioner, mentors, and how the person connects to peers.
- Repeated interviews become a dataset. He has recorded since the early 1990s (\~4,500 hours; later "more than 5,000"), with 20–40 interviews of some artists, and wants to run the archive through "a proper AI model" to surface connections he missed. Tangen said NBIM has "implemented many of the ideas and the findings" from its own podcast into how the fund works.
- Attention and reach: visitors spend a "Few seconds" in front of an artwork, so multi-sensory shows hold them longer. Peter Doig's show doubled as a listening space with Sunday sessions, and people came back every week. The KAWS show ran inside Fortnite at the same time: "in 2 weeks 150 million visitors", more than any Serpentine exhibition, and teenagers brought their parents to the gallery.
- Reinvent by adding layers, not by dropping what works. Museum organigrams are "quite similar all over the world", so Serpentine added an art-and-technology strand \~12 years ago (now five curators) and an ecology strand. The pavilion began in 2000 with Zaha Hadid for architects who had never built in the UK; once famous, from \~2012 it switched to younger names (Sou Fujimoto; Sumayya Vally, invited at 29). A good program mixes famous artists shown "from an unexpected angle" with new ones.
- What business can learn: artists are "really good at pivoting" and work "with questions often more than with answers", which suits a less predictable world. He backs the 1960s Artist Placement Group idea (John Latham, Barbara Steveni) of an artist in residence in every company "and maybe also an artist on the board". No show should be routine: "if it becomes a routine... something gets lost".
- Time habits: the Brutally Early Club meets at 6:00–6:30 a.m. because then "nobody can say that they have prior schedule" (almost 100 people came). An hour with a "night producer" (10:30–11:30 p.m.) gets correspondence and transcriptions done overnight. He needs 6–6.5 hours' sleep; Balzac's "up to 50 coffees" a day, tried for \~6 months, was productive "but not sustainable".
