### 303. Rob Vinall — Judging management: skip the middle of the bell curve (Good Investing Talks, Apr 2019)

*Founder of RV Capital (2006) and manager of its Business Owner Fund since 2008, in a short interview on his research routine, his mistakes and how he judges managers.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c30hLHAmUkM)

**Takeaway:** Trusting the people who run a company is "by far the most important aspect of an investment to get right", because what an analyst can't see tends to surprise well under good people and badly under bad ones. Most managers can't be judged, so he looks only at the obvious extreme: those for whom the company is their life's work.

- Process: desk work first (annual reports and primary sources, notes, a journal on every company he follows, and a model built "not so much to value the company" but to understand how the business works). Then he visits the company and meets management, wherever it is based.
- Size: his sweet spot is market caps of \~1–3bn. Smaller companies tend to have "a very short track record" or a long, mediocre one, and the largest have often used up much of their growth. With \~200 million under management (Apr 2019) he has more voice with managers.
- Mistakes of commission came early: in the early-2000s crash he bought stocks below net cash, and some on "a very simplistic balance sheet analysis" at big discounts to book; a handful went bankrupt. His far more common mistake now is omission, missing good opportunities, so he writes down each thesis and reviews it later.
- Novo Nordisk (sold): he overlooked that its growth depended more on raising prices than on selling more insulin.
- Baidu (sold): the thesis was that it would repeat Google's success in China, but its results were poor and commercial, e.g. its own food-delivery service ranked above rivals. That brings traffic now but builds a service people don't trust, so they use it less often.
- Why people matter most: from outside, much stays hidden. Where he liked and trusted management, "the stuff I didn't see was generally positive"; where he didn't, "a lot of the surprises were were negative".
- Most managers sit in the middle of a bell curve where his judgment would be wrong as often as right. Avoid the dishonest, misaligned or unmotivated tail "like the plague", and focus on the other tail, where "the company constitutes their life's work".
- The main tool is deciding that this is what you look for; it changes which companies you dig into, and then \~90% of the hard work is done. The signs are obvious: a very long tenure, often the founder or someone who joined early in their career.
- Examples: Grenke (German small-ticket IT leasing, e.g. a few laptops for \~5,000 euros) bought a small Hamburg bank out of bankruptcy in early 2009 for almost nothing, gaining deposit funding. Trupanion founder Darryl Rawlings is still among the largest shareholders and runs it as the centre of his life; Howard Schultz shows the "love" for a company he looks for.
- Culture is an operating manual for unclear cases (at Amazon: what is most customer-friendly?). It comes from the founder's values and personality, so he sees leader and culture as almost interchangeable.
- Facebook (one of his largest holdings, Apr 2019): Zuckerberg is among the most mission-driven people he has met. His mistake was believing too dogmatically in openness, which bad actors exploited for hate and election interference; values must now add safety and privacy, and Zuckerberg no longer needs the money.
