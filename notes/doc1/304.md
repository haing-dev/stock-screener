### 304. Howard Marks — Risk control first, cycles, and the rare times forecasts work (NBIM, Jun 2024)

*Co-founder of Oaktree Capital, a credit-focused investor known for his client memos, interviewed in Oslo by NBIM CEO Nicolai Tangen.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vZJHQrGS7PE)

**Takeaway:** Marks builds everything on the limits of knowledge. Control risk, specialise in inefficient markets and skip macro forecasts and market timing, except at the rare extremes when a return toward the mean is dependable. In mid-2024 he saw US stocks \~20–25% overvalued but not at an extreme.

- Oaktree's six tenets: risk control first (the skill is to "make money with the risk under control"), consistency ("a little bit above the middle", rising to the top in bad years), only less efficient markets, deep specialisation ("you can't be a successful generalist"), no reliance on macro forecasting, and little expected from market timing.
- Economists are not "right consistently enough to follow": "an economist is a portfolio manager who never marks the market". Markets are people, and as Feynman said, "physics would be much harder if electrons had feelings". So randomness rules, and "you can't tell the quality of a decision from the outcome".
- Cycles come from excess: after a few good years every company builds a factory, "now there are too many factories", utilisation falls and building stops. The most common mistake is assuming a trend will continue; reversion "is much more dependable".
- Forecasts pay only at extremes, "when the market is crazy high or crazy low", and only on whether it reverses, not when or how fast. He has done it about "five times in 50 years". Before the GFC he knew nothing about subprime; he saw markets "acting in a Carefree nonrisk conscious manner", which "is the most dangerous thing".
- Taking the temperature: judge how uniformly, how strongly and how smugly opinions are held. Being contrarian is easier when markets are frothy; in the 2000 internet bubble the biggest winners held their views "for the silliest of reasons".
- Where we are (Jun 2024): "the middle ground", a bit above fair value. The S&P's P/E of 21 vs a post-war norm of 16 makes it "20 25% overvalued", yet the odds of a fall within a year are only "a little better than 50/50", because overvalued markets can become bubbles. A colleague's rule: "if you name a price don't name a date".
- The Fed: in Dec 2023 the consensus of six cuts in 2024 was "goldilock thinking" when the Fed's own dot plot showed three; by mid-2024 most expected two or three. Negative rates were set by central banks and unsustainable, but in the short run "don't fight the FED".
- Bubbles and bargains (Jun 2024): bubbles form around something new that impresses everyone, as some say of AI stocks, and China, called "uninvestable", may be too cheap. Oaktree's rule: "it's okay to have an opinion on these subjects it's just not okay to bet the client's money".
- Risk: "risk avoidance usually results in return avoidance". Take risk you are aware of, can analyse, can diversify and are "highly paid to take"; "risk means more things can happen than will happen" (Elroy Dimson). There is no separate risk department: the analyst who backs an investment owns its risk.
- Fixed income is "a negative art" (Graham and Dodd): an 8% bond returns 8% at best. Hence Oaktree's motto, "if we avoid the losers the winners take care of themselves"; strategies aiming for 15–20% must also find winners.
- Contrarianism means weighing the payoff, not just the likely outcome, like backing an underdog at good odds. Don't simply invert the consensus: ask why it might be wrong and what would expose it. "the big money is made by betting on the things they hate". After Lehman, Oaktree bought \~$650m a week for 15 weeks (\~$10bn), with partner Bruce Karsh's support.
- To excel, dare to be different, to be wrong and to "look wrong" for a while. Randomness means "if you're right 60% or 70% of the time you you'll be the smartest man in the world"; anyone who must always be right should not invest.
- Screen implication: his view that the big money lies in what others hate (and so is cheap) is the logic of buying sound companies after sharp sell-offs, and his capacity-glut account of cycles is the logic of buying good cyclicals at depressed margins.
