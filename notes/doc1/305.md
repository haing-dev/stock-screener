### 305. Rob Vinall — Identifying managers with talent and integrity (Robert Vinall, Jun 2017)

*Founder of RV Capital, presenting at an investor conference in Omaha the day before Berkshire Hathaway's annual meeting; his talk there three years earlier was on price, this one on judging management.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DoKb51qhJV4)

**Takeaway:** Investors overrate their models and underrate their ability to judge people, so management is a neglected edge. Vinall focuses on traits that are important and easy to see (capital allocation, alignment, humility) and on integrity, which is hard to see and shows only in situations where there is "an opportunity for a reveal".

- Three criteria: management with talent and integrity, a moat and an attractive price. Price depends on the business: an average one has to be "quite incredibly cheap" to beat a great one, which can justify prices value investors find "eye watering". A moat matters less for its width than for whether it is growing, since someone is always chasing.
- High concentration with low turnover lets him keep a wide universe yet know companies and managers deeply. Companies need loyal long-term holders too, and he applauds good managers "very loudly and very very publicly".
- Management matters on the downside (fraud, "stupid Acquisitions"), and "to be a great investor you just need to avoid doing you know the dumb stuff". On the upside, good managers carry the culture, allocate capital well, push decisions "to the lowest possible point" and give people permission to make mistakes, which innovation needs.
- MutuiOnline (Italian online mortgage broker): in the 2011–12 euro crisis revenue almost went to zero, and management wondered if the servers were broken. It built new businesses that now bring more than half of revenue. Buyers who bet on a mortgage recovery were right, but the big gain came from management's decisions.
- It can be done: Buffett's near-100% record in picking managers shows it. We "overestimate our ability to forecast financials" and underestimate people; in the 1960s, studying Berkshire's textile mill instead of Buffett was the way to miss it. "we should be a little bit more skeptical about our ability to build Excel models".
- Why it is hard: few investors really try; managers self-select as good salespeople; tailwinds get credited to the CEO (the halo effect); meetings are scripted and no CEO volunteers bad news; people avoid speaking ill of others; and everyone has some charm, even Bernie Madoff.
- Meetings: questions about the quarter, footfall and capex, or about competitive advantage and "what keeps you awake at night", get rehearsed answers that are "table Stakes". Get the manager "off of home turf": go for a walk, and talk about hobbies, family and former jobs.
- Example (\~5–6 years earlier): a consumer-goods CEO opened by boasting he would fire 20% of staff and lift the EBIT margin from 10% to 20% for a huge payout. Asked about his past, he told how private equity had made him choose between his own severance and his colleagues' (he kept his), a trauma Vinall thinks drove his aggression.
- What to judge: only a few traits. Important and easy: capital allocation (look at the record), alignment (win-wins with staff, customers and suppliers) and humility (dominant personalities show it at once). If financial incentives matter a lot to a manager, "it's probably not a manager you you really want". Important but hard: integrity.
- He plays down IQ, strategic acumen and even talent, which suit an old GM-style model of "the lone genius at the top". Modern firms push decisions to those nearest the customer, so the CEO's main job is guarding the culture by "setting the right example"; a lavish office or a private jet kills a frugal culture.
- Sources: management meetings; annual reports read as a time series for inconsistency (a promise dropped and never mentioned again); former employees, since one fired by the CEO who still praises him nearly settles it; suppliers and competitors (win-wins or bullying); and company biographies.
- Reveals: no one announces dishonesty, so watch dilemmas. In a downturn, does the CEO cut staff to hold earnings guidance or protect employees and strategy? Unnecessary layoffs show his true character.
- The most important source is yourself: "you need to live by the values you seek", because we like people similar to us. Biases can help: commit publicly to finding integrity, and trust liking, as Buffett did with Ajit Jain and with Precision Castparts' CEO after a \~30-minute meeting ("I just like the guy").
