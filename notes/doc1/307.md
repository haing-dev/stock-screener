### 307. Jens Stoltenberg — A more dangerous world, freedom before free trade, and the fiscal rule behind Norway's fund (NBIM, Oct 2024)

*Just stepped down after 10 years as NATO Secretary General; earlier Norway's prime minister and finance minister, who helped create the fiscal rule for spending from Norway's oil fund. Interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s3J_gs1Dy_k)

**Takeaway:** The world is more dangerous than it has been for decades, and Stoltenberg's lesson from Russian gas is that "freedom is more important than free trade": avoid dependence on autocracies without isolating China. Peace in Ukraine comes from changing Putin's calculus on the battlefield. At home, spending only the fund's return "worked even better" than planned (Oct 2024).

- Threat picture (Oct 2024): "the world has become more dangerous", with full-scale war in Europe, a devastating Middle East conflict with escalation risk (especially Iran), great-power rivalry and constant cyber, hybrid and terror threats. The good news: NATO is stronger, protecting "close to 1 billion people".
- Ukraine: "the quickest way to end a war is to lose a war", which brings occupation, not peace. You can't change Putin's mind, but you can change "his calculus" by raising the cost, since "there is a very close relationship between what happens on the battlefield and what can happen around the negotiating table". Allies should have done more before 2022; since then they have sent far more than Putin expected (Abrams and Leopard tanks, Patriots, long-range artillery, cruise missiles, F-16s).
- Finland as a partial parallel: invaded in 1939, it inflicted heavy losses but ceded roughly 10% of its territory and its second city, Vyborg, and in return gained borders and security that lasted decades. Only Ukrainians can decide what to give up, and outside talk of concessions "can be self-fulfilling prophecies".
- China: he was "a great believer in China" after Deng's opening lifted hundreds of millions from poverty, with Norway selling energy and buying cheap goods. His view turned when China sanctioned Norway over the 2010 Nobel Peace Prize to a dissident, then with its South China Sea claims (lost in court in The Hague), rehearsals for invading Taiwan and an opaque nuclear buildup. China is now "the main enabler of Russia's aggressive war against Ukraine" via microelectronics, alongside North Korea and Iran.
- For investors: "freedom is more important than free trade". Until 2022 many governments treated Russian gas as a commercial matter, but it was "a security vulnerability" Russia used to coerce Europe. Don't repeat that with China: avoid being "over dependent on specific Commodities or technology from China", yet "we cannot isolate China". Governments set the laws; within them each company must use judgment.
- Tangen's paraphrase of him, that business is too serious to be left to business leaders alone: gas from Russia, reliance on Chinese commodities, exporting key technologies to authoritarian powers or letting them control critical infrastructure may each be "a good business deal" yet dangerous for security.
- EU defense is good if it tackles "the fragmentation of the European Defense industry" and raises spending, bad if it builds rival structures. 96% of EU citizens live in NATO countries, and 80% of NATO defense spending comes from non-EU allies (the US, Canada and the UK, the second-biggest spender); non-EU Turkey, Norway and Iceland matter for geography. Together North America and Europe are "50% of the world's military might" and half its economy.
- Technology and war: AI may change warfare "as fundamental as the Industrial Revolution" did (autonomous systems, facial recognition, drones, cyber), and space is being weaponized, with Russia and China building offensive capabilities. As with nuclear weapons, restricting only ourselves makes us vulnerable, so the aim is balanced, transparent, multilateral arms control.
- Cyber: allies have offensive capabilities (one took down ISIS networks in 2016 or 2017), but attribution is hard and attacks happen almost daily, which blurs when Article 5 should apply.
- Defense innovation: from the 1940s to the 1970s governments built defense technology (the Manhattan Project, GPS, the internet); now AI, cyber, Silicon Valley and SpaceX are private, so militaries depend on private business. Tangen notes US venture firms have moved into defense while Europe lags; the Western tech edge "is not so obvious anymore" given China.
- The fiscal rule: Norway's first net oil revenues came in 1996 and much more was coming, so he feared "Dutch disease". From 2000, as prime minister, the rule put all oil and gas revenues into the fund and spent only the financial return, estimated at "3% in yield terms per year". Two aims: share the wealth with future generations and "separate earnings from spending and prevent overheating".
- It "worked even better" than planned, because the fund grew far beyond expectations (more production, higher prices, good management); its return now finances \~25% of the state budget. Experts, including a group led by a former Norges Bank governor, said politicians "will spend if they get the money": "we have proven them wrong". The new risk: so much wealth in equities means market swings can hit the economy. And "it's better to have an imperfect rule which is credible" than a perfect one that keeps changing.
- Leadership: compromise, in coalition governments and among 32 allies, often gives a better answer and is "the only way to make decisions"; you want staff with "the courage of telling you that you are wrong". In crises (the July 2011 attacks, Ukraine) stay calm, sleep and ask for help. With Trump he focused on NATO's core issues: his demand that Europe spend more was the same message as Obama's and Biden's.
