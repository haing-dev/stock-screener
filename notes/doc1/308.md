### 308. Jens Stoltenberg — How Norway built the world's largest fund, and the paradoxes it now faces (NBIM, Jun 2026)

*Norway's finance minister again, 30 years after first holding the job in 1996; twice prime minister and NATO Secretary General for 10 years, who helped create the fund's fiscal rule. Interviewed by Nicolai Tangen at the Ministry of Finance for NBIM's In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwkIyWbE140)

**Takeaway:** Norway's fund comes from three political choices, not luck: save every krone of oil and gas revenue, spend only the expected 3% real return, and invest in equities. Now worth over $2 trillion and paying for 25% of the budget, it faces new paradoxes: ethics rules that bar defense makers, top-10 tech concentration, and mega-IPOs it must buy once they list (Jun 2026).

- Norway vs the UK: both produced about the same oil and gas, but Norway has a fund of "more than two trillion US dollars" while the UK has none (and, Tangen notes, a lot of debt). The difference is "a big government take" ("we have 78% tax rate" plus strong direct state ownership) and high general taxes, so all net revenues could be saved.
- Three political decisions, all controversial at the time: how much to save (by law, all oil and gas revenue, "Every cent"); how much to take out (the 2001 fiscal rule: only the expected real return, estimated at 3%); and where to invest (equities, decided in 1997 in "this very room" at the finance ministry). "This is not luck. This is political decisions".
- No cap: the fund now pays for \~25% of the state budget, and he wouldn't have capped it at, say, 10%; the wealth belongs to the Norwegian people, present and future. Spending only 3% of a small fund was "extremely controversial" at first; now that 3% of a big fund is a lot of money, the rule is accepted.
- Where the money went: the 2001 white paper named work incentives, knowledge and infrastructure. Infrastructure spending has been heavy but not always profitable on cost-benefit, and roughly one third of oil and gas revenues financed tax cuts, especially on electric cars, which is why almost 100% of new cars are electric.
- A "Norwegian disease"? Many people on disability and welfare schemes, weak work participation, and a budget 25% financed by a fund that swings with markets. Hence pension and welfare reform: "the main source for our wealth is not oil" but work.
- Ethics paradox: last year he suspended the independent ethics council because its guidelines kept the fund out of major defense producers. Norway buys F-35s but can't own Lockheed Martin, or Boeing, and the Patriots and air defense sent to Ukraine come from companies it can't own. An expert council chaired by a former central bank governor will propose new rules, with a public hearing later this year and a proposal to parliament next spring.
- Caterpillar and big tech: the fund divested Caterpillar because a small number of its trucks, sold via the US government to Israel, were used to destroy Palestinian homes. If that alone excludes a company, "it's very hard to see how we can be invested for instance in the big tech companies", and as tech and defense merge, a fund that can't own big tech can't stay broad-based.
- Tech concentration (Jun 2026): Tangen says the fund made \~1,600 billion kroner from its top companies over five years, and the top 10 holdings are \~25% of the fund. Stoltenberg won't politicize the index: "if we had made that decision four five years ago we have lost a lot of money", and "if we try as politicians to forecast the market, we will end in a very bad place".
- Breadth: holdings were cut from close to 10,000 to \~7,000 to save administrative costs, and more adjustments are possible, but broad diversification is "the best way to have the best return over time and the lowest risk".
- Mega-IPOs: SpaceX, Anthropic and OpenAI are listing this year. The fund can't own private companies (listed ones are more transparent and easier to benchmark), so it will buy them as soon as they list. He calls that "a paradox" but says it's "far too early to decide" on a change.
- Stress test: a scenario in the government's revised budget shows that a market path like the one after the 1970s oil crisis would cut the fund "dramatically" and take "more than 15 years before we came back", hitting the budget. The buffer: in strong markets Norway has spent \~2.7–2.8% rather than 3%. The biggest risk is unpredictability: "wars are bad for business", and AI-inflated share prices could fall; his job is to diversify, not forecast.
- Norway is the European country "using AI the most" per EU statistics, and his ministry now links AI models to Statistics Norway and ministry data. Politics is more fragmented than before his 10 years at NATO, so finance ministers must compromise more; for the next 30 years, "oil and gas is good but it's not enough".
