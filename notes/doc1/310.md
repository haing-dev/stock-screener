### 310. Joe Tsai — Alibaba's reset, the Chinese consumer and the chip squeeze (NBIM, Apr 2024)

*Co-founder and chairman of Alibaba (NBIM owned almost 2%) and owner of the Brooklyn Nets, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mT6mRJehJdw)

**Takeaway:** Alibaba fell behind rivals because it "forgot about who our real customers are", and is resetting by admitting mistakes and rebuilding the organization around a user-focused strategy. Tsai sees Chinese consumers held back by a \~30% fall in property prices and job worries, China \~two years behind the top US AI models, and US chip export controls hurting Alibaba's cloud.

- Investor AB (1995–99, private equity in Hong Kong) taught him to invest: the McKinsey valuation book and DCF models. In 1999 he visited Jack Ma's Hangzhou apartment: 28,000 registered users logged in a notebook and about 10 toothbrushes by the sink.
- The scale only became clear in 2005, when Yahoo put in $1.25bn (partly buying out VCs in a secondary) at a valuation of over $4bn. Around then Alibaba even considered selling Taobao, now China's largest consumer e-commerce marketplace.
- Monetization: Taobao had buyers and sellers, but efforts in 2006 and 2007 "didn't work out that well". Search advertising fixed it, "a Google way of monetizing the marketplace on an eBay type Marketplace", and from 2008 cash flow grew exponentially.
- Losing ground (Apr 2024): "we have fallen behind because we forgot about who our real customers are", the shoppers using its apps. The fix is to acknowledge mistakes, then reorganize people and structure around the strategy. Big companies fit the direction to the org chart, but "it should be the other way around". The group CEO, 12 years younger than Tsai, also runs Taobao and Tmall, and his direct reports are drastically different from three months ago.
- Morale has been poor for three years: COVID, a flattening Chinese economy, competition and regulatory scrutiny with "big fines" ("all behind us"). Regulation is not increasing; the rules are clearer now, which "is uh very good for business".
- The Chinese consumer: China is \~31% of global production but only 14% of global consumption, and consumption is slightly over 50% of its economy vs over 70% in developed markets. Confidence is low: property prices are down \~30% on average (a wealth effect) and young people worry about jobs. Alibaba itself is cutting headcount through attrition. Household cash and savings are still high.
- Manufacturing: China has \~800 million productive workers, and even 600–650 million would dwarf Vietnam (\~100 million people, a 60 million workforce). Supply chains are diversifying to Vietnam and Mexico, but "they will never be able to replace China as the manufacturing center of the world".
- AI: an in-house large language model is "the left hand and right hand for our Cloud business", because developers who build on it use Alibaba's computing services. E-commerce has the richest use cases, such as virtual dressing rooms and merchants generating photos and product descriptions. China is "probably two years behind" the top US models.
- Chips: since last October (Oct 2023) US export restrictions on high-end Nvidia chips cover all of China, and they "did affect our Cloud business". Stockpiles let model training continue for the next year to 18 months, inference can run on less powerful chips, and in the long run China will make its own high-end GPUs.
- Geopolitics: Alibaba sells over $60bn of American products to Chinese consumers a year, plus \~€14bn of French and \~7bn of German products. It runs little consumer-facing business in the US because of data-privacy and cybersecurity concerns. He calls the debate over whether China is investable "quite ridiculous": "you can't bet against the Chinese people".
- Management: give people "a sight of what the future looks like"; give feedback immediately, not at quarterly or year-end reviews; admit mistakes; and don't always be the smartest person in the room, or "you kill Innovation". He works in intense two-to-three-hour bursts and then rests, like interval training.
- Brooklyn Nets (bought 2017): the NBA's collective bargaining agreement shares economics with the players, and \~$2.7bn of national TV revenue a year is split evenly among the 30 teams, unlike the EPL. That sharing, "almost like a little bit socialist", means owners rarely lose much money.
- Career advice: build one or two skills where you are in the top 10% (his was tax law, three years as a tax lawyer, still useful for international structuring), then add data science and psychology.
