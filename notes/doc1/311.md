### 311. Johan Norberg — Peak Human: how golden ages end, and why closing up is the real danger (NBIM, Oct 2025)

*Author of "Peak Human", on golden ages from ancient Athens to the modern Anglosphere, and of "Open"; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wF5nPeovgAE)

**Takeaway:** Golden ages end not from disasters but when a society swaps an outward "Athenian" mindset for a defensive "Spartan" one: tariffs, walls, scapegoats and strongmen. Norberg fears the Anglosphere's open, rules-based era, running since 1688, has reached that turn, and that lost confidence in a leader's currency or institutions can make capital leave fast (Oct 2025).

- A golden age is "a combination of cultural creativity, scientific discovery, technological innovation, and economic growth", usually recognized only afterwards. The Anglosphere's began with Britain's industrial revolution and checks on government, moved to North America, and after 1945 became a US-guarded order of "open seas and free trade rather than gunboat diplomacy". Unlike Rome or Baghdad, whose knowledge was lost when they fell, it is global.
- How they end: famine, plague, war and climate rarely end civilizations, since cities get rebuilt, but they trigger a shift from the "Athenian mindset" of exploring to "a more Spartan mentality where you stay at home to try to protect what you've got". He sees the West shifting that way over two decades: the 2007–08 crisis, the pandemic, wars, Ukraine, terrorism, migration and a sense that others now out-innovate it.
- Threats breed self-harm: societies react with a collective fight-or-flight and "build walls and tariff barriers" or "find scapegoats". Deglobalization worries him most: golden ages "imitate quite a lot", and repatriating supply chains means "we deny ourselves access to the brains of other people" and to their goods and intermediate inputs.
- Song to Ming: Song China grew rich by openness and was, some historians say, close to an industrial revolution 500 years before Britain. The Ming burned the world-leading fleet and the maps and "put the death penalty on international trade": "they got stability and stagnation for 500 years", and the richest country on the planet became desperately poor.
- Venice traded with everyone, "even to the gates of hell", and declined as war and religious conflict closed its trade routes. The Dutch, "up against the mightiest empire on the planet" with not even land to stand on, were forced to innovate in business, art and philosophy.
- "Strategic tolerance": openness for power, not niceness. Migrants raise the odds of serendipity and "get more Nobel prizes" than locals; Rome absorbed outsiders, while Greek city states that guarded citizenship shrank. Per Montesquieu, the Romans ruled because they "quickly abandoned their own ideas, methods and traditions" when they found something better.
- Hegemons: protected countries usually turn on a fading bully, but the US order was rules-based and let others prosper, so they defend it for fear of a worse bully. Now "America is fed up" with it, partly for good reasons, and risks an "overcorrection" that hurts itself; the order survives only if Europe, East Asian democracies and Latin American traders "do their bit and step up" for the WTO and open seas.
- Institutions: golden ages restrained rulers (courts, guilds), so people could "invest long-term in the future in new exotic eccentric ideas or business models" without fear of expropriation. Endings bring intellectual orthodoxy (the death of Socrates; today on campuses from the left and now the nationalist right), strongmen surrounded by "yesmen", attacks on courts and media (Turkey, Hungary) and legitimized corruption, e.g. late Chinese emperors handing the military monopolies and taxing powers.
- Fast unravelling: decline can be slow and muddled, but "if the world loses confidence in you", in the investment climate, the reserve currency or independent courts and central banks, "things might unravel very quickly" and "capital abandons you quickly".
- Confidence: economic decline turns a positive-sum game into a zero-sum one, so people stop investing and innovating, and "losing relative power has a very powerful effect on our psyche": Japan until the 1990s, then China and the 2008 crisis. Others' drugs, technologies and culture are a gain if adopted; shut minds and markets and only they benefit.
- Business: "nothing was in the blueprint": YouTube began as an online dating video app and Tencent as a chat program. Loyalty to the original model plus confirmation bias is "your way to ruin": "We have to learn to quit". Tangen: being "the most transparent fund in the world" made NBIM more open to outside ideas and faster to adopt AI.
- Peace enables long horizons: Greek olive growers lobbied for peace because trees take "some 20 years" to bear fruit, and golden ages needed systems peaceful enough for people to think long term.
- His own habits: he writes down each time he learns something he doesn't like, shares ideas early for feedback, and says "Failing is a great thing", as long as you admit the mistakes.
