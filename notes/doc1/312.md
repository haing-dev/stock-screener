### 312. John Armitage — What makes a company worth owning: management, incremental margins and no EBITDA (NBIM, Oct 2026)

*Founder of London-based Egerton Capital (started 1994), where Nicolai Tangen worked for him for five years before founding AKO; a "master class" on judging companies, building positions and running a firm for 30 years.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6431POnuLKw)

**Takeaway:** A moat is worth little without good management, capital allocation and culture. Look for pricing power above cost inflation and high incremental margins, value companies on conventional measures (never EV/EBITDA), and cut when a disappointment can't be explained.

- A good company isn't automatically a good investment. Moats matter, but "if you have a moat without the right management you get destroyed" (Boeing under its previous CEO; Rolls-Royce's "burning platform"). Capital allocation (Berkshire grew out of a closed textile mill) and culture (Intel didn't dare innovate) matter as much.
- First filter: is it worth the time? Many businesses are fairly priced, so why study them. Second: can I understand it? A big drug company's CFO told him they can't assess Phase 3 trials and think of them "as a series of options", and Egerton is "not very big on drugs".
- Know the "inherent operating leverage": what would make a business massively beat or miss expectations. A high incremental margin on a lower stated margin turns small sales surprises into big profit beats or misses. In a recovery no one else has found, you want "massively high incremental margins", because people don't dare to model them.
- Pricing power, raising prices despite competition, matters because every company faces cost inflation and more competition from lower trade barriers and technology: "if you can price more than your cost inflation, you're going to structurally achieve margin growth". Great companies change too: Coca-Cola has probably lagged the market for 25 years.
- Egerton isn't style-bound and invests in cycles, but with a cyclical "you often feel the best before a downturn". He likes stocks he wouldn't worry about if the market closed, with valuation protection on the downside and realistic managers. He avoids managers who use accounting policies to flatter results or for whom life is "always rosy".
- Modeling is a "fine art" of the right detail: with too much, small changes in assumptions send everything haywire. Much is private (Airbus order prices, reinsurance contracts), so infer it: for Airbus, the incremental margin per plane and when backlog pricing reaches revenue; for reinsurers, how reserves have developed, since "good companies tend to do better and bad companies tend to do worse".
- Below operating profit he checks accounting and depreciation policies, then cash flow ("what's the point of profitless prosperity?") and the balance sheet, since accrual accounting, as a finance director told him, "is a balance sheet output". He tracks inventories, payables and receivables, whether cash really rose, and "artificial measures" such as factoring receivables.
- Valuation support on a conventional basis (P/E, free cash flow multiples, maybe EV/EBIT) matters to him, and has probably cost him "lots of great growth stocks". "I hate with a passion" EV/EBITDA: depreciation and amortization are real costs, neoclouds are valued on EBITDA though depreciation is their biggest cost, and serial acquirers put too much into intangibles and add back the amortization.
- Oct 2026: a good company with decent double-digit growth near 30 times next year's earnings is attractive "in today's environment"; GE at 35 times earnings is "a bit rich". When he started, 20 times was high.
- Judge management by whether they admit mistakes: Ryanair's Michael O'Leary talks as if he had made a series of mistakes, not built the world's most profitable airline. When Pat Gelsinger arrived at Intel he had stock pay added back to earnings: "is that realism or not?" Red flags: consistently missing expectations, and a picture always slightly biased to the positive.
- One of his best sales was Altice, a levered, acquisitive French cable-turned-telecom group with little top-line growth. A slightly light quarter showed the model "didn't have much room for something to go wrong", so he sold. Fiserv was similar: results he didn't understand, a sale, then a big fall. He usually trims after a disappointment, and "The benefit of a high margin is that if you have a sales shortfall, your P&L isn't shot to pieces."
- Too much contact and information breeds "paralysis by analysis" (his partner Ralph's phrase). He won't lean on channel checks or card data, since they are "a function of something else which is more powerful, which is the business itself". Permanent capital or lockups are "a trap" that lets you sit on a collapsed stock; weekly redemptions keep the tension.
- Portfolio (Oct 2026): \~40 stocks, the biggest \~6–8%, thematically diversified, avoiding portfolio leverage. He dislikes the AI concentration, "but it's very powerful", and owns Nvidia, Amphenol, ASML and two hyperscalers. Alpha now needs high concentration, "marvelous when it goes right" but a disaster when it goes wrong, so he won't put "everything on red".
- 2026 was a poor year: he missed "a humongous great trade" but stuck with numbers he believed (Visa, once unwanted, is up 20% since). Egerton does best in trending markets and worst in violent ones; in 2008, from London, he thought 7% a year US house-price gains were "quite low" and missed the linkages to the wider economy.
