### 313. John Elkann — Exor: committed family ownership, great CEOs and pay for results (NBIM, Jun 2024)

*CEO of Exor, the Agnelli family's Dutch holding company with Italian roots (Ferrari, Stellantis, The Economist Group, Juventus, a stake in Philips), interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BLdDJ8BWqhw)

**Takeaway:** Elkann's model is committed ownership that joins ownership, governance and leadership, as in a founder-led company. He took over a near-bankrupt Fiat in 2003–04 and has spent one decade on survival, one on stability and the current one on growth. He wants pay that is variable, long-term and equity-based, and would pay above industry norms for outperformance.

- Exor "builds great companies with great people": it chooses companies, commits as an owner and aligns ownership with governance and leadership. Its role models are Berkshire Hathaway and the Wallenbergs (he visited Buffett in Omaha and the family in Stockholm). Healthcare is the newest focus.
- To last you must renew: Tangen cites Elkann's figure that only 45 of a million companies survive a century. Customers and competitors keep a company alert; exploring and inventing renews it.
- Family-controlled listed companies combine "the stability that a family provides and the long-term thinking" with the "stimulus" of public investors.
- Turnaround: after his grandfather and great-uncle died (2003–04), "the likelihood of us not making it were extremely high" at Fiat. The family put in capital, renegotiated with banks and hired Sergio Marchionne after four CEOs in two years. The Fiat 500 relaunch (2007) marked the recovery, and the financial crisis then led to Chrysler.
- Simplification: Fiat Chrysler was formed and the group split into CNH (farm equipment), Iveco (trucks) and Ferrari, which "had a very different Destiny" from a mass carmaker. The holding companies were merged into Exor, which now tries "to own less companies but more of these companies". The decades were survival, stability, then growth.
- The common thread is ownership: the most effective model is a founder-led company that combines "the ownership the governance and the leadership".
- A great CEO is "interested in the job and not the title", competes, likes the people, thinks long term but acts now, and is strategic yet operational; Exor looks for "ambition and humility". CEOs move between Exor companies (Marchionne from SGS to Fiat, Gerrit Marx from Iveco to CNH) or come from outside: after a year as acting Ferrari CEO, Elkann decided it needed someone from neither cars nor luxury, Benedetto Vigna.
- Pay: NBIM voted against Stellantis's pay package. Elkann wants pay tied to results: packages that are "long-term they are Equity based they are result based", with executives owning shares and broad employee ownership. And "if you overperform within your industry you should also be overcompensated".
- "Ferrari is not a car company": it is rooted in racing and extending into lifestyle. Its first electric car (unveiling from 2025) is "a white canvas" for new technology, developed with Jony Ive and Marc Newson's LoveFrom.
- Stellantis (15 brands, including China's Leapmotor) aims to be cost competitive at scale in EVs; more than two-thirds of 2023's EV sales were in China. Carmakers need some battery supply they control: ACC (with TotalEnergies and Mercedes) builds in Germany, France and Italy, alongside LG and Samsung partnerships in North America.
- Venture: Exor has invested in over 100 startups in mobility and healthcare, hosts Italian Tech Week, funds Italian startups at seed stage (Vento \[?\]) and runs a Y Combinator-style founder program, because "a country needs a future despite its great past".
- Healthcare: the Philips stake rests on the view that rising health costs will need solutions; Exor focuses on the "enablers".
- Screen implication: Elkann's standard (variable, long-term, equity-based pay and executives who own shares) is a ready checklist for the Owner-Operators manual review.
