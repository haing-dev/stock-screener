### 314. John Graham — CPP Investments: the total portfolio approach for 22 million Canadians (In Good Company, Jul 2026)

*CEO of CPP Investments, which invests the \~$800bn Canada Pension Plan fund; a former experimental scientist, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen of Norges Bank Investment Management.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LX8VCpl7rHQ)

**Takeaway:** CPP Investments' only mandate is to "maximize return without undue risk of loss". It meets it with a total portfolio approach: decide the risk level, diversify as "an act of humility", think in factor exposures rather than asset-class labels, and pick securities last. As a pension fund it will give up some upside when risk looks undue, and in Jul 2026 Graham says market concentration may be such a time.

- Origin: \~30 years ago the pay-as-you-go plan was heading for exhaustion (an aging population, fewer children, longer lives). Canada raised contributions, modified benefits and created CPP Investments when the plan was about 15% funded. The first check, \~27 years ago, was $12 million. Today the fund is \~$800bn, of which 550bn (70%) is investment income.
- The mandate, set in federal law, is to "maximize return without undue risk of loss" while accounting for what affects the plan's funding; no ministry sets the asset mix. What copycats of the Canadian model get wrong is governance: independent investment decisions plus accountability. That independence gives levers (public vs private, active vs passive, domestic vs global), and "I'm a big believer in optionality".
- Three decisions, in order: how much risk to take; how to diversify ("diversification is an act of humility. We don't know."); then which securities to own.
- A pension is "not a wealth maximizing vehicle": at times it gives up some upside to protect the downside. "we may be in that time right now with the concentration risk in the markets" (Jul 2026).
- Total portfolio approach: "We think in factor space" (duration, inflation sensitivity), with soft rather than hard allocations, because asset-class labels mislead and hard allocations cause odd rebalancing. He sees public and private equity not as different asset classes but as different ownership structures.
- Boundaries: no blanket divestment (it keeps investing in oil and gas), exposure to \~50 countries but real activity in only \~12, and it has never invested directly in crypto.
- Private equity has had a challenging couple of years, with returns below expectations, but over 10–15 years it has been one of the biggest drivers of return. Private ownership suits certain companies at certain stages: away from public-market scrutiny, with engaged boards. CPP backs top managers and co-invests or co-underwrites with them at better economics, so it judges the blended return rather than internal vs external teams.
- The benchmark is bonds and equities plus sector indices (real estate, credit, infrastructure, energy). Public markets concentrated in a handful of US tech stocks are hard for most active managers to beat.
- Geography (Jul 2026): \~12% in Canada and \~45% in the US (it can reach 50%), kept in a 45–50% band so it doesn't just keep growing. With the US at 65–70% of global equity markets, that is arguably underweight.
- Canada looks more interesting than in years because governments want to "build big things", and pension funds like "big assets that are cash generative". He welcomes foreign capital competing with CPP, since that lowers the cost of capital.
- China exposure has shrunk as the rest of the portfolio grew around it, without selling. A long-term investor must have some exposure to the world's second-largest economy, but should avoid sensitive areas such as defense and dual-use technology.
- AI: every employee has several LLMs, and bootcamps are available. Whether AI improves investment decisions is "TBD". Headcount is roughly flat vs 3 years ago with about 300 billion more assets, but "it's madness to stop hiring juniors".
- Investing is not science but "a quantitative art": experiments replicate, while markets are "this living ecosystem that's changing every day", and models remain best guesses "until someone gives me data on the future".
- Lessons: "you can't diligence a bad investment into a good investment"; more work or clever structuring may only convince you it is good, so know when to quit. Leaders' most common regret is waiting too long to build their senior team. Culture must be purpose-driven, not a star culture, and "Delegation without alignment leads to chaos". Bureaucracy "only goes one way", so periodically declutter it.
