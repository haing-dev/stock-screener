### 315. John Stankey — AT&T: fiber as the backbone, why it sold content, and why it won't build data centers (NBIM, Dec 2025)

*CEO of AT&T, at the company for nearly 40 years; interviewed live in New York by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GQPMFl-JlLI)

**Takeaway:** In a competitive industry growing at about GDP, Stankey's plan is focus: sell non-core assets, cut legacy costs, and pour the cash into fiber and spectrum, where AI-driven upstream traffic should bring another growth cycle. He sees AI data centers as a scale game for three or four players, with "some carnage" ahead.

- Industry shift: technology now delivers what deregulation aimed at, "real intermodal competition between wireless and fixed and satellite". The resulting "reordering of assets" will "readjust the winners and losers".
- Why telcos lag Big Tech: a competitive industry tends "to grow at GDP or GDP a little bit plus" and has not carved out a dominant, high-margin position. AI should start "another growth cycle" in data demand, giving "consistent steady improvement of cash flows if you invest correctly".
- Monetizing data growth: despite customers' "insatiable demand" for data, efficiency, added value and pricing keep returns solid. Convergence (one company selling a household both fixed and wireless) improves "monetizing that household".
- WarnerMedia (bought in 2016, per Tangen): changing a business model needs heavy investment, and "trying to reposition in two industries at the same time probably requires more cash and more patience than what the public markets are willing to offer". Selling content "was necessary" to be good at connectivity.
- Customers: connectivity is "a staple" and "we're not the $5 subscription", so customers forgive the odd misstep. But "switching costs are lower" and the old switching inertia is gone: an unhappy customer just pops in another SIM card.
- "I don't think about us as a utility", but as "a networking company". A software-driven network lets AT&T sell security, guaranteed quality of service for AI workloads and parental controls on top of connectivity.
- Why stay in the US: countries are more protective of "foreign ownership of critical assets" ("balkanization"), and for telecom returns "the United States stands above every place else". So AT&T builds fiber at home, including outside its traditional markets.
- Europe: "a developed set of economies that are basically on third world communications infrastructure in many instances". "Players aren't getting return on capital", so they don't reinvest; markets moving quickly from four players to three could start a reinvestment cycle.
- Fiber first: "everything's a fiber network with different access technology hanging on the end of it". AI will drive "more upstream traffic" (cameras and sensors sending video to hyperscalers), and "Fiber is symmetrical"; 5G and 6G are access technologies, not rivals. Video is "over 80% of the total traffic" today. New low-band TDD spectrum lets AT&T engineer more upstream capacity.
- Satellites (LEO) are "more complementary than directly competitive". AT&T plans day passes and subscriptions as real services arrive in 2026, though LEO may take some IoT work from terrestrial networks.
- Data centers: AT&T stays out. "There aren't going to be 18 people building data centers. It's going to be a handful, three, four", on their own chip designs. He expects "fewer winners than the number that are investing", "some carnage" and "a little bit of irrational exuberance in some places". US power supply is "a natural gating factor" on the announced build-outs.
- Policy (Dec 2025): the Trump administration probably did more in a 9-month period than the previous 4 years to set a roadmap for bringing licensed spectrum to market. Cyber is "the thing that keeps me up at night", but AT&T can sell its threat hunting to customers.
- AI inside AT&T: better service, lower operating costs and less churn in sales and support, "dynamic models of pricing", and cell sites retuned in real time to follow crowds such as a marathon.
- Turnaround: focus through divestitures, cost cuts and exits from legacy products ("another couple years of work") fund industry-leading investment, a "5-year journey" so far. He rejects the "Imperial CEO model": under one long-serving boss the organization's "smaller muscles" atrophy. Advice: careers now run in four-to-five-year chapters, so be "the dean of your own education".
