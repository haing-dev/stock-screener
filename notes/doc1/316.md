### 316. Jon Gray — Good neighborhoods, investment committees and why scale wins in private markets (NBIM, Jan 2025)

*President of Blackstone and former head of its real estate business, which he grew from \~$5bn to over $300bn; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1-_hxKKmIsA)

**Takeaway:** Blackstone has moved from classic value investing to high-conviction bets on "good neighborhoods" (logistics, data centers and power, life sciences), buying capital-light, recurring-revenue businesses with moats. In private markets size is an advantage, and private credit grows because it matches the duration of loans and the money behind them.

- The job is a premium over liquid markets: about "500 basis points of excess return" in private equity and real estate (15% net instead of 10%), and 150–200bp in investment-grade private credit.
- A good business: a large, growing market rather than a niche; a physical or brand moat; higher margins; recurring revenue; no exposure to one client or a government policy change; room for adjacencies. "you love businesses that are capital light".
- Best business he has seen: Hilton (he has chaired it for 17 years). Third parties own the hotels and Hilton is paid a percentage of revenues, so it grows without much capital. Its loyalty network pulls travellers and hotel owners into the system. Blackstone is the same kind of business: capital light, built on a brand.
- From value to conviction: early Blackstone would "project out a series of cash flows" for anything. Now it leans into themes: global logistics (the world's largest investor), data centers and the power behind them, and life sciences (buildings, trial companies, Phase 3 drugs). The goal is "trying to buy better businesses in better places".
- Memos keep the text up front to 3 pages or less and the whole memo under 20 pages: "it's less about page 58 in the footnote and more about those first couple paragraphs". Those cover the neighborhood, business quality and the big risks (technological disintermediation, labor costs, regulation). Gray sits on 10–12 committees, most meeting weekly, and reads memos on weekends.
- Committees go straight to problems, e.g. growth "helped by an acquisition" while capital intensity, working capital and capex rise. Teams score business quality (margins, capital intensity, management), and the committee pushes back: "you gave that a green that really feels like a yellow or red". It also asks whether a team is too conservative in a competitive auction.
- Consensus still needs a champion: Blackstone became the biggest data-center investor because a team championed a large data-center deal three and a half years earlier. The aim is to "avoid really big mistakes": flawed structures or businesses below the quality bar.
- Data plus instinct: 20 years of 4% revenue growth "sounds more like a long-term infrastructure asset". But the past would not have shown data-center demand rising 20-fold in two and a half years, or US power use, flat for 20 years, rising 4% a year. Management is judged by instinct: in many industries, with the same conditions, "one has grown to enormous size three have treaded water one went out of business".
- The portfolio is a data feed: 230 companies and 13,000 properties report KPIs on wages and inflation, and CEOs are surveyed quarterly. In Q3 2024 only 16% of US CEOs saw a recession; European CEOs saw a tougher environment. Eighteen months earlier Blackstone had told staff that inflation would fall and the Fed would cut, so invest before the all-clear.
- Scale is an advantage in private markets, the opposite of public ones. Blackstone bought AirTrunk, Asia's biggest data-center company, for $16bn (2024) because it could write the check itself, and can do a $3bn loan alone. It offers everything from senior investment-grade debt to mezzanine, preferred, minority and control equity. The risk of size is bureaucracy.
- Data centers (late 2024): overbuilding "will happen here" at some stage, as with railroads and cell networks. For now two things limit it: nobody builds a $2bn data center on speculation without a long-term lease, and scarce power gives owners leverage over tenants. Blackstone also plays it through power generation, transmission, backup power and financing (e.g. CoreWeave). But "if you pay way too high a price or you buy a bad business in a good neighborhood you could still lose money".
- Real estate (late 2024): back into grocery-anchored shopping centers because "no one's building shopping centers these days", groceries are harder to move online and they need less capex. Office values have "fallen very very significantly"; Blackstone left offices early over capital intensity and will buy only the best buildings at a big discount, selectively.
- Private equity has changed: 1980s returns were "mostly Financial Arbitrage" (leverage and cost cuts). Its two biggest 2024 deals, Smartsheet (workflow software) and Tropical Smoothie (food franchising), are fast growers bought with "a fraction" of the old leverage. Returns come from helping them grow: go-to-market, sales organization, quality control, marketing.
- Private credit is Blackstone's largest business ($430bn AUM), run as a third-party manager, not as an insurer or bank. It grows for three reasons. Lending straight from investors to borrowers cuts out origination and securitization costs, so investors earn more for less liquidity. Banks can flex a loan from 300 to 400 over, while "we're running a storage business not a moving business". And it matches duration: First Republic's $70bn super-prime mortgage book had almost no defaults, but long mortgages funded by deposits sank it.
