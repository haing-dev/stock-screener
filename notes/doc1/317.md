### 317. Josh Wolfe — Venture's "excess of excesses", scarce hard-science deals and Lux's directional arrows (Capital Allocators, Jul 2022)

*Co-founder and managing partner of Lux Capital, a $4bn venture firm backing deep science and technology, in Ted Seides's "Venture is Eating the Investment World" series; recorded in late 2021 (he expects Varda's first launch "next year", Q3–Q4 2022).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kfw0MLtnXqo)

**Takeaway:** Wolfe saw an "excess of excesses" in venture in late 2021 (100x-revenue valuations, cursory diligence) and expected LP indigestion to end it. Lux's answer is to avoid auctions and back scientifically hard companies with fewer than five competitors, imagining every way a deal can fail and then killing those risks.

- Excess (late 2021): venture money is "probably triple" what it was when he first said there was too much, and "being early is the same thing as being wrong". Diligence is "inversely proportional" to speed: a growth investor heard of one Lux company's raise on a Sunday, met at noon Monday, and by 1:12pm had made a market-clearing offer \~2.5x the next-best term sheet.
- Companies with under $10m of ARR get $1bn valuations: "these are 100 times revenue", against the 1–10x revenue Lux used to see. Prices rest on people believing that others will keep believing.
- History: from the Nasdaq peak in Feb–Mar 2000 the fall lasted two years and reached 78%, and recovery took another 12 years with dividends (\~17 after inflation). Most of ARK's holdings were already 20–70% down, and the turn comes when buy-the-dip investors decide "these losses aren't temporary they are permanent". Bottoms come with layoffs, liquidations and margin calls, and "right now nobody is ringing the bell".
- The trigger will be "lp indigestion": one LP's whiteboard of GPs returning to market looks "like a scene out of a beautiful mind". LPs will ask GPs to delay, GPs will slow their pace and tell startups to cut burn, and it may happen "more slowly but more fiercely than anybody expects".
- Lux won't compete in auctions on price; it starts companies itself with "total price control". The scarce asset is "scientifically rigorous" companies with fewer than five rivals and a true IP moat, such as a Nobel winner's microscope that sees into living cells.
- Software winners can take 60–70% share at high margins, but there are many rivals and the winner is often random (luck, a key sale, momentum). Hard science has fewer competitors because technical readouts take 2–5 years. The trade-off: a 1-in-50 shot at a decacorn, or \~20% odds (five or fewer rivals) of perhaps a $10bn outcome.
- Santa Fe Institute lessons: balance exploration and exploitation; respect power laws (20%, or 1%, of things make 80% or 99% of the value); and "failure comes from a failure to imagine failure". Lux lists a priori what can go wrong (people, financing, technology, competitors, customers) and throws time, talent and money at each risk. Most investors' diligence is "cursory at best".
- Biggest mistake: Control Labs. Lux fought Facebook's bid, which nearly tripled after a first rejection, while a co-founder still living in a Columbia dorm stood to make $95–100m. Not giving founders some early liquidity was "a colossal and historic mistake"; with a few million in hand they could have stayed the course.
- Directional arrows (late 2021): capturing smell digitally; the "tech of science" (new microscopes, lab software like Benchling), which he expects to attract "compounding value investor types"; and metaverse infrastructure (Subspace) rather than VR headsets.
- Deception versus detection: the contest with China (and Russia) across air, land, sea, space and cyber is "more important than climate change" to him. Lux invests aggressively in rockets, satellites and imagery AI, drones and counter-drones, autonomous boats (Saildrone) and hypersonics.
- Belief sets the timetable: a molecule works whatever people think, but crypto or space manufacturing (Varda) need belief, and "the rush of people into an area is this great tractor beam that pulls the future closer to the present". Lux stays out of nuclear power (104 US plants, nearly 20% of electricity) because adoption depends on public acceptance. Its nuclear-waste company Kurion worked because cleanup spending ($6bn of the Department of Energy's $25bn budget) didn't depend on belief.
- Crypto: he changed his mind. If Bitcoin took half of gold's $11 trillion as a store of value, that is $5.5 trillion, against $2.5–3 trillion for all crypto then. NFTs make sense by analogy with GPS: many computers agree that a digital item exists at one place and time.
- Internally Lux won't say it is slowing ("anybody that does that is going to lose deal flow"), but it asks whether each deal beats the last and whether the CEO is a top-five CEO. Partner meetings bring "nothing but good news and that to me is bad news"; the gains owe much to "the kindness of strangers" in public markets and to the Fed.
- Africa is his newest obsession: neglected by developed-world investors, with engineers returning from Stripe, Google and Amazon to Nigeria, Ghana and Kenya (like Latin America 5–7 years earlier), amid a US–China contest and Sahel instability. Lux plans $20–25m of small bets that year, accepting it might lose all of it, to learn who is real before doubling down.
