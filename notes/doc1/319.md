### 319. Georgi Ganev & James Anderson — Kinnevik: backing novel business models and waiting for extreme outcomes (Norges Bank Investment Management, Sep 2024)

*Georgi Ganev is CEO and James Anderson chair of Kinnevik, the Swedish listed investment company (founded 1936, its founding families still the lead shareholders) that has built businesses in telecom, e-commerce and healthcare and backs fast-growing companies. A live podcast with Nicolai Tangen of NBIM, which owns just under 4% of Kinnevik.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2FC2x0_z2IA)

**Takeaway:** Returns come from a few extreme outcomes, so Kinnevik backs novel business models, judges founders on their 10–20-year ambition, stages its capital and tries not to interrupt compounding. Anderson argues that spot price targets are meaningless for such companies: imagination and long-run scenarios matter more.

- Ganev's first question is how big something can become: people look at average returns and forget they are built up by extreme outcomes. Anderson: in America since 1926 "just 72 companies provided half the returns over bonds".
- Ganev: Kinnevik takes business-model risk, "something that very few investors do". Novel models lack peer groups and often fail on timing or structure, but when they work the outcomes are fantastic. It looks for models that can disrupt whole sectors where consumers are badly served by lazy incumbents, as with its telecom challenger in the 1980s and early e-commerce.
- Founders: ask about the 10- or 20-year outcome, not a three-year plan that just extrapolates. Kinnevik wants bold founders with grit who also deliver every week; it is impatient for results, but "we have the patient Capital to actually wait for the large outcomes".
- Healthcare: with Recursion, Kinnevik backs "the platform of drug Discovery", while many investors count candidates passing Phase 1 and 2, which are only "the short-term proof points".
- Anderson on prediction: no forecasting expert would give "a spot number often to three decimal points" for a company on a date. Use scenarios over 10–15 years instead. Every really successful company he has owned "has way exceeded anything that I could primarily envisage", so imagination matters more than precision.
- Tesla: his most useful input came from academics, notably Jessika Trancik (MIT, Santa Fe Institute), on how energy technologies improve. A minimum improvement rate of 15% a year (it proved higher), against the 2–3% even Toyota could deliver, signaled exponential change, and "as long as your clients allowed you patience" the odds were high.
- Traits of great companies (Atlas Copco, which he first bought in 1986, and ASML): stable long-term owners, whether founders or long-run families ("very few companies that don't have those" become truly successful); "constantly widening the market" rather than exhausting tiny niches; and long-serving CEOs who embody the culture. ASML never had a founder but has had astonishing leaders for 40 years.
- Selling: interrupting compounding is rarely sensible (Munger), and his sins of commission have been worse than his sins of omission. He sold Apple in 2016 because he couldn't see its growth after Steve Jobs, which was wrong in stock-market terms. Sell only when growth is exhausted (judged with imagination) or the culture changes: once a company reaches Bezos's "day two", it is beginning to die.
- Three ways to own markets dominated by a few winners: own everything (his largest client was Vanguard), improve companies through ownership (NBIM's route), or identify the traits of truly great companies and give yourself at least 10 years (his). Most of the industry plays a short-term game; he cites a New York figure that 60% of people are paid on their daily P&L.
- Europe vs US (Ganev): more than half of Kinnevik's portfolio is now in US businesses. The US has a higher level of ambition, while Europe focuses on legislation and regulation, and European startups must cross languages and cultures after a year or two. Sweden's affordable home-PC program and early fiber helped its startup scene.
- Private vs public: Kinnevik can "save companies from going public", where founders start managing the share price. Nvidia was nearly wiped out in a setback (around 2006, Ganev thinks) because it invested heavily in R&D that later built a trillion-dollar platform: "the markets are not rewarding businesses typically in the public environment to invest for the future".
- Anderson's three areas for the next 5–10 years (Sep 2024): clean technology, which has better business models than people believe; data meeting genomics, which could lift our understanding of human biology from \~15% to 50%; and AI. If AI compounds at \~60% a year, ask what problems it can solve in 10 years: healthcare, autonomous driving, general-purpose robots. Ganev expects "a lot of margin compressions" for AI "thin wrappers" with little tie to customers' workflows and very limited moats.
- Risk and failure: Kinnevik stages its capital as a company develops (a position can reach 100 million or more), and at some point admits a mistake. Anderson judges "the caliber of the decision making more than the outcome": Home Depot was within 3 weeks of bankruptcy around 1985–86, he says, and became one of the 25 best investments. Ganev: after a market reset, some companies that could have become extraordinary will go bust for lack of capital.
- Anderson's advice to NBIM: rethink the metrics. He sees no evidence that the financial rewards prevalent in investment produce better investing. Katalin Karikó was judged on the money she brought in each year, then won the Nobel Prize after 20 years.
