### 320. Kipp deVeer — Building Ares's private-wealth business, and why private credit holds up (Capital Allocators, Jun 2025)

*Senior executive at Ares Management, a credit-led alternative asset manager (\~$500bn AUM), on Ted Seides's Private Wealth series about selling private credit and other alternatives to wealthy individuals.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cp9mmo2Unh0)

**Takeaway:** Wealthy investors want more than a 60/40 portfolio, and the channel is consolidating around four or five big managers; Ares bought its way in and doubled its sales force. Private credit's edge is control, since a lead lender can restructure or take the company. But retail money is pro-cyclical, and growing too fast invites poor underwriting.

- Ares has \~$500bn of AUM (Jun 2025), still mostly credit, and is building real assets; the GCP acquisition added digital infrastructure and a Japanese real estate business.
- Entry by acquisition: Ares bought Black Creek during COVID for its industrial real estate and inherited a 75-person retail sales team. Its head, ex-Morgan Stanley Raj Dhanda \[?\], argued that four or five alternatives firms would dominate the channel, so Ares doubled the team to 150 globally.
- Around 2021 Blackstone had \~60–65% of a channel that then meant only non-traded REITs and BDCs, and advisers asked "do I really want to be 60% exposed to Blackstone?". Now four or five firms, including Blackstone and Ares, hold \~30–40% of the market, and the top five have been stable for a while.
- Sales metrics: engagements per salesperson and dollars raised per person in the field; "the death of a salesforce" is pitching products clients don't want. Wealth tickets of $200,000–$500,000 are "quite sticky", especially in income REITs and BDCs. The pitch runs on three themes: durable income, diversified private equity and tax-advantaged real assets.
- About $40bn of Ares's \~$500bn comes from wealth. Peers expect 40% of future fundraising from this channel; deVeer thinks that is high and expects \~20% of Ares's 2025 raise. Demand followed a year (he says 2021) when the 60/40 portfolio had "negative returns in both public equities and public fixed income".
- Advisers prefer semi-liquid funds to Ares's listed BDC because clients dislike its price swings, though his advice was "the stock goes down, buy more". Advisers want three to five managers with a curated range; Ares offers six or seven products.
- Private credit looks less volatile partly because of quarterly, model-based marks. But public credit also loses money through selling: buy a loan at 99.5, exit at 97, and two points vanish against a 6–7% coupon.
- The real merit is control: the lender originates, does the diligence and can "control your downside". Deals lever a company 5–6x against an enterprise value of 12–15x. If things go wrong, leverage may reach 8x while the company is still worth 10–12x, and the lender can restructure or own it: a "free call option as a lender to own companies". Ares's charge-offs have beaten public-market defaults over 20 years.
- Semi-liquid really means semi-liquid: investors probably can't get back more than \~20% over one to two years, so a semi-liquid private equity fund needs wide diversification and an undrawn credit line. Gates protect investors from fire sales.
- Fees are "almost off the shelf": every non-traded BDC charges the same, distribution fees are standard, and nobody negotiates yet (Jun 2025).
- Retail flows are "inherently procyclical": money arrives in good markets and dries up in bad ones, and retail-heavy rivals with a big flow quarter underwrite aggressively. He would not want "75% of my credit capital" in such vehicles; Ares balances drawdown funds, separate accounts, the public BDC and retail funds. Rivals that stop taking new money when there is nothing to buy are "really smart".
- Main risk: growing too fast with too little investor education, then poor underwriting and weak returns. The retail investor has not been tested: most money came in during and after COVID, in a low-volatility period.
- Global push: European income products raised far more than expected in 2024, after pre-GFC listed income funds there went badly; Asia may be next. Coverage is people-intensive: local salespeople for advisers running $500m–$1bn, plus group events through platforms such as iCapital and CAIS.
