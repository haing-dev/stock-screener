### 321. Magnus Carlsen — Intuition, time limits and risk: what investors can learn from chess (NBIM, Aug 2024)

*Former world chess champion and the highest-rated player in history, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on what investors can learn from chess.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d9cR7NvgFCo)

**Takeaway:** Carlsen's edge is fast, pattern-based intuition: he narrows the search to two or three options, stops deep thinking after about 15 minutes and matches his risk to the opponent. He sees edges eroding as everyone uses the same engines, bounces back by taking more risk after a loss, and puts the right mindset "somewhere between optimistic and delusional".

- Intuition first: top-level chess trains the brain "to narrow the search" and recognize patterns under time limits. When he loses, it is usually because an opponent looked deeply at something he had dismissed by intuition, which is also how others can gain an edge in business. He usually weighs two options and tries to strip out his bias toward one: "if I think about more than three Alternatives I'm clueless".
- Time limits: about 15 minutes is right for a deep think. After that he widens the search "or just end up going in circles" and forgets why earlier moves were rejected, and perfectionists who think for 45 minutes often play the obvious move. He agrees with Tangen that in investing, more time makes you more certain but doesn't improve the decision.
- Risk depends on the opponent: against solid, risk-averse players he takes more risk; against aggressive ones he is cautious and lets them create the imbalance, which favours the better decision-maker over time. The most dangerous opponents are solid but carry a real counterattacking threat.
- Edges erode as information spreads: his coach of 11 years, Peter Heine Nielsen, finds new ideas far harder than 15 years ago, because "you have a market that's much better informed now and the people are using more or less the same tools". An opening that took a month to learn now takes about five hours with engines. Early users of neural-net engines (2019) gained an edge for a few months, until everyone had the same tools.
- Bouncing back: his classical rating has ranged 2820–2880 for years, but his performance right after a loss is over 2900. He takes more risk and focuses harder after losing, whereas investors, Tangen notes, usually take less. In fast formats a painful loss can still make the mind spiral.
- Optimism: "the right balance is somewhere between optimistic and delusional". Optimists see and take more chances and put pressure on opponents, though some of his winning streaks looked less impressive on later analysis.
- Using engines: separate human mistakes from those only a computer would find, because "your opponents they will continue to be human". Neural nets changed how he weighs time, material and king safety.
- Keep exploring: an early mentor made him drop a comfortable Sicilian line and learn a new opening, since without exploring "you're not going to learn". His advice to the young: be curious, read and play chess, "but don't pursue it as a career unless you're going to be the best in the world".
