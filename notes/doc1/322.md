### 322. Malcolm Gladwell — Demographics over technology, consistency over intensity, and the person five steps down (NBIM, Jan 2025)

*Journalist and author of The Tipping Point, talking with Nicolai Tangen (CEO of NBIM) about change, admitting mistakes, motivation, contrarians and storytelling.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X0RNhfHswiA)

**Takeaway:** Technology moves far faster than human nature, and demographic shifts matter more than either. Gladwell's lessons for leaders and investors: value consistency over intensity, admit wrong ideas openly, judge people through those around them, and look for detail five levels below the CEO.

- "demographic Trends to my mind will always Trump technological Trends". An older workforce adopts new tools slowly: getting "a 55-year-old doctor" to hand diagnosis to AI is far harder than persuading a medical student, and in experiments where the AI is better, doctors resist because it "takes away the fun of practicing medicine". An "exogenous shock" like COVID speeds adoption. US college applications are "plummeting", which may end 150 years of expanding college education. Tangen said that after meeting more than 50 US CEOs in New York, it was the first time he had heard them talk about Europe's declining demographics.
- Leaders confuse being wrong about an idea with a moral failing. A moral failing does lasting damage; admitting a wrong idea or action, if you understand the mistake and act on it, "strengthens my belief in you as a leader" and shows a culture that doesn't penalize risk-taking. His own reversal: The Tipping Point (25 years ago) praised aggressive policing of small disorder, which he now calls "simply wrong". Crime comes from "a tiny tiny tiny fraction" ("a couple of thousand people" in a city of nine million) on the same street segments for years, so targeted work pays: "instead of trying to track a thousand people you can track 10".
- Consistency over intensity: running taught him "there's no such thing as instant gratification" (train in October for a race in May) and that results depend more on the consistency of effort than its intensity. He never works at the last minute: "better to do two hours a day you know starting six months early than to do six hours a day starting one month early", which leaves time to set work aside and see what's wrong. Tangen: "a consistent track record is much better than a more volatile track record".
- Hiring and judging people: many CEOs like hiring former athletes, but a mediocre athlete who persists may gain as much as a star, since "the time costs of being exceptional" crowd out other skills, and persisting without reward shows self-generated motivation. He likes people who "find pleasure and joy in very very small things" (easy to motivate). "there's almost nothing we do worse than um assess someone's personality in a short period of time"; for a 10,000-word profile a good journalist picks unlimited access to everyone in the subject's life over access to the subject.
- Go five steps down. He would pass on an hour and a half with any Fortune 500 CEO: the CEO offers "a broad conceptual understanding of where the company is headed very useful if you're an investor", but the person five steps down has the specifics and anomalies (an IBM logistics expert showed him COVID bottlenecks were "a behavioral problem"). CEOs should be boring: trust comes from consistent self-presentation, and "you don't want manic behavior in someone who's running a large scale Enterprise".
- Power removes feedback. The rich stay sensitive to small tax changes and keep complaining because we "permanently inhabit the world of our youth", and because the circle that corrects you shrinks as you rise ("you're not getting feedback anymore"). Working into your 70s or 80s for enjoyment is fine; the worst case is staying on without self-awareness, like Joe Biden or "the CEO who who is no longer capable of doing their job".
- Contrarians pay a social price. Nassim Taleb's long-tail fund lost "a little bit of money" every day, hoping to make it all back "in a rush", and faced "social pressure um for 95% of the time" of the fund's life. The inventor of combination chemotherapy was ostracized at the National Cancer Institute for 10 years. A journalist pays no such cost; in Gladwell's own circle "it's quite contrarian to believe in markets".
- Storytelling: a thriller is "disordered information" that withholds the outcome but paces it so the reader trusts the teller can "land the plane". He drops books when that trust goes, often when they "try and tell you too much on the first page". He and Tangen (citing their friend Mala Gaonkar) apply this to investing: you entrust money to a company with a compelling story, as Steve Jobs conveyed at Apple. Ideas come "after activity not before", so keep moving.
