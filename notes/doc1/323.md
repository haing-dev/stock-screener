### 323. Marc Rowan — Purchase price matters: Apollo, private investment-grade credit and indexed markets (NBIM, Nov 2024)

*Co-founder and CEO of Apollo Global Management (\~$700bn AUM; NBIM owns just under 2%), interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yOoJd58jERw)

**Takeaway:** Rowan runs Apollo on three rules: purchase price matters, excess return per unit of risk, and alignment (about half the assets are Apollo's own balance sheet). Most private credit is investment grade and earned through origination, not by reaching down the capital structure. With spreads tight and valuations high (Nov 2024), he holds back and stays senior.

- Apollo is two businesses. Retirement Services (with its European affiliate) has \~$400bn and grows $70bn a year organically, the world's largest. Asset management has \~$700bn: $550bn credit, mostly investment grade, and $150bn equity, of which \~$100bn is traditional private equity and the rest "hybrid" (between debt and equity).
- "origination is the lifeblood of our business". Apollo's baseline view is that public fixed income markets "offer no Alpha", so it built lenders like the old GE Capital (fleet, aircraft and securitization finance), with 4,000 staff outside Apollo itself. It keeps "25% of everything and 100% of nothing" and places the rest with clients.
- Private credit is mostly investment grade and covers "almost every asset on a bank balance sheet". Banks fund short and lend long, so they are poor holders of long assets (infrastructure, energy transition, data and power); Apollo matches those with long insurance liabilities. Banks are levered 12–14x and their balance sheets are \~60% investment grade, against over 90% at Apollo. A bank wants the client and its services; Apollo wants only the asset.
- Credit is moving from banks to investors. Bank lending is under 30% of US credit but 65% in Europe, which squeezes banks without freeing investors: "Europe suffers from a capital deficit". US public companies have fallen from 8,000 to 4,000, and 80% of companies with over $100m of revenue are private.
- Public markets are "indexed and correlated and it is about Capital flows", not short-term price discovery. Over 60% is passive, 10 stocks are 39% of the S&P 500, and active managers have failed to beat the index "90 plus% of the time for the past 20 years".
- The last decade's private-market vintages will disappoint. Deals were done while the US printed $8 trillion and rates sat near zero, and "60% of quote private Equity was actually growth". Good companies bought at high prices on cheap debt must refinance at much higher rates and will be held longer. Expect a shakeout between "private markets beta" and "private markets Alpha", with mid-sized firms squeezed.
- Run for return, not growth: Apollo's $100bn+ private equity franchise can't double, because "this business is run for rate of return it is not run for growth". Taking in too much money "will dilute our returns". Fees have been, and will stay, "remarkably stable".
- Three principles. First, "purchase price matters": value in equity, and in credit protection of principal earned through structure and origination, not credit selection or reaching down the capital structure. Second, excess return per unit of risk. Third, alignment: of \~$700bn, $350bn is Apollo's own balance sheet, so "we are generally the largest buyer of everything".
- Cost of capital: private equity needs "a 20% plus rate of return", which suits few companies. Apollo has taken its origination skills down to investment grade, so its average investment now carries a "six or 7% cost of capital".
- Stance (Nov 2024): "is the world cheap today no is risk being rewarded today no spreads are tight". Reaching down the capital structure "feels like a bad bad idea today"; being senior and getting paid for origination and structure is a good use of time. He will take risk after markets correct, as "at some point they always do".
- US setup (Nov 2024): growth, low unemployment and unspent fiscal stimulus (a nearly $2 trillion infrastructure bill, $52bn for chip plants, the IRA), plus three years as the largest recipient of foreign direct investment. Rates rose 500bp and "home prices went up stocks went up". The worry is a $2 trillion peacetime deficit at 4% unemployment: long-term, until the bond market, the world's disciplinarian, reacts.
- Liquidity risk: fixed-income market-making capital is \~10% of its 2008 level while the market is three times bigger, and selling an investment-grade corporate bond takes 5 days even in good times. In the next big risk-off "we should expect little to no liquidity in publicly traded fixed income". Fixed-income ETFs almost broke in COVID, and UK LDI showed what happens when everyone owns the same risk. Daily liquidity for 50-year savings ($12–13 trillion in US 401(k)s, mostly index funds) is "the biggest imbalance".
- Convergence: over the next 18 months institutions will split fixed income into public "beta" and private "alpha" investment grade, with rating agencies as the common yardstick. Equity comes later: "you may not own private Equity but you will own Equity that is private".
- Culture: "most companies who are successful eventually revert to the mean" once leaders start "playing not to lose". Rowan says he is right about two-thirds of the time and fails fast. His mistakes are about people, scaling and time frames; his biggest success, Retirement Services, came from being positioned early.
