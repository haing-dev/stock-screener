### 324. Marcus Wallenberg — 175 years of family ownership: adapt, stay close, let foundations own (NBIM, Oct 2024)

*Chair of SEB and vice chair of Investor AB, the Wallenberg family's holding company (Ericsson, Atlas Copco, Electrolux, AstraZeneca, ABB, SEB), interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on In Good Company.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bdfUZcK2I9Q)

**Takeaway:** The Wallenbergs have lasted \~175 years by adapting to each technological wave and acting as close, long-term stewards of a few large companies, while foundations that nobody owns hold the wealth and fund research. A great company needs management, market position, innovation and stable finances; a good chair stays close enough to both challenge and support the CEO.

- The key to surviving 175 years is being ready to "adapt with the times": from the steam engine through electrification and the internet to AI.
- Two tracks. First, stewardship of companies where the family has a financial interest, staying close so they remain competitive in a fast-changing world. Second, foundations that give substantial sums to Swedish universities, funded by the companies' dividends, which also keep the family close to what is coming in science and technology.
- There are now \~15 family foundations, and "nobody owns the foundation", so generations don't quarrel over money; family members who work in the group do so out of interest.
- Reviews of "future proofing" cover people, the ability to innovate, sustainability, and every company's data, AI and cyber strategy. A great company has "management Market position ability to innovate stable finances"; even engineering groups like Atlas Copco face constant pressure to innovate.
- Swedish companies became world leaders because a small trading nation must compete and innovate. Most Wallenberg companies do less than 10% of their business in Sweden, so they rely on free trade, which is "perhaps not an obvious question today".
- Next generation: \~30 members, introduced over more than 10 years to each other, the businesses and the foundations. Some will work in the business, some in the foundations, and some will be a "happy family member"; nobody gets a free ride because of the name.
- On buying in sell-offs: the family has taken "a very step bystep approach", because a very large shareholder cannot easily move its position. It has invested heavily over 10 years through Patricia Industries (Investor's private arm) and recently raised its Ericsson stake.
- EQT was modeled on AEA Investors, which used retired CEOs of big US companies who "still had a lot of mileage left in them" to develop portfolio companies. Listing EQT let it buy Baring Private Equity Asia with shares. The lesson from private ownership: you can be "more Nimble" and much closer to companies than a minority owner can.
- The foundations fund basic, often high-risk research: 50% backs people and 50% large long-run projects such as AI and quantum. The research is open, and in Sweden the researcher owns the IP.
- For a bank, risk now goes well beyond credit, market and liquidity risk to cyber, regulatory, political and climate risk.
- Leaders: the right one depends on the situation, since a turnaround needs a different leader from an expansion. Moving people across companies builds leaders (Börje Ekholm ran Investor before Ericsson). Tangen calls Atlas Copco a CEO factory; Wallenberg credits its habit of making even small units take full responsibility. Former CEOs often become chairs.
- Good chairs have "presence": close to the company, forward-looking and alert to where things can go wrong. The Nordic split of chair and CEO works because the "CEO needs somebody to talk to" and "chairs can both Challenge and support", if they have the time.
- Dual-class shares are "a contractual right", often created when entrepreneurs needed capital but wanted to keep influence, and investors are free to buy or not. NBIM generally votes against them but not at Wallenberg companies, where it bought "with open eyes", Tangen says.
