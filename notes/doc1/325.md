### 325. Michael Bloomberg — Building the terminal nobody could catch, and a company run on its people (NBIM, Jan 2026)

*Founder of Bloomberg LP, mayor of New York for 12 years and philanthropist, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen at Bloomberg's New York offices.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8qC_rBlNwHc)

**Takeaway:** Bloomberg built a product users didn't know they needed, spent three years on it before a real customer, and kept adding functions without raising the price, so rivals that do "one or two" of its tasks never caught up. He runs the firm on looking after employees and customers, rewards trying over succeeding, and gives away virtually all its profits.

- Career lesson (1966): offered $14,000 a year at one firm and $9,000 at another, he took the $9,000 job (plus a $2,500 loan) because he liked the people. "people make the mistakes of going to work for a place where they get paid the most": early on, get experience, build friendships and try things instead of chasing titles.
- The idea: trading-desk screens showed prices, "but there was nothing on your desk that told you that let you do what you needed that data for", such as a graph, a calculation or a message. Before PCs existed he built hardware in engineer Ron Harris's \[?\] barn. Once PCs arrived, Bloomberg got out of making computers and bought PCs.
- The moat: there were 3 years of work before a real customer, and by then "it was too late for anybody to catch up". Rivals have good products, but "they only do one or two of the 10 20 things that we do", while users need many inputs across different customers and markets.
- Product and pricing: Bloomberg "just kept adding functionality, didn't increase the price". Customers are worth asking but can't imagine what a computer could do, so his team watched them try to use the product (e.g., bond terms in, price or yield out).
- Preparation wins deals: at an early bank demo, employee Glenn Walner \[?\] forgot the power cord but had printed every screen, and the bank ordered two or three computers on the spot.
- Culture: outwork others while keeping some balance; at 83 he is in the office by 7 a.m., and attrition is very low. On risk: "you don't have a penalty if things don't work. Quite the contrary, the fact that you tried is a plus".
- Spend on people and customers: offices go in expensive places because "the most important asset this company has is its employees". On companies using AI to cut low-paid customer-facing staff: "Last time I checked, the really important group was your customers".
- With 26,000 employees, someone or their family is hurt or dies every week, and he calls every one, which tells everyone they are valued. Give credit away ("I was lucky enough to help him"): people think better of you, and the other person owes you.
- As mayor (12 years), life expectancy in New York rose by three years and test scores went way up. The gap between Black and Latino and white and Asian students was cut in half, "by bringing the bottom up". City and company run on the same principles, but in government you are not the owner, so you explain the problem and stake your career on the fix.
- Philanthropy: more than $20bn given away (Tangen), and he gives away "virtually all the profits of the company". His foundation (\~350 people) follows up to make sure each gift does what was promised; examples include two rounds of $100m for Bill Gates's polio work and $75m for children's glasses.
- Climate: a new satellite finds methane leaks, and producers are told they can sell the gas instead. He says his campaigns closed over half of Europe's coal-fired power plants and about 75% of America's.
- Succession: he won't retire, but has appointed a CEO and a president so customers can be comfortable that service continues "if I drop dead".
