### 326. Michael Dell — The direct model's inventory edge, going private, and AI at 10% of the S-curve (NBIM, Dec 2024)

*Founder, chairman and CEO of Dell Technologies, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dG1WSP72mnY)

**Takeaway:** Dell's edge was a capital-light direct model: days rather than months of inventory, in an industry where parts get cheaper every month, gave a structural cost advantage and a higher return on capital. He took Dell private in 2013 when the market priced it as a dying PC maker despite billions of free cash flow, and he places AI in "the first 10% of the scurve".

- The opening: at 16 he took apart an IBM PC (launched Aug 1981). None of the parts were made by IBM, and the price was "roughly six times the cost of the parts". IBM was then \~80% of the value of the technology sector.
- Growth: incorporated in May 1984, Dell had $6 million of revenue in year one and \~$33 million in year two. It grew \~80% a year compounded for eight years, then \~60% a year for the next six.
- Inventory as a cost moat: material costs keep falling, so a company with 5 days of inventory between factory and customer beats one with 90 days: "there's only one company that's going to win". It also earned a "way better return on Capital", since less capital was tied up. The model came from necessity: "we didn't have any capital".
- Selling direct gave "incredible Fidelity in the signal of the demand": each order says exactly what the customer wants, and that signal goes straight back to suppliers. Most sales were always to businesses, governments and institutions, not consumers.
- Going private (2013, after 25 years listed): from \~2010 Dell moved into cloud, networking and storage, partly by acquisition, and "the more we did that the less the market liked it". With smartphones and tablets rising, the market's logic was "the PC's dead so therefore Dell's dead". Yet Dell generated "billions of dollars of free cash flow every year" ($150bn over its life), so he chose to "buy back all the shares".
- Private for the long view: by 2012–13 Dell was "overly short-term focused". Going private moved the focus from quarterly swings to five- and 10-year outcomes and revived the entrepreneurial culture, which Dell has kept since becoming fully public again at the end of 2018.
- EMC ($67bn, the biggest tech acquisition, bought while Dell was private): the thesis needed no "heroic assumptions", only that large customers would rather have fewer suppliers. It brought $20bn of organic revenue growth in the first several years. Tangen calls it the most profitable private deal ever; Dell: "that's what I'm told".
- AI stage (Dec 2024): "the first 10% of the scurve". Today's tools are "the worst they'll ever be", and most of the world's data is untouched. Early adopters get "10 or 20% productivity Improvement" without much effort, with "sightings of 30% or 40%", so firms in the same industry will separate faster. Larger firms gain more but carry more cultural inertia.
- Tangen says Sam Altman told NBIM to target a 20% productivity gain; Dell calls it "a completely reasonable Target".
- Chips: Nvidia has "an incredible early lead", but he doesn't see one company winning everything. AI will go where the data is, so most of it will run in the physical world (PCs, phones, cars), not only in training data centers. He calls Dell probably the world's leading builder of large AI server farms for training and inference.
- Compute: the cost of a unit of computing is approaching zero, but anything interesting and valuable needs more computing power.
- AI PC cycle: new PCs carry an NPU (neural processing unit), and maybe 85–90% of PC AI will run locally. About 750 million of the world's \~1.5bn PCs are more than four years old and can't do it. The PC is not Dell's main profit business (cloud and data center is), and buyers replace it only when the new one is significantly better.
- Management: "if they don't disagree with me then I get very worried". Beliefs change with new data, and he gives more responsibility to people who speak up, "see around corners" and challenge how things were done.
