### 329. Mike Gitlin — The Capital System, 8-year pay and active management in concentrated markets (In Good Company, Feb 2026)

*President and CEO of Capital Group, the employee-owned manager of $3.2tn (Feb 2026), interviewed by Nicolai Tangen of Norges Bank Investment Management.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nXqGnIFQhTo)

**Takeaway:** Capital Group's edge is structural. Several PMs and analysts each run part of every fund, so there is no key-person risk; pay is driven mainly by 8-year results; and private employee ownership lets it invest through downturns. With passive at about half of equities and benchmarks concentrated, he argues a broadening market leaves active managers "no excuses".

- Private and employee-owned, so no quarterly-earnings pressure: "the S&P 500 can be down 20%. And we can still invest in that technology project". Ownership is "rented by the owners": staff sell their shares back at retirement, no one owns more than \~1%, and there is no long-term debt. On listing: "Why would you take what may be one of your biggest advantages and willingly give it away?"
- The Capital System (1958) came out of the founder's heart attack: no key-person risk. Analysts invest real client money (a research portfolio) instead of issuing ratings, and several PMs each run a sleeve of the same fund under a principal investment officer, so "you're not left with one individual's 300th best idea".
- PMs are combined by complementary styles, using data on each one's upside and downside capture. Portfolios are open to everyone and there is no hierarchy, which he says prevents groupthink.
- The sin to avoid is style creep: a value manager who turns growthy in a roaring bull market is caught offside when it ends, and if everyone did it "the overall portfolio would become way too growthy".
- Incentives: "The number one driver of an analyst or portfolio manager's quantitative compensation is their 8-year number", then the 5- and 3-year numbers; 1-year results are the smallest part. Rob Lovelace (40 years at the firm, grandson of the founder) on his edge: "Time. I have the time to be right."
- Low turnover by design: "there's no such thing as high turnover proving in correlation terms to produce better investment results". Reacting to every quarterly report makes no sense for "a business that's going to grow 12% compounded for the next 25 years".
- Passive has gone from nothing a little more than 30 years ago to "about half the marketplace in equities" (Feb 2026), which concentrates benchmarks. Institutional RFPs are rising as clients look for diversification away from them: "there's 53,000 and seven public companies. There's not seven public companies."
- 2010–2020 was hard for active managers: returns were skewed to a few stocks, and US 40 Act concentration limits capped how much of one stock a fund could hold. As the market broadens, "there's no excuses for active managers not to beat benchmarks", and he sees that broadening starting.
- AI: the firm has digitized 94 years of research, "Every single report on every single stock ever written at Capital Group". A PM can ask what mistakes they made in past environments like today's (rates coming down, valuations relatively high). Client uses include a "prep me" button for advisor meetings and instant translation.
- Private markets: build, buy or partner. Building means practicing "on your client's money" and distracting teams; buying is a cultural risk. So Capital Group partnered with KKR ("They've done it for 50 years"), which runs the private portion while Capital Group runs the public one, blended for clients.
- Plan to the 2031 centennial: manage money well; serve clients through whatever vehicle they want (SMAs, CITs, ETFs, blends with passive and private); simplify, since "Every business to some extent is bloated by its own bureaucracy. Period."; and keep people. Attrition is half the industry's and in low single digits for investment staff.
- Hiring takes 6–12 months and nobody has a veto. New investment professionals spend 3–6 months on an industry review before running money. The cultural fix he wants is to stop "associating excellence with speed or the lack thereof" and move at pace without losing quality.
