### 330. Paul Black — Growth stocks at WCM: the direction of the moat, rising ROIC and culture (Capital Allocators, 2018)

*Portfolio manager and co-CEO of WCM Investment Management in Laguna Beach (\~$26bn), which he joined in 1989 when it managed $200m; interviewed by Ted Seides in episode 51 (recorded \~2018, citing data to Dec 2017; posted on YouTube Sep 2022).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x_-VewxglUE)

**Takeaway:** Buying high-quality wide moats at a discount leads to value traps. What matters is the direction of the moat, seen in a rising ROIC, and a culture aligned with the competitive advantage; get those right and valuations look "ludicrously cheap" five and ten years out.

- Early lesson: at 25, running $200m at Bank of America, he moved clients from IBM into faster-growing Digital Equipment. "the most rapidly growing companies although they're kind of sexy and fun ultimately they can be massive destroyers of capital".
- Most active managers buy high-quality wide-moat businesses at a discount, so they all do the same thing and land in value traps. Nokia in 2007 had 53% phone share, a top-three brand, no debt, \~30% returns on capital for five years and sold at 60 cents on the dollar, but Apple's iOS and Android were eroding its moat.
- Every business is either getting stronger or weaker against its competitors. Bet on companies likely to widen their advantage over 5, 10 and 15 years; get that right and "any valuation work you do is going to look ludicrously cheap five and ten years out".
- ROIC direction beats level. Most managers screen for a 10–11% ROIC hurdle over five years, but he finds "a one-to-one correlation between the direction of the roic over a five-year period of time and stock performance" across quintiles. He prefers ROIC rising from 4% to 5, 6, 7, 8% over a stagnant 12%.
- Moat "typologies" cut across sectors. Outsourced R&D firms such as Core Labs (analysing oil and gas core samples, spun off from an integrated oil company) and Chr. Hansen (enzymes for yogurt and cheese) start as a cheap input and climb the value chain. So they deserve higher multiples than sector peers, and their margins "ought to grow from 25 to 30".
- Culture must fit the moat. Costco and Sam's Club look alike, but Costco has 4–5% same-store sales vs 1–2%, $1,000 vs $500 sales per square foot, 12% vs 50% employee turnover and a 12 vs 4 ROIC. Founder Jim Sinegal owned a lot of shares but never made more than $300,000 a year, against CEOs paid $20–40 million or given $200 million golden parachutes.
- Different businesses need different cultures. A retailer needs happy employees (Sam Walton's Walmart beat Sears, JC Penney and Mervyn's); a railroad such as Canadian National or Canadian Pacific needs accountability, since failure is costly. His five or six largest positions are where values and moat align best.
- Judging culture means building a mosaic: talk to people who left on good terms, suppliers, vendors and competitors, not just the CEO. Questions developed with Harvard's James Heskett: what would you tell a friend to succeed here, what's hard for new hires, tell me about your failures. At Whole Foods, John Mackey called the office buzz "an absence of fear".
- WCM's founder kept 100% of the equity and every decision, and the firm never passed $200m from 1976 to 1998. Four partners bought him out in 1998, shared equity with young talent, made pay transparent and adopted two values, gratitude and fun. Assets grew to $26bn.
- Worst mistake: in 2000–07 its US large-cap growth strategy bought wider, cheaper moats, "yahoo instead of google ebay instead of amazon and dell instead of apple". Those three "murdered" performance, and clients pulled $4bn. Earlier, in 1994, the founder's son, an engineer, suddenly took over the portfolio and had a bad year; the lesson was that the edge lies in qualitative work, not numbers.
- Most of Wall Street spends 95% of its time on numbers and DCF models, which "has zero competitive advantage". A focused \~30-stock portfolio gives the best chance to win. Of 2,000 active managers with 10-year records to Dec 2017, about half beat their markets; he would call it a quarter after survivorship bias.
- Downside: 33 stocks, never more than 4–5% in a name, and companies with growing moats that, unlike weaker rivals, aren't constrained by financial markets and can invest through bad times. The median holding is \~$35bn in market cap, about the same as 13 years earlier.
- Emerging markets (\~2018): own companies in "optimistic" countries; China is hard to bet against (WCM held Tencent). In Latin America, 10 of 12 companies blamed the macro and WCM bought the two that never mentioned it. Play the emerging middle class through companies selling to newly rich consumers, not through commodities as Jim Rogers suggested.
