### 331. Paul Enright — Business picking vs stock picking, and how a long-short book is really run (Capital Allocators, Aug 2022)

*Former Viking Global portfolio manager (\~12 years; ran its TMT and later its consumer team), now investing his own money through a single family office, Kranos Capital \[?\]; interviewed by Ted Seides.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZaUV7viJSZc)

**Takeaway:** A great business is not a thesis: returns come from the price paid against expectations, so pair business analysis with stock analysis (valuation, revisions, liquidity, technicals, factors). Then run the book actively: force-rank ideas, keep longs above 100% of capital, and flex gross exposure with the expected spread.

- Picking a business and picking a stock are "very, very, very different things". With rates at zero, stocks rose even while missing numbers, so people confused the two: "There was no dispersion on the way up, either."
- Quality alone is no edge: "who doesn't think Snowflake's a great business? Why is that a thesis?" He recalls that the half-life of companies is \~10 years and fewer than 5% compound above 20% for more than five years, so buying the great business everyone likes usually means overpaying. "How you make money is not the quality of the business you buy, but the price you pay for the quality of the business, period."
- Be thesis-driven instead: a minimum quality bar, then trends that are accelerating or a market that thinks things are "much worse than it is". An edge over 0–6 months is mostly self-delusion; work on a rolling 9–18 months, updating "very Bayesian" every day against what's priced in.
- Time horizon means nothing without price. Riding a stock up 300% that you know is "stupid expensive" through a 50% drawdown isn't patience. A 10–15% "punch in the face" is reasonable; 50–70% is not, especially after consensus numbers have already risen.
- The stock work: start from your own numbers and the financial statement where your edge sits, then valuation, hidden assets, supply and demand, earnings revisions and IRR. A great business whose earnings are "still 20% too high" is a falling knife; a great business down 90% after three estimate cuts (maybe a fourth) with 10x upside is a buy.
- Liquidity rules: don't own 20 days' volume of a thesis-driven idea, since you may have to change your mind. Never short a stock with more than 10% of its market cap sold short, or more than a day's volume of one that could double in a day. In a drawdown, gross exposure rises as capital shrinks, which "every hedge fund manager forgets that at least once a year".
- Build short books, not just short ideas: shorting only bad businesses ends in "a real bad bear trap". He used three buckets: offensive shorts against a business, shorts tied to the dynamics behind his longs, and a few market proxies when the market is expensive (23x earnings) or rates are zero. Baskets get hard limits, e.g. 10 SPACs at 100bp each, never above 12%.
- Keep gross longs above capital: longs can triple or quadruple, while shorts fall 100% at best and more likely 10–20%. A 20% unlevered long return becomes 24% at 120% gross; he ran 110–130% depending on the volatility target. Funds that stopped shorting to grow assets now run 85–95% gross long and don't get full value from their best ideas.
- Force-rank constantly, keeping the five or so best ideas at the top. If two of five 12% positions randomly run to 14–15%, ask whether they are really better now or whether to recycle into the other three. Weekly test: if the book were liquidated by mistake, "would I buy it back in the exact same form?" Going to cash has a high opportunity cost, and selling on a 10% stop-loss "doesn't make sense to me, either".
- The target math: 10% positions that each make 300–500bp, with losses held to \~50bp. A handful of 50bp losses shows you are taking enough risk. Before selling a stock up 30%, ask why: where did consensus go for each coming quarter and year?
- Flex gross with the expected spread (expected long return × size vs expected short return × size). After 20 points of spread in six months, with longs showing 15% upside and shorts flat, cut 200% gross to 160%: "spread's an accordion". Flexing gross helped his team stay top-decile from 2008 to 2016; platforms do the opposite: "the better I do, the more gross they want to give me".
- 2022: hedge-fund longs fell 50–60% in a market down 30 while shorts fell \~10, exposing a "bull market strategy" of high net and gross on longs that was really a factor bet. Size kills shorting too: short 1% of a company and you can escape a squeeze, short 14% and "you could be dead" (Volkswagen hurt even in an up 2008).
- Market structure: passive flows now exceed active ones, and a Fidelity analyst once owned 14% of a company. Huge multi-manager pods crowd into the same good businesses, fewer dealers will take a block off your hands, and trading costs have risen.
- His favourite setup: "when I can isolate a change in trajectory in revenue at the same time that there won't be a commensurate change in expenses", such as a company leaving an investment period or entering a pricing period. For a new industry: read the filings (and competitors') before any sell-side report, reduce it to unit economics, then take the outside view so you don't think you've "discovered plutonium".
