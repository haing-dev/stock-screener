### 332. Peter Harrison — Schroders at 200: family ownership, small accountable teams and "crossing a valley" (In Good Company, Oct 2024)

*CEO of Schroders (\~200 years old, 44% family-owned) shortly before handing over to Richard Oldfield; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen of Norges Bank Investment Management, a Schroders client for \~25 years.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DwHGDRCqiLI)

**Takeaway:** Harrison credits Schroders' survival to a family owner that takes a 20-year view. That gave him the licence to invest through the industry's disruption (passive, private markets, collapsing fees) instead of shrinking. His model for active management is small, local, accountable teams with great tools and data; his regret is not moving into private markets and acquisitions faster.

- Ten years of change (Oct 2024): massive growth of passive, a shift to private equity and private debt, a collapse in fees, plus new governance and climate agendas. US managers won through domestic scale, partnership structures and investing to grow: "you cannot shrink to grow". In private markets, the bigger you are, the bigger the moat.
- Family ownership (44%) brings capital discipline and patience. Bruno Schroder sat on the board for 60 years and said at nearly every meeting: "get it right on a 20-year view". The cost is that dilution is mentally hard for a family, so Schroders' share count is virtually the same as in 1959.
- "Crossing a valley": traditional equity businesses across the industry are "down 90% or actually don't exist anymore". Schroders doubled in size over 10 years, mainly through private markets and wealth, and the new businesses now grow faster than the old one declines. Tangen pushed back that fees and profits are down and the market cap is lower than when Harrison started.
- Scale: managers of 50 to 200 billion are "not viable" because they can't fund data science. Schroders' 770 is all active, private and wealth (high-fee, no big passive chunk). His regret is not being "more bold": he wishes he had entered private markets earlier and bought more teams. Schroders bought only small firms to protect its culture, since public and private cultures clash, but "the disruption in our industry has been way quicker" than anyone expected.
- Teams: small, local and led by brilliant people with great tools, grown internally rather than hired as rock stars (that is the Citadel and Millennium model). Give them room to be wrong "for three or four years at a time", but understand the risks they take and the path dependency of returns.
- Great risk-takers know why they were wrong. A China position can fail because of state-owned enterprises or government stimulus, because it was the wrong company, or because the moat disappeared and "you end up clinging on to it for too long". Watch price action and how the business is changing. People who waver rarely navigate long-term change.
- Accountability: small teams beat a whole-organization process, in which one central oil-price forecast can blow up the whole system. Firms run that way "can go through long downward spirals".
- Tools: 35 years ago the edge was keying 10 years of accounts into a spreadsheet to build a two-year earnings model; now that takes two seconds. Today's edge is processing unstructured data on what companies and supply chains are really doing. Bloomberg has \~100,000 functions, and a great PM might use 30.
- UK markets: the UK had one of the largest pools of domestic savings in the world and "regulated that out of existence" into government bonds, putting consumer protection first, and the equity market shrank with huge outflows over 15 years. A governance code also rewarded voting against management. London's private-market boom runs on foreign capital. "300 billion in tax advantage cash savings and virtually no Financial advice", yet "if you don't take risk you don't earn return".
- Brexit undermined confidence in the UK as a place for long-term decisions and caused two or three years of hiatus, followed by the Truss budget and the gilts crisis. Reforms (new listing rules, a return to comply-or-explain) are helping, but "scars take a while to heal", and "in the UK there is no money".
- Public markets are shrinking fast (he cites \~40% fewer companies over 20 years), and "the availability of alpha is shrinking". Innovation in climate transition, synthetic biology and AI will be funded privately, so wealth clients will move into private markets. Europe needs a single capital market to compete with the US but won't get one: "too politically difficult".
- ESG has "passed Peak debate" and been politicized, but has gone "from hand waving to data". GAAP treats all profits alike, yet a tobacco maker creates cancer, an oil company carbon and a soft-drinks maker diabetes, so impact-adjusted profits differ widely.
- Asia (Oct 2024): China is in a major correction after its property crisis, with cash piling up in bank deposits. Schroders' China business is "losing US money", and the size of that bet was a mistake, though he is happy with it on a 20-year view. India looks great now, the reverse of 10 years ago, "and I'm sure in 10 years time it will move again". Japan's corporate-governance change is "a sleeping giant".
- Leadership: he realized he wasn't a good enough investor. As CEO he had to stop driving from the back seat and melt the "permafrost" of layers that think change doesn't concern them: "shine daylight into dark Corners it's the best disinfectant".
