### 335. Rob Vinall — Emerging Manager Day 2017, part 1: get the fund's structure right on day one (Robert Vinall, Mar 2017)

*Founder of RV Capital, which runs the concentrated Business Owner Fund (launched 2008); part 1 of a day for emerging fund managers, on how he started and \~10 lessons on structuring a fund.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5tYSjP3IRAs)

**Takeaway:** How a fund is set up decides which investors it attracts, and they decide whether the manager can stay patient, so the structure must be right "on day one". His recipe: a structure that allows concentration, no marketing, ultra-low costs, no promoter equity, full transparency and investors who find you and self-select.

- Origin (2002): after the dot-com bust, former high-flying internet IPOs traded "at huge discounts to their cash", some with decent businesses on top. He bought them before he had heard of Buffett or Graham; a colleague then found the Intelligent Investor passage on stocks trading below net cash after 1929.
- At a Swiss activist fund (from \~2004) that took very large stakes in illiquid companies, he learned the value of scuttlebutt: visiting suppliers and customers to work out what a company really does.
- Solving the track-record "chicken and egg dilemma" (2006): capital was plentiful but people who could generate ideas were scarce, so he started as an "outsourced" buy-side analyst. The Rentrop family office paid him \~€4,000 a month plus a share of the upside, and in 2008 the family money he already managed seeded the Business Owner Fund.
- Why "Business Owner": activism means "always basically fighting someone", since the manager almost by definition "has to be incompetent or dishonest", normally both. He flipped it: companies with thoughtful, engaged long-term owners are better companies, yet often trade at a discount to comparable companies. The market dislikes an anchor shareholder, liquidity is lower and there is no restructuring "fantasy", so you get a "superior class of company" at an "inferior valuation".
- The structure must fit the strategy: "having a diversified portfolio is a guarantee that you're going to be roughly in line with the market". In Europe only a hedge-fund structure allowed concentration, so his long-only fund was "a product without a market" at first; 10 years on, that difficulty is an entry barrier.
- Don't waste time marketing. Pitches failed for reasons like a fund "only 10 million" big, so he gave up "more out of frustration than strategy". The investors who later called him knew he was small, young and concentrated: they had self-selected, and most invested.
- Keep costs ultra-low. Offices in Mayfair, an analyst and a receptionist push managers into short-termism; he hears "we don't have the luxury" of waiting because "we have bills to pay".
- Turn down promoters who offer seed capital for 40–50% of your business. The promoter is "purely short-term financially interested", and a successful manager comes to resent "paying a 50% tax". David Swensen's book on portfolio management also says he avoids funds with a bad ownership structure.
- Communicate openly. Investors want "a good emotional experience" as well as returns, and a manager who shows up after 5 years claiming 20% a year meets skepticism unless the philosophy and holdings were explained in advance. Passed-around letters also drew like-minded managers to him.
- Say no to the wrong investor: anyone who needs daily liquidity treats the fund "as a bank account". Short-term investors get excited by good monthly numbers, bring in like-minded friends and leave after a couple of bad quarters, so the referral flywheel runs in reverse. For the same reason he would never hire a marketer, whose feedback pushes toward more liquidity and diversification.
- Seek responsibility: early hires of compliance officers, analysts and co-fund managers often serve to diffuse responsibility, while investors want to know who decides.
- Treat the fund as a business with a moat. The moat is a loyal investor base that allows "a very long-term perspective", which is "incredibly rare"; then the easy opportunity is buying great companies when they are a little out of favor and waiting.
- Seek partners who challenge you. His partners meet him at least once a year and probe each thesis, which exposes inconsistencies: "I'm trying to build a long-term track record".
- Screen implication: Vinall's case for owner-run companies (better businesses, often at a discount to peers, with thinner liquidity) backs the Owner-Operators screen.
