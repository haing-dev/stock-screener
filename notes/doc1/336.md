### 336. Rob Vinall, Gil \[?\], James Ferguson, Nate Chesley & Jens Große-Allermann — Emerging Manager Day 2017, part 2: what allocators want from a new fund manager (Robert Vinall, Mar 2017)

*Q&A between emerging managers and four early backers of RV Capital: Gil \[?\], a German one-man allocator who spent 10 years at Morgan Stanley; James Ferguson of Global Endowment Management (GEM); Nate Chesley of MIT's endowment; and Jens Große-Allermann of the family office that seeded Vinall.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XroaS6f7pIs)

**Takeaway:** Short track records prove little, so allocators judge temperament, integrity and setup, and watch how a manager behaves when down 50%. Fee terms and broken promises to cap assets show a manager's hand; Gil \[?\] wants investors to keep at least half of any value added. Vinall dropped screens for low P/E, low P/B and 52-week lows: alone they tell nothing, and the cheap pool is mostly below-average businesses.

- Track records (Gil \[?\]): anything under 10 years "doesn't give you that much evidence". A good 5-year record from a stranger is "almost worthless", since "you only get to see the monkeys that you know hit". Five good years from a manager you backed at the start do tell you something, and even 10 years gives "maybe 70% confidence", so a qualitative judgment is unavoidable.
- Temperament (Ferguson): The Intelligent Investor says to buy more when a stock halves while intrinsic value falls only 10%, but doing it after losing 50% is hard. GEM spends less time on track records and more on getting to know people, asking how it would want to respond when a concentrated manager is down 50%. In the last 18 months it added significantly to one such manager (process intact after pushing hard on the endowment effect) and redeemed from another, who was "almost visibly shaken" and changed a lot to "stabilize the portfolio".
- Inputs over outputs (Chesley): prices are outputs; temperament, behavior and focus are inputs. If a holding gaps down 50% and its intrinsic value is down 50%, "there's not a decision to be made"; if it is "complete irrationality" and you love the assets, doubling down can be obvious. Managers should evolve, but a partner shouldn't coach "during someone's back swing".
- Bad stretches (Gil \[?\]): almost everyone has one, typically 1–3 years ("Buffett was a huge outlier"), and he forgives mistakes; even when his own pick turns out badly, he sticks with the manager as "the way to punish myself for a bad decision". The real risk is drift: "the evolution is usually to the worst" as hubris, analysts and assets grow, in \~90% of the cases he has seen. "I have never seen a firm that started out small and started then to hire analysts uh to get better".
- Fees (Gil \[?\]): he will pay high fees for outperformance, but the investor who risks the capital must keep "at least 50% of that value added". With the market at 6–8%, 2-and-20 needs the manager to outperform by 12% to be fair, and he won't take a meeting on \~1.5% plus 10–20% over a zero hurdle ("calling zero a hurdle ... is obnoxious"). By the same test, Vinall's 1% plus 10% over 6% needs 3% or more of outperformance. Advice: "either be cheap or be super Fair".
- Alignment signals (endowment panelists): a budget-based management fee (salary, travel and research passed straight through) is well aligned and falls to single-digit basis points as capital compounds. A manager who vowed to stop at $500M, then said $1bn, then $2bn, shows his hand just as fees do. Vinall: the fee structure "reveals your hand"; had he copied 2-and-20 on day one, "I don't think uh any of these guys would be sitting here today".
- Generalists win (Gil \[?\]): "Specialists disappoint". What matters is knowing "when you don't know"; only a few ideas look "idiot proof", which needs a field of "literally thousands of different companies". Chesley: "asset allocation is an output not an input", and MIT uses specialists only where domain complexity is high (China, India, biotech). All four panelists give managers freedom rather than style boxes.
- Why MIT uses managers (Chesley): it runs $18.5bn in its endowment pool (2017) plus a pension fund and outsources for competitive advantage; there is "no way" it beats Sequoia, Greylock or Benchmark in early-stage venture. It avoids a direct stock book because trading on its own account while demanding full transparency from managers would be "a huge conflict", yet \~80–90% of its time with managers goes on discussing businesses.
- Vinall stopped screening: 10 years ago he screened for 52-week lows, low P/B and low P/E, and has "stopped doing that completely". In isolation such a number "tells you absolutely nothing", and because the market is mostly right, a low-P/E universe is mostly "below average businesses", "the wrong pool" for him. Ideas now come from following a company's customers, suppliers and competitors (switching to the better one, a generalist's edge) and from like-minded managers who pre-filter ideas.
- Independent conviction (Vinall): he never discloses an investment before the position is built. During research he asks seed backers Jens Große-Allermann and Norman Rentrop not to give their view unless they know of fraud or a dishonest manager (the "tsunami" exception), and after investing he asks all four backers not to say if they dislike a holding, so each idea comes "out of my conviction".
- Transparency (Gil \[?\] vs Vinall): Gil doesn't need full transparency, finds stock stories "always convincing" but mostly a waste of time, and warns that public reasoning breeds "consistency bias". Vinall welcomes the commitment his stated principles create (e.g., only backing managers with integrity), avoids public fights and writes two letters a year, drafted a couple of months early to test what will still matter.
- Down-market marketing (Vinall): touting a fund that rose in a falling market is "an unbelievably stupid thing to do", because it draws capital that has self-selected to be jittery. When his fund was up or flat in down years, he told investors to expect a long-only fund to fall in down years. An endowment panelist praised a manager's slide titled "why you should not invest in this fund" (e.g., if you can't tolerate a 50% drawdown).
- Base rates and setup (Gil \[?\]): "at least 90% fail", mostly from Vinall's structural mistakes rather than lack of brains, and copying others is "a disaster waiting to happen" because you learn only from your own mistakes. Keep the setup "super cheap", allow "10 15 maybe 20 years" to reach a decent size, and consider a managed account with one lead investor (Vinall started that way). A panelist cites \~$3tn of hedge fund assets in \~10,000 funds (2017), with \~1,000 opening and closing each year.
- Benchmarks (Gil \[?\]): beat the MSCI World over 10+ years "by several percentage points", not 50 basis points; he never looks at a manager's 1- or 3-year numbers and calls quarterly letters "stupid". Vinall quotes the DAX only because his German seed investors used it; he owns no DAX company and aims to beat all major markets over time.
