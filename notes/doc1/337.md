### 337. Rob Vinall & Wolfgang Grenke — RV Capital investor meeting 2018: selling too soon, culture over moat, Shake Shack and Ryman (Robert Vinall, Jan 2018)

*RV Capital's annual investor meeting in Engelberg: an interview with Wolfgang Grenke, founder of the fund's largest holding Grenke (formerly Grenkeleasing), then Vinall's Q&A with his investors; the captions are poor and much of the Grenke interview is unintelligible.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sYJH2RsgtFU)

**Takeaway:** Vinall thinks the common mistake is selling great compounders too early, not holding too long, so he holds through expensive-looking stretches and would sell on something like technological disruption, not a 20% jump in a quarter. He now weights culture and people above competitive advantage, and judges returns after allowing for growth costs that run through the P&L.

- Grenke was \~30–35% of the fund at launch and is still the largest position at \~20% (Jan 2018). In the interview, Vinall stresses Grenke's credit scoring "using the data to continually improve" and notes that "a lot of your competitors were wiped out through the financial crisis". Grenke mentions being in Toronto and sees Hong Kong as "the entry to the Chinese" market.
- Emotions help: "emotions can play a very important role in investing". Liking the people who run a business makes him hold, and while that risks not selling, "the risk of selling a company too early" is bigger: experienced investors call it their biggest mistake. "people tend to sell too soon", and constant re-ranking creates cost and risk through turnover.
- Sell discipline: he would sell Grenke on "technological disruption" that fundamentally changed the business, not because the stock rose 20% in a quarter. He builds in room to change his mind: his letters say that if he is wrong, "I'll be the first to admit it", which is hard inside a committee or with junior colleagues after your job.
- Paying up: investors underestimate "how quickly really the earnings can be transformed"; it "really doesn't make a whole lot of difference when you pay a higher price" if the expected compounding comes about. Linear DCF models miss that "in a really good company there's all kinds of things that are gonna happen over time".
- Returns understate growth: asked about a holding (apparently Grenke) on 15% ROE and 75% retained earnings, he says dividing last year's net income by equity gives "roughly a 15%" but "I don't think that shows you anything". The P&L carries growth costs (new countries, new offices, new product areas) that earn nothing yet, so he suspects the underlying return is "much much higher".
- Research: first pass is the annual report and website. If he is very interested, he reads "all of the available reports going back as far as possible", calls competitors and the company, and reads everything on the industry. The best book illuminates an unfamiliar industry: "Setting the Table" by Shake Shack's Danny Meyer took him from knowing nothing about restaurants to knowing quite a lot (the fund recently bought Shake Shack).
- Access as a filter: the portfolio is \~10 companies, 3–4 very large ones (also "sources of liquidity if there's a crisis") and \~7 smaller ones where he has "spent many many days" with management, salespeople and others. He used to fly to New Zealand for a one-hour meeting and pad the trip with companies he cared less about; now he asks for a full day or two (it motivates employees), and a company's willingness is "a good selection criteria".
- Culture over moat: his emphasis is "shifting more towards the culture and the people" and away from competitive advantage. The trade-off: the best-culture company may not have the widest moat, but the best people can "exercise their excellence"; you can't watch every employee, so doing the right thing has to come "through culture".
- Shake Shack (Jan 2018): comps matter most where new units ramp slowly; a new car-sales branch opens at \~60% of its future revenue, breaking even or losing money. A new Shake Shack is "a sensation" with queues and earns \~70% on the capital employed from day one; as with "a savings account that pays you seventy percent", what matters is not 70% vs 68% but whether it lasts. Its brand is known in Asia and Europe, "very unusual for a restaurant concept", and he sees "a lot of pricing power", with a "win-win" from keeping prices lower.
- Ryman Healthcare: "the opportunity is almost unlimited". It started in New Zealand, and its first Australian city, Melbourne, has roughly New Zealand's population, so the whole model can in theory be "replicated in Melbourne", then in Australia's other big cities. Its culture is "a multi-headed Hydra" with "hundreds of heads".
- Walking away: he "followed very closely for a number of years" a company whose founder-CEO seemed to treat it as his life's work. Over lunch, the founder said staying was "a question of negotiation" with the supervisory board over pay, and he had "lots of opportunities elsewhere". Vinall passed.
- Engagement: information should flow "both directions", so he passes on ideas he sees elsewhere (e.g., in China) to managements. Long-term holders must be as vocal as traders, telling managers "we don't care what this quarters results are".
- China VIEs: investors "don't have any ownership rights", so the risk is real but "a small risk" that has to be priced. For his Chinese holding, growth far above a comparable US opportunity (he cites \~60% a year in search) as China comes online offset the risk: "the risk probably paid for in the price I paid".
- Sourcing: screens, newspapers and conferences were "quite an inefficient way to do things", since most companies don't fit. Ideas now come from his network of investors and emerging managers, who know how he invests.
