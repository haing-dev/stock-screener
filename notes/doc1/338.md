### 338. Rob Vinall, Nate Chesley, Gil \[?\], James Ferguson & Gerhard Schöningh — Emerging Managers Meeting 2018: building the fund, and what allocators watch for (Robert Vinall, Jan 2018)

*Nate Chesley of MIT's endowment interviews Vinall (RV Capital, Business Owner Fund) on how he built the fund; then a panel of allocators takes questions from emerging managers: Gil \[?\], a German one-man allocator who backed Vinall early; James Ferguson of Global Endowment Management (GEM); and Gerhard Schöningh, a fund co-founder turned private investor (he took over Berlin's racecourse).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cGN2A84tADM)

**Takeaway:** Vinall's cheap, concentrated, one-man setup was built by his seed investor for capital allocation, not marketing, and it let him stay the course in 2008–09. As Gil \[?\] puts it, "the setup doesn't lie": fee terms, staff, kept or broken promises to close, the manager's own money in the fund and his behavior under pressure reveal more than a short track record.

- Origin (Vinall): after the early-2000s crash, tech stocks that had lost 90–95% traded far below net cash: "the company has 100 of cash and the market value is 50 that feels cheap to me". Starting out (2006–08), a fund's running costs were "a little bit less than a hundred thousand euros" a year, 2% of €5M or 1% of €10M "lost before you even got started", so he hired no analyst, trader or receptionist (€50–100k each).
- Fee math (Vinall): the fund started with \~€7M, so the 1% fee gave \~€70,000, \~€50,000 after travel, and "it gave me my independence". He calls the fee structure "the single most important" setup decision because it "reveals how you think about" investors. His 10% performance fee sits over an absolute 6% hurdle, about what equities return over time, so no bonus is paid when the fund is down 20% and a benchmark is down 50%. Gil \[?\] also prefers an absolute 6% hurdle.
- Alone in the crisis (Vinall): starting in September 2008, months before the crisis peaked around February 2009, was lucky. At a Zurich conference in January 2009 people looked at him "like there was some kind of an idiot" for calling stocks cheap. Working alone kept him out of the "contagion", and working from home with little overhead let him "stay the course however long I wanted", while managers with Bond Street leases suffered.
- Built for allocation (Vinall): the Rentrop family office, whose account he ran well in 2006–08, created the fund in 2008 around returns, not marketability. Fewer than 10 stocks in a hedge-fund wrapper was "a disaster" for marketing but right for compounding: with excellent returns "there's never going to be a shortage of people that want to invest with you". After \~5 years he began turning away poor fits, such as intermediaries investing other people's pools of money.
- Network instead of staff (Vinall): his network acts as his colleagues, "much more talented people than anyone could ever collect in one firm", and they would lose independence on a payroll. About 5 years earlier a friend in his network told him that Credit Acceptance was "a much better business" than his leasing holding; a few months later they visited Detroit together. Published holdings widen the sounding board: "people like nothing more than to point out a mistake".
- Better idea flow (Vinall): ten years earlier he skimmed "the 52-week low list" of a hundred companies on numbers like price-to-book, "hoping to find the needle in the haystack". Referrals from like-minded investors work far better, and trips now cover one company and its competitors, not 15–20 companies. "a good investment comes from some kind of variant perception", often an insight imported from another geography or industry.
- Crypto (Vinall, Jan 2018): his first view, "a complete Ponzi scheme", may hold in the short to medium term, but blockchain could be "very very disruptive to a lot of existing businesses", just as in 2002 nobody knew the internet would produce Google and Facebook. He won't own currencies: the "currency is the golden egg and the company of the golden goose".
- Capacity promises: when MIT invested at the start of 2012, Vinall agreed to close at $400M on a five-year view. At \~$300M in early 2017 he expected to reach $400M by year-end, but a \~50% USD return took the fund to \~$450M; given compounding, a cap "can't be set in stone" forever. Gil \[?\] writes down what a manager says about closing and checks years later; announcing a close while opening another fund is "cheating".
- "the setup doesn't lie" (Gil \[?\]): letters are "all words", but the setup, fee structure included, is a fact. He has "a strong preference for one-man shows" with "no institutionalization whatsoever", because he wants to stay invested "30 or 40 years" and a team will change. "partnerships usually work when the business is mediocre"; success brings "character drift": lifestyle change, laziness, analysts and raised caps.
- Red flags: Gil \[?\] lost faith in a manager who raised far more than expected and answered an emailed question with "I don't do one-on-ones"; "arrogance is a form of stupidity", and track records "never get better". Ferguson: the first warning was "a significant swing" in the manager's gross and net exposures, which managers are "almost certain" to get wrong; GEM redeemed because he "wasn't wired to run towards the fire".
- Track records and transparency (Gil \[?\]): "anything less than 10 years is not worth a whole lot", so picking managers is "intelligent guesswork". He thinks "transparency never helps performance" and makes it harder to change your mind (he cites Vinall's public Facebook case), like campaign promises. Vinall: opacity was a hedge-fund "marketing gimmick", and documenting ideas in advance builds "a more credible track record than a purely quantitative one"; young managers should be transparent because nobody cares yet.
- Allocator incentives (Ferguson): "asset management doesn't scale"; large organizations have "quite big diseconomies", and hiring an established manager with a proven record is a "defensible way to fail". The riskiest share of a fund is a middle one (\~40%): redeeming hurts the business without closing it. Gil \[?\] refuses to be a vital part of any fund or to accept a vehicle built for him ("I have to reserve the freedom to walk out any day"), except for a tiny early manager like Vinall.
- Skin in the game: seed capital in exchange for a stake in the manager's business is "a rotten deal" and "a total no-go" for Gil \[?\]. He wants a meaningful share of the manager's wealth in the fund (50% is fine, 100% is "mildly stupid" for a big fund, 10% "raises some questions") and asks for the percentage, not the amount. Ferguson avoids vehicles built because running a hedge fund "is a great compensation scheme" rather than to compound the manager's own wealth.
- Generalists: Gil \[?\] says specialists often disappoint (narrow universe, overconfidence, falling in love with their companies), Buffett is "the prototype of a generalist", and "any information or any data point in isolation is worthless" without wide context. GEM's managers are almost all generalists, except where technical depth is needed (biotech, venture, distressed). Schöningh is warier of managers who claim they can invest in anything ("I find that off-putting").
