### 339. Rachel Botsman — Trust: "to do what?", owning mistakes and the trust pause (NBIM, May 2024)

*Trust researcher and author of What's Mine Is Yours and Who Can You Trust?, in a bonus episode with Nicolai Tangen (CEO of NBIM) on what trust is, how it breaks and how leaders and institutions rebuild it.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1bEarAE7dkM)

**Takeaway:** Trust is a belief that someone or something will do a specific thing, and it is what lets people take risks. Leaders rebuild it after a mistake by owning it and changing culture and incentives, not by blaming the system; misaligned interests, surveillance and frictionless speed wear it down.

- Trust is a belief, so it is contextual: "whenever we talk about trust, we should follow up with to do what?" "You cannot navigate uncertainty without trust." People trust by default and wait for warning signals. Transactional trust is quick to tear down; relational trust, built over time, is harder to break, so a failure handled in a "human and relational" way can earn trust back.
- Misalignment is "the deepest deepest trust problem to fix": trust in government is "at an all-time low" because people no longer believe its interests align with theirs. Applied to NBIM: "those percentage points, those increases, they don't mean anything to the average person"; "the trust bridge is the narrative of what's in it for me". Tangen: "knowledge builds trust"; people who listen to the fund's podcast trust it more, and he calls NBIM "the most transparent fund in the world" (it publishes how it votes).
- After a mistake, admitting it plus accountability can raise trust, but "accountability to fix trust has to move to change and action". It backfires when leaders "point to the system" (the algorithm, a product failure); Boeing and Facebook/Meta struggle to recover "until there is accountability in the character side of trust, not the capability side": culture, incentives and reporting lines, not just the product.
- Trustworthiness has a capability side (reliability, competence) and a character side (integrity, empathy), weighted by role: a teacher needs empathy most, a lawyer integrity. "the most empathetic leaders often score very high on curiosity": asking questions people have never been asked is a scalable way to show empathy. Consistency matters more than intensity (James Clear: "Intensity makes a great story, but it's consistency that leads to progress"): people should know what to expect of you.
- Deep trust forms in moments of vulnerability: a failed project, "an investment has gone wrong", a media attack. First find the real trust issue (fear of losing something triggers the urge to "put me back in control"), then make the person feel heard, then act. Leaders "get that listening phase right, and then there is no action": a boss once called her "totally unemployable" as a compliment, then did nothing ("feedback with no catch").
- Remote work and surveillance: the gap left by no face-to-face contact fills with negative stories and "productivity performance" (staying visible on Slack and email). She believes trust can't be built virtually in the same way, and leaders report staff "uncomfortable sitting physically in a room with a client". Tracking tools are "incredible signals of low trust" that measure "how people physically show up", not value: "if you have to implement any kind of surveillance system, you've got a culture problem".
- Speed and the "trust pause": Tangen's rule is that an email answered within 1 minute can be one sentence, after an hour it needs a paragraph and after a day a page. Botsman says responsiveness is not the problem; speed becomes "the enemy of trust" when people pass on unread articles or click through consent boxes and scam texts. "if everything is frictionless, our tolerance for friction goes down", and we give trust away too easily.
- AI and the arc of trust: trust ran from local to institutional (contracts, insurance, unions), then distributed via platforms, and now a "fourth phase, which I call auto sapient trust", where we can't tell a person from a machine. People already trust AI for information, but in most sectors (finance included) "they don't trust it for decision-making". Judges who a year earlier barred lawyers from using AI to prepare briefs reversed course "in less than a year".
