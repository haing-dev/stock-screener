### 341. Robert Wallace — Running Stanford's endowment: fewer, better partners and getting in early (NBIM, Aug 2025)

*President and CEO of Stanford Management Company, which runs Stanford's endowment of more than $40bn; he trained under David Swensen at Yale, led a London-based investment firm and joined Stanford in 2015. Interview with Nicolai Tangen (CEO of NBIM).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NAGBpbOgPig)

**Takeaway:** An endowment paying out \~5% a year against 3–4% higher-education inflation needs \~9% returns, hence \~70% in equities. Wallace's edge is concentration and partnership: he cut Stanford's managers from 300 to under 100, picks them for rigor, temperament and alignment, gets in early and adds money when good partners have lagged.

- Goals: Stanford pays out "about $2 billion" this year (\~5% of the endowment), which helps make it one of the cheapest private four-year colleges and lets "nearly 90%" of undergrads graduate without student debt. The second goal is to be at least as supportive of all future generations. With a 5% payout and higher-education inflation of 3–4% (it runs above consumer prices), "you need an expected return in your investment program of around 9%".
- Hence an equity bias: "roughly 70% of the portfolio is invested in equities" and 30% in less risky assets. Peers range from 60/40 to 80/20, so 70/30 is "roughly probably in the middle of the pack"; endowments differ mainly in execution, i.e. what fills the 70 and the 30.
- From David Swensen: think from first principles, with each step backed by "logic informed by data"; that rigor separates investment from speculation when decisions run "five or seven or 10 or even 12 or 15 years out". Watch "the intersection of supply and demand and fear and greed", and be part scientist, part psychologist. Judge external partners on skill, discipline, temperament, character and alignment of interest, which Swensen "paid enormous attention to".
- Concentrate partners, not asset classes. In 2015 the \~$20bn portfolio had "300 external partners", and real estate (8% of assets) alone had 53, the product of a culture of "very diversified kind of low conviction investment". Stanford put 265 partners into liquidation, kept 35 and added 50, aiming for fewer than 100 "with whom we could develop a strong, trustful uh knowledgeable relationship".
- Be contrarian with your partners: lean in "when their opportunity set is particularly attractive", which "usually means their performance is has recently been poor". Sending money to a lagging manager needs deep knowledge of them and confidence in their alignment; Stanford does it "routinely", which is only possible with few partners.
- Is the endowment model "dead"? (Aug 2025) After a near-historic bull market since 2008, US valuations are "at near all-time highs"; on several metrics only 1999 was higher, with multiple expansion on top of strong earnings growth. In such a run a diversified portfolio looks "a little less good" than one all in US equities.
- Private markets pay only at the top. Outcomes are widely dispersed and access is expensive, so net of fees "the middle of the range is not very good", and half of outcomes sit in a "huge left tail" that does worse than US public equity. In venture, "10 or 12 in the United States" generate "the substantial majority of all the profits" in a cycle.
- Get in early. Investing $40–50bn with a large alternatives allocation, "You need to be early. You need to create your own opportunities": disciplined partners cap their capital to fit their opportunity set, so "you're never going to get enough of your endowment to work to have a high conviction portfolio if you're not there first". Stanford's Silicon Valley network lets it create its own venture deals, which he "could not have done" from London.
- Sourcing and diligence: almost all partners are found directly or referred "in some sort of qualified way" through the network, "very very seldom" by brokers or cold calls. Many in-person meetings cover process, motivation, judgment, ethics and alignment; he wants people who "treat Stanford's capital like it's their own capital or better". References (past colleagues, peers, competitors) and a quantitative look at the record go into a "40 or 50 page investment memo", mostly text. Writing it down still catches gaps: "you write the memo and you read the memo and you've missed something", even after 21 years as a CIO.
- Turn-offs: being "sloppy", throwing capital around "without discipline", or being "out to make money for yourself". Turn-on: Chris Hohn. Yale was TCI's first investor, with Wallace as point person; the first meeting (London, 2003, just after Hohn left Perry) ran \~3 hours, and Yale spent "six or nine months with him" before he took anyone's capital. Hohn could isolate "the two or three or four things that really really really mattered" and weight them correctly. Great investors chase the work, not wealth: "the asset gatherers focus on the money".
- When to exit a partner: it loses discipline, outgrows its opportunity set or loses motivation; Stanford's own work was incomplete; or the opportunity, or access to it, goes away. China made Stanford "a tremendous amount of money" in a "wildly inefficient market", but US–China politics made it far less accessible to a US nonprofit: "the opportunity sets as rich as ever but for Stanford it's not". Average partner tenure over his 20-year CIO career: "12 or 12 plus years", maybe closer to 15.
- Team: one or two analysts a year straight out of Stanford undergrad, from "dozens and dozens" of applicants, spotted through a 20-student seminar and internships; they become "fantastic investors" after 7–10 years. He looks for curiosity and the ability to combine quantitative and qualitative data. Risk appetites can differ as long as each person is "on the efficient frontier". Deals are worked iteratively in small teams (not by consensus; he decides as CIO), and asset allocation is reviewed once a year and seldom changed.
- Ballet and patience: he danced professionally for 16 years from 17 (hired by Mikhail Baryshnikov at American Ballet Theatre), then entered Yale as a freshman at 32 and interned in its investments office. Swensen called being the only boy in his first ballet class "your first and most important lesson in supply and demand." Both crafts mean daily work that pays off "five or 10 years later". Advice: follow bottom-up signals (what you love, people you learn from) over top-down CV-building; he turned down Harvard Business School to stay with Swensen.
