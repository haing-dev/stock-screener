### 342. Ruth Porat — Alphabet's full-stack AI bet, capex returns and lessons from 2008 (NBIM, Mar 2025)

*President and chief investment officer of Alphabet, which she joined as CFO in 2015 after serving as Morgan Stanley's CFO, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen; NBIM owned 1.3% of Alphabet, \~$30bn.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hcWSDxrE1uI)

**Takeaway:** Porat argues Alphabet's edge in AI is the full stack: talent, models, its own TPU chips and platforms used by billions. AI capex returns rest on a "theoretical economic upside" that must still materialize, so Alphabet keeps investing for the long run while cutting the capex denominator through chip and model efficiency.

- Search "remains so core". Generative AI widens the types of queries and deepens engagement (AI Overviews), and enterprise use is the second route to monetization. The cautious start was about quality: with hallucination risk, a parent checking a child's medicine dose needs answers where "there can be no margin for error".
- Chips: Alphabet works closely with Nvidia and runs both GPUs and its own TPUs. TPUs deliver the training performance it wants and better energy efficiency, "a cost benefit". Trillium, its latest TPU, is \~67% more efficient than the prior one.
- Capex returns (Mar 2025): asked whether tech now accepts lower returns, she cites \~$20 trillion that AI could add to global GDP in a decade (\~$4 trillion in the US). That needs companies to radically rethink their operations, not just add chatbots. She sees "a self-calibrating element to the pace of investment", and Alphabet works on "the efficiency of that denominator all the capex".
- Capital allocation (Tangen: \~$100bn of cash): "if you don't invest for the long run you're sewing the seeds of your own destruction". The early mantra was 70% core, 20% adjacent, 10% moonshots. X, the moonshot factory, incubated Waymo, Wing (drone delivery, working with Walmart) and Intrinsic (a robotics operating system), and its discipline is to "kill things fast".
- Waymo (Mar 2025): 200,000 paid rides a week in San Francisco, LA, Phoenix and Austin, with a pilot in Japan. The original thesis: more than a million people die on the roads every year. Whether people will still own cars in 10 years is "too early to call".
- Quantum: the Willow chip did a computation in under 5 minutes that would take the best supercomputers 10 septillion years. On commercial use: "whether we're three years five years you know hard to say".
- Resilience: subsea cables built as trunks with nodes (e.g., Africa to Australia), data-center redundancy and zero-trust cybersecurity: "it's better to remain paranoid". A third of the world is still not online.
- Crisis lesson 1 (at Treasury with Hank Paulson from Jul 2008, on Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and AIG): find your greatest vulnerability early and protect it. For banks it is liquidity: in Sep–Oct 2008 "you couldn't procure liquidity durable liquidity if you tried", but six months earlier you could have.
- Crisis lesson 2: AIG's crisis began in its UK derivatives subsidiary, not the insurance business, and nobody could see the risk: "you would not drive a car with mud on the windshield". In a crisis there are no good choices, so pick "the least worst and just keep moving", with a team that can connect issues across the business.
- Google's own biggest vulnerability is failing to innovate and invest long term, and a dinosaur named Stan on campus is the reminder. There is "a sense every year that this is the last great year": in 2015 the mantra was "the desktop is dead", and market cap has gone from 400 billion to "north of two trillion".
- Innovation: Sundar Pichai revived Google Labs (small "Scrappy" teams), which produced NotebookLM. Google Lens handles 20 billion camera searches a month. The ethos is "why not": Demis Hassabis set out to predict all 200 million protein structures when one used to take a year or two.
- AI in health: a Google tool for early metastatic breast cancer found 20% more cancers in an 80,000-sample test with no false positives. Early detection is an "assist", not a cure-all.
- Work with data: "you can't argue with data", and "don't give me flat data put it into a sensitivity analysis" so that assumptions about growth and investment can be debated.
