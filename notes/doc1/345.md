### 345. Seth Klarman — Timeless value investing: forced sellers, catalysts and an untested "everything bubble" (Capital Allocators with Ted Seides, Jul 2023)

*Seth Klarman of the Baupost Group (founded 1982, \~$27bn), author of Margin of Safety and editor of the 7th edition of Graham and Dodd's Security Analysis, interviewed by Ted Seides.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wHsEt6MchhE)

**Takeaway:** Mispricing comes from human behavior and rules (forced sellers, index changes, downgrades, funds that must close), so Baupost searches "miles wide", digs "miles deep", sizes up when a catalyst exists and hedges the risk of a rich market multiple. In mid-2023 he called it one of the "weirdest" times in 40 years: an "everything bubble" not yet worked off, high moral hazard and private credit that has never been stress-tested.

- Mutual Shares lessons (from Jan 1979, under Max Heine and Mike Price): his first case, Telecor, was losing its distribution deal with a Japanese supplier and would pay out \~$8 in cash plus a fraction of an Electro Rent share, which left Electro Rent priced at "under one times earnings". Lesson: "complexity can be an Investor's friend", so "soak yourself in the details" (approvals needed, payments owed, taxes due).
- Markets are inefficient in practice: had academics sat at the Mutual Shares trading desk, they "might have seen what really inefficient markets look like"; he found the random-walk thesis "just silliness". The causes are human: swings between exuberance and depression, and rules such as "you cannot own a stock below a certain market cap or below a certain share price". "even AI wouldn't make the markets fully efficient", because odd deals have a "sample size of close to one".
- Alignment: Baupost first planned to hire outside managers, but meetings in 1982 showed "a great deal of group think" (everyone owned Warner for Atari) and few managers with their own money in their funds, so it ran the money itself. Staff and families are still "by far the largest client on a collective basis". It took families by word of mouth, its first institutions only in 1998, avoids "hot money type clients", and has refused money until a client read five years of letters: "if you don't have long-term oriented clients you can't make long-term Investments".
- Value now needs more work: in Graham's day you could buy at "six times earnings and two-thirds of working capital or two-thirds of Book value" and be right, because the problems were cyclical. Now a start-up in a garage can leave a company barely existing in five years, so investors must weigh "secular change as well" and dig much deeper. Klarman was "too enamored of Book value" early on and says Margin of Safety did not do "a good job on understanding the value of intangible assets".
- Sourcing: no industry analysts or coverage lists; Baupost hunts "Supply demand imbalances": a delisting that forces index holders out (even in Turkey), downgrades, bankruptcy filings, a bank or insurer dumping a souring loan by quarter-end, a 12-year-old real estate fund selling its last assets, misread litigation. "we go miles wide to look for opportunity", then "drill miles deep". Brokers were told not to call "with IBM insights" but with "a bond that you've never heard of", a secondary partnership interest or an illiquid private stake: "we'll have a bid for you".
- Out of favor pays: stocks dropped from an index have "a lot of people that have to sell it nobody that has to buy it", and stigma ("a failed acquisition or a management misstep") keeps others away. Of two identical companies, the one outside the S&P at a 20% discount is the better long-term buy "every time"; over long periods, returns come from lower multiples of earnings or book and higher dividend yields. When a stock is "30 or 40 or 50 percent undervalued" today, "you have to be right about less".
- Process: "a big enough discount maybe offsets a lack of the deepest possible knowledge", but a stock down 20% in days did not fall "for no reason", so make sure you are not "the Patsy at the table". Diligence asks what free cash flow and margins are really doing and whether management is "stretching to put up good numbers". He values in "a range of value": not a $52.50 target but $50–60, and "if you buy it at 35 you're okay".
- Sizing: he rejects funds with "200 ideas" and "none of them more than half a percent"; if you can tell good from bad, you can spot great, and great should be bigger. Baupost has made "our big dollar profits ... usually in ideas that have gone against us at first and we average down", ideally with a catalyst (a reorganization or liquidation plan, a merger closing, an asset sale funding buybacks). "the presence of a catalyst makes us comfortable having a bigger position"; without one, "being early and being wrong look exactly the same".
- Risk: Baupost has "only lost money in five of its 41 years", all but two in the mid-single digits or less. Tools: downside work on each position, offsetting correlations, catalysts that shorten duration, senior credit (you might still get "80 cents on the dollar back"), cash, and overlay hedges (macro, commodity, rates, puts on the market). The market multiple averages \~17x but was 20x (Jul 2023), at times near 30x or 10x, so a rich market leaves you exposed to the multiple falling back.
- Illiquidity: demand "several hundred basis points" ("200 or 500") for giving up "the right to change your mind", but illiquidity itself doesn't pay; forced sellers do. A whole building can be sold when you choose, while "9.9 percent of the shares of a 400 million dollar company" can be an illiquid block. Thought experiment: a 5-year Treasury-like asset yielding 10% when Treasuries yield 4% but untradeable for five years suits any hold-to-maturity Treasury buyer; Baupost finds such claims in liquidating trusts and cash-like holdings.
- International: mostly US and Western Europe. He avoided China for decades over its authoritarian system, but recently found stocks discounting so much China risk that "for the first time ever" Baupost felt well paid, e.g., one down 90% (a small position). He has no view on frontier-market premiums, but "when I find a stock trading at 25 cents on the dollar I know that those things tend to work out".
- Macro (Jul 2023): "one of the weirdest times" in 40+ years. Near-zero rates made "a credit bubble that became an everything bubble" (start-up manias, SPACs, meme stocks, crypto); it collapsed in 2022, but the market has "rebounded again to overpriced levels". After a 35-year bond bull market, institutions such as SVB ended up with asset-deposit mismatches: "I'm not convinced we know where all the bodies are buried". With the Greenspan, Bernanke and Yellen puts and the SVB deposit rescue, "the moral hazard is very high".
- Private markets warning: the private-credit build-up is untested ("it's not been tested"), and it "wouldn't surprise me to see pockets of private credit blow up". Private equity deserves "an asterisk" for 2008–09: its debt traded at "50 cents on the dollar" until Fed cuts and stimulus rescued the deals, and voters may balk at rescuing "wealthy Wall Street financiers" next time. "study Financial history": unless it is the Great Depression, a 40–50% fall is already steep.
- Positioning (Jul 2023): after 12 years starved of yield, Baupost has rebalanced into credit (downgrades below investment grade, distressed and bankrupt debt), now \~15% of the portfolio and rising. It is "not anything like Peak opportunity" yet, but could grow because many companies carry more debt than is prudent. In the 2010s it leaned on real estate and "one-off hairy deals" in private equity. Equity exposure is down, more of the equity book sits in positions with catalysts, and the portfolio is "pretty well hedged".
