### 346. Shantanu Narayen — Adobe: AI as a tailwind for creative software, S-curve pacing and "unreasonable expectations" (NBIM, Jun 2024)

*Chair and CEO of Adobe (CEO since 2007), maker of Photoshop, PDF and the Experience Cloud marketing software, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ey0q5CmIV1E)

**Takeaway:** Narayen argues that every platform shift (desktop publishing, cloud subscriptions, mobile) expanded Adobe's market when channeled, and that generative AI will do the same by removing the fear of "the blank page", using Adobe's own models trained only on licensed data. He paces each product by where it sits on its S-curve and drives teams with "unreasonable expectations".

- Three businesses: creative software (Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere, After Effects, Express); Document Cloud (PDF; Tangen cited 400 billion PDFs opened a year); and Experience Cloud, which helps businesses engage customers digitally and is "close to a $5 billion business". Adobe was "the first major software company" to move to the cloud.
- Longevity (40+ years): Adobe is "a product company" that keeps reinventing itself, from licensing PostScript to a few hardware makers to applications, the cloud and digital marketing, with "a willingness to make that investment to invest in deep technology". Founders John Warnock and Chuck Geschke wanted "a company that has great impact and where great people want to work".
- S-curve pacing: "software has an scurve", and "you have to be aware of which part of that scurve you are on": new platforms need speed, mature products like PostScript don't. "be impatient", but choose what for: functionality first, then customer acquisition, then revenue. Pace comes from inspection: "people do what you inspect not what you expect".
- AI as tailwind: every big technology shift is "a phenomenal Tailwind if you Channel it effectively", or else a threat. Desktop publishing, monthly cloud subscriptions and mobile each "expanded dramatically" Adobe's market and installed base; generative AI tackles creators' fear of "the blank page", and "used effectively any piece of technology is going to dramatically expand the addressable Market".
- Three AI layers: interfaces (a conversational PDF reader, campaign creation, Generative Fill, "the most widely adopted" feature in Photoshop's history); foundation models (Adobe's own Firefly models for imaging and video, while integrating others such as OpenAI's Sora, Runway and Pika in Premiere, and OpenAI for text); and data. Adobe trains only on licensed data, "so we can indemnify a customer like you".
- A heterogeneous world: Adobe supported Mac and PC, Android and iOS from day one, and will support language models from Nvidia, Microsoft, Google and OpenAI. Next comes reasoning (video needs physics), and while today's activity is training, "the real value is going to come from inference".
- Not replaced by a model: "people who use AI will replace people who don't use AI". Software writing is where AI is most advanced, yet firms want developers to do more "at the same cost", not fewer of them; creativity needs "a soul".
- Deepfakes: Adobe's Content Credentials act like "a nutrition label" showing who made and changed content, carried by thousands of partners (camera makers, Meta, the New York Times, the BBC). The plumbing exists; the open question is whether consumers will check it, in a year when \~50% of the world's population votes (2024).
- Experience Cloud: selling directly online showed Adobe that marketing, unlike supply chain, finance and HR, wasn't automated. It bought Omniture (2009) for analytics ("50% of my marketing is working I just don't know which 50%") and then Day Software, built a real-time customer-data platform from scratch, and told the board Omniture would be "one of many" deals.
- Leadership: "set unreasonable expectations" ("I have more faith in what you can do than perhaps you have in yourself"); the cloud subscriber targets that looked impossible were "greatly exceeded". Share information rather than treat it as power, keep "your head in the cloud and your feet on the ground", and seek truth-tellers such as your children and new graduates.
- Investing mindset: from KKR's George Roberts he took "I don't use the word risk": "we're paid right in our jobs to make investments", some of which fail. From the Pfizer board: pharma bets often pay off under a successor, so think long range.
- Governance: Tangen said NBIM generally wants the CEO and chair roles split. Narayen's case for holding both: a lead director who can call meetings and act on ethics or performance, private board sessions, board refreshment with members who "push back" (e.g. Eli Lilly's Dave Ricks), and a chair role that "needs to be earned". He notes the "information asymmetry" between management and boards is increasing.
- India: education, curiosity and a long-term view shaped him, plus jugaad, the resourcefulness to "figure things out". He, Satya Nadella and Ajay Banga attended the same Hyderabad school. When he left in 1984, 60–70% of his class went to the US; now maybe 5% do, since India offers capital and talent instead of 18–20% bank loans and no venture capital. He hopes post-COVID "walls" don't end free trade.
