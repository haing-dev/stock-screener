### 348. Sir David Spiegelhalter — Deep uncertainty, trustworthy evidence and resilience (NBIM, Sep 2025)

*Statistician and risk communicator, a board member of the UK Statistics Authority, whose research was long funded by the charitable arm of David Harding's Winton Capital; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen (CEO of NBIM) on uncertainty, data, AI and how to communicate evidence.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=meEdz9v793o)

**Takeaway:** We face deep uncertainty: we can't even list what might happen, so textbook "rational" decision-making fails at the first step. Combine analysis with imagination, diverse views and judgment, watch the data for outliers, admit uncertainty openly (it builds trust, even among skeptics) and make resilience the first priority.

- Think uncertainty, not just risk: we face "deeper uncertainty" where "we can't even list the possibilities". Risk appetite is not one trait; people reckless with their bodies can be "extremely cautious with money". Aversion to unknown odds (Daniel Ellsberg's study, \~70 years ago) is reasonable: when you can't size the risks, people hedge "against major losses".
- Decision theory "fails at the first step", because you can't list every outcome, let alone put numbers on them. Instead, imagine extreme scenarios, which "requires diversity of inputs": Obama's bin Laden decision drew on advisers who didn't talk to each other, from "30 to 40%" to "80 to 90%" sure he was there. Analysis "never tells you what to do. You also have to have judgment", and numbers are not "cold hard facts": "there's always judgment behind every statistical analysis".
- Look at the data. Harold Shipman murdered "at least 250 and possibly 400" of his patients over 20 years and could have been "detected after a few years if somebody had been looking at the data", given his excess deaths. His team adapted industrial quality control (statistical process control) to medical outcomes; UK children's intensive care now uses it, and the NHS is "only now" building one for maternity units. He wants official statistics to say what the data "does not mean", and deplores the US sacking the head of the Bureau of Labor Statistics "when he doesn't like the numbers".
- Admitting uncertainty builds trust. John Krebs' five crisis rules: say what you know, what you don't know, what you're doing about it, what people can do meanwhile, and that "our advice will change as we learn more". Randomized trials on "thousands of people" found such balanced messages raised trust most among skeptics, while hammering a message "bam bam bam" lowers it. Jonathan Van-Tam changed AstraZeneca vaccine advice by showing the data: "It's not a U-turn. It's adjusting our course."
- Old data misleads in a changing world. For his own prostate cancer, abiraterone trials show median survival of "18 months or something", but they enrolled sicker patients and treatment has moved on: "When you're asking for a long-term prediction, you can't". Polls are biased because they ask how you would vote now. The UK's COVID "half a million deaths" was a do-nothing scenario later misquoted as a forecast. He bought one lottery ticket, when rollovers made "the expected return was higher than the ticket price".
- AI shines on "tightly constrained problems" (DeepMind's games, protein folding, breast-cancer and eye scans), but he distrusts jumps to general claims. In weather, DeepMind's pure pattern-matching black box now competes with physics models and is "doing really well"; he is "secretly on the side of the blackbox" but wants probabilities, which DeepMind's first Nature paper lacked. AI "poses an extreme risk and I think it's probably overrated."
- Climate attribution compares an event's odds with and without man-made warming; the relative risk becomes a probability of causation, and above 50% meets the civil "balance of probabilities" test. He expects it to be "a big growing area uh financially when people start suing uh fossil fuel companies", though pinning events on particular companies is "rather more difficult".
- Resilience is "the number one priority", and "as relevant to corporations as it is to human beings", because under deep uncertainty you can only prepare to learn from whatever happens. Take risks but "Don't be reckless": "cover yourself from the damn major downsides". He credits Norway with treating its oil as a resource for the whole community, unlike the UK, which sold off rights for short-term money.
