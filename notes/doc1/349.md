### 349. Sir Paul Marshall — Alpha capture, man plus machine, and an AI bull market heading for a bubble top (In Good Company, Aug 2026)

*Co-founder, with Ian Wace in 1997, of Marshall Wace (\~$90bn, per host Nicolai Tangen), which combines discretionary and systematic investing; author of the book 10½ Lessons from Experience. Tangen interviews him for Norges Bank Investment Management.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LGWLEKX9LDo)

**Takeaway:** Marshall Wace has lasted by innovating constantly. Alpha capture turned sell-side ideas into a systematic strategy, and AI is now remaking its fundamental side. Information edges are gone, but reading the market's "ecology" (positioning, leverage, forced de-risking) still pays. In Aug 2026 he sees no AI bubble yet, but expects "a big bull market with a bubble top".

- Continuous innovation is why the firm has survived \~30 years: "we're almost now a tech firm rather than an investment management firm". Most managers fail from hubris and size: they grow without capital discipline until their positions own them. Humility is built in, since "a really good manager might have a 53 54% success ratio".
- The industry has concentrated: starting a fund took \~50 million of AUM in 1997 and takes 300 to 500 now, mainly because of regulation. In 2008 AUM fell from 14 billion to three and a half billion although performance was no worse than peers'. Clients were mostly funds of funds (there was no marketing staff until 2004–05), and as peers gated without real liquidity problems, Marshall Wace "became the cash point".
- Alpha capture (TOPS, 2002): Wace wanted to measure the sell side rather than vote on commissions. Intern Anthony Clake built an intranet where brokers' salespeople ran transparent, intraday virtual portfolios. Marshall, with "the arrogance of the buy side", was the skeptic and was "completely wrong". A real portfolio followed about two years later (\~10% of capital, Tangen's figure), with the US added in '05 and Asia in '06; contributors now number in the hundreds.
- TOPS then grew into "a proper very market orientated systematic" strategy. Optimization weights contributors and signals, turnover of about twice a month pushed the firm into algorithmic trading early, and machine learning after 2010 mined broker notes, social media and balance-sheet data.
- AI's biggest impact so far is on the fundamental side ("completely revolutionary"): tools distill all information overnight, and fundamental teams have built agentic portfolio managers. His head of tech expects 200 quant researchers to become 10,000 (meaning agents) through recursive self-improvement. Headcount is \~750, over 200 in tech, and has been flat for two years; only one fundamental team in 15 has cut staff.
- Edge: "efficiency itself is a kind of convenient myth". Information edges are nearly gone, but an "analytical edge still remains". AI will empower retail investors, and high-retail markets (China, Taiwan, Korea, increasingly the US) are likely to be more inefficient. Nowcasting quarterly earnings and positioning, the pod-shop space, is "getting commoditized".
- Man plus machine: in the Korea-led momentum crash of the prior four to six weeks (Aug 2026), hedge-fund risk managers de-risked because volatility was high, "rather than because of anything wrong with the fundamentals". It is "the human who brings it all together and sees the opportunity", reading leveraged ETFs, foreign and hedge-fund holdings, and fundamentals together.
- Market view (Aug 2026): AI is "an intelligence explosion", like a Cambrian moment or the steam engine. The firm's own token use is exploding, and he expects recursive self-improvement within six to 12 months. It is not a bubble yet: the US semiconductor index sits at a five- or 10-year low valuation, Korea at an all-time low, and the stocks have only moved up in line with earnings.
- His bubble checklist: low rates encouraging speculation, very extended valuations (not yet), and unsustainable leverage. Korean leveraged ETFs fell from $60 billion to 20 in five weeks; packaging GPUs into CDOs and selling two-times-levered versions would be the kind of thing seen in 2007–08. His sell signal for AI is deceleration in token use, not a fall, which "would be far too late".
- AI and rates: AI is "somewhat inflationary" in the short term (chips, the energy supply chain) before productivity brings disinflation. He expects entry-level labor weakness within six to 24 months, which would be bullish because it takes pressure off interest rates.
- A good investment is "not quite the same as what makes a good company": respect both the weighing machine (free cash flow) and the voting machine (positioning). His horizon is 3 to 12 months, so he needs catalysts in that window to confirm the thesis against the market.
- Shorting is hard: brokers and the press are set up for longs, a losing short grows instead of letting you average down, and "the view of the stock is reflected in the borrow rate" set by smart hedge funds. He still loves shorting, which also funds the longs.
- Risk lessons: his worst year came from a long-only portfolio heavy in oil services and oil stocks going into the first Iraq war (invasion Aug 2, 1990), "a major major failure" of risk management. Now there are drawdown limits and debriefs for portfolios, not single stocks. PMs see success, win/loss and slugging ratios (gains concentrated in a few high-conviction names), and fundamental PMs need "a very high slugging ratio". A stock that costs 3% of NAV gets a deep review.
- People: PMs join with multi-year records; growing one in-house takes \~10 years. In the AI age traits beat skills: agency, curiosity, stress tolerance, and being "very open and very disagreeable". Experience as pattern recognition "is heavily undervalued". Europe is 25% of gross commitments, but the US leads in technology; on Brexit he was right about sovereignty but "wrong on the economics".
