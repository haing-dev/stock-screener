### 351. Susan Cain — Quiet leaders, mixed teams and the cost of performed positivity (NBIM, Aug 2026)

*Author of Quiet (8 years on the bestseller list) and Bittersweet, talking with Nicolai Tangen (CEO of NBIM) about introverts, leadership, AI and authenticity at work.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W4uofN2WMEc)

**Takeaway:** Introverts are as driven as extroverts, often more focused, and studies suggest they lead as well or better, especially with proactive teams, yet our "culture of personality" keeps promoting charisma. Managers should design work for different temperaments, hear from everyone in meetings and drop forced positivity, which blocks the candor that keeps teams safe and productive.

- Introverts are "just as high agency as extroverts" but tend to focus on "one or two or three goals", so AI can amplify them as much as anyone. The most "robot-proof" people will be those who care most about human authenticity, notably "highly sensitive" people (\~15–20% of the population), who react more to everything, good and bad, and have "a deep appreciation of excellence".
- Temperament shows from birth: newborns who salivate more for sugar water tend to be the quiet ones in a new playgroup at 2, pausing to "get back to equilibrium". Introversion, extroversion and high sensitivity "are among the most heritable" personality traits. At 40, the same people weighing a business deal tend to be more attuned to its risks and subtleties.
- Lead the person, not "an undifferentiated mass of humans": give each the right amount of stimulation (less sensitive people get "bored and sluggish" without it), with both lively and quiet spaces and "designated zones" of meeting-free flow time. A Kellogg study found "three people doing 70% of the talking" in a typical large meeting, so good ideas go unheard and weaker ones get overweighted; Tangen added that the talkers are often extroverts "and also the most highly paid".
- Why extroverts get promoted: at the turn of the 20th century we moved from a "culture of character" (integrity) to a "culture of personality" (magnetism), and people rate those who speak louder and faster as more intelligent. Yet "many studies" suggest introverted leaders deliver outcomes "as strong or in some cases stronger". In Jim Collins's Good to Great, all 11 top-performing CEOs were described as "quiet, unassuming, soft-spoken, shy" but with "a fierce sense of dedication to their companies".
- Introverts reach leadership by other routes: commitment, deep expertise and networks of people who share their passion. Douglas Conant, a self-described shy CEO of Campbell Soup, wrote "30,000 of those letters" of personal thanks to employees in his 10 years there. Adam Grant (Wharton) found extroverted leaders do better with less motivated staff, but introverted leaders do better with proactive employees, because they listen to their ideas.
- Build mixed teams: "mixed teams do the best", and as CEO she would want a more extroverted co-CEO or deputy so each plays to strengths. She calibrates her own calendar daily between social and solitary time; a packed one would make her "burn out quite quickly", and working against your temperament adds "a kind of cognitive load". Hybrid "is the way to go"; she never writes at home ("much too isolating"), only in coffee shops.
- Forced positivity has a cost. When everyone types "I'm feeling great" in a Zoom chat, "there's no way that's true"; performing happiness drains energy and blocks real connection. A Harvard Business School study of a Gulf of Mexico oil rig with a no-doubts, macho culture: before a move to a bigger, more dangerous rig, its leader Rick Fox had crews talk openly for 10 days. Accident rates "plummeted" and productivity "went way up", largely because workers stopped pretending to know what they were doing and flagged machinery problems.
- Career: she wanted to write from age 4 but spent about 7 years as a corporate lawyer, partly a mistake, though it shaped her outlook. Her advice to the young: recall "what did you really love, and who were you really when you were a child?", because that shows who you are under outside pressure.
