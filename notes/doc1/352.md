### 352. Rob Vinall — Owner return, life's-work managers and a momentum market's casualties (The Investor's Podcast, Aug 2026)

*Founder of RV Capital (2006) and manager of its concentrated Business Owner Fund (launched 2008), interviewed by William Green on Richer, Wiser, Happier ahead of the firm's 20th anniversary.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MMyfAJRU6o4)

**Takeaway:** Vinall bets on managers who have made the business their life's work and values stocks by "owner return" (cash paid out plus growth in earnings power, 15% target) rather than by multiples. He finds today's momentum-driven market, with software and China out of favour, easier for a patient owner, not harder (Aug 2026).

- Dot-com lesson: as a Frankfurt telecom analyst he saw stocks lose 99%, some at a "10 million market cap but you know 100 million of of cash". Most of those early cheap buys did very well, "but some went to zero because they were kind of frauds or the business didn't work".
- He soon found P/E had "very little predictive power": 2x and 5x earnings are both very cheap. What matters is whether the earnings are real and how good the business is after you buy.
- Owner return = the cash a business returns each year + the growth in its earnings power, assuming no change in the multiple. His target since the start is 15%, e.g. a 15% dividend yield, 15% earnings growth or a mix. Green says he has done slightly better over 18 years.
- Three stages: great prices, then great businesses, then great managers. Analysis only sees "the tip of the iceberg"; under good people what lay beneath was "at the very least not terrible", while under weak ones "the surprises were always negative".
- He can't rank managers: given 100 CEOs he would be "no better than average and and quite possibly worse". His niche is the small cohort who make the business their life's work (founders, career lifers or later arrivals who buy into the culture), where "the odds are just massively massively stacked in your favor".
- First filter, done mechanically: reject companies whose management is "a revolving door of MBAs", the rule rather than the exception. Founders are not automatically good: some lose interest, some sell their stock "but still feel like they're entitled to the economics", and some are overtaken by change.
- In meetings he takes CEOs off-script (a walk, his home, unrelated topics) to learn what motivates them and the company's history. Liking someone is decades of pattern recognition (Buffett hired Ajit Jain because he liked him), and since we like people like ourselves, "you need to cultivate in yourself the qualities you're looking for in other people".
- Carvana: he calls co-founder Ernie Garcia the epitome of a founder building his life's work. Told in 2023 of a CEO's "Goldilocks" 20% growth rate, Garcia said that CEO only grew 20% "because he wanted to be home by 6:00".
- Mistakes: in 2022 the fund fell 47.6% (Green) with Carvana down \~98%. The market was bad but "wasn't a terrible market", and the drawdown was "in large part due to missteps that I'd made".
- Moats: his view has shifted. Moats "can create complacency", especially in fast-changing industries, so he prefers a narrower moat that keeps a company "on their toes" and is widening over a bigger one that is shrinking.
- China (Aug 2026): about a third of the fund, four of the top 10 (Luckin Coffee, Tencent, H World, Yum China). Not speaking the language, he keeps to founder-run, wide-moat, cheap companies; at least 10% earnings growth plus 5%+ a year of dividends and buybacks gets him "very comfortably" to a 15% owner return.
- Momentum market: the S&P sets highs on a small corner of chip hardware while most of what he follows is \~50% below its highs. In March 2026 the question was "which of the 20 or 30 which are down 60 70% from their previous highs" in software to buy; shares now "go down 25% in a day in a heartbeat" on small misses.
- He won't chase the next Nvidia or Micron; many non-winners will still give "very satisfactory returns". Constellation Software (bought early March 2026) appealed as "full of owners", with new CEO Mark Miller a founder in his own right, unlike software peers with "way too much stockbased compensation way too little owner earnings".
- Process: "a company is worth the cash it's going to produce over its lifetime". He brainstorms widely for disconfirming views but decides alone, as he "in some respects" doesn't trust himself not to follow others. Norman Rentrop, his first client, told him "Don't change anything", so he never had to unlearn diversification (\~10 stocks).
