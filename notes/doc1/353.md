### 353. Thomas Curran — The perfection trap: why perfectionists don't perform better (NBIM, Nov 2023)

*Psychologist at the LSE and author of The Perfection Trap, talking with Nicolai Tangen (CEO of NBIM) about the three kinds of perfectionism, why it is rising and what organizations can do about it.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9MZ3NqUl9bU)

**Takeaway:** Perfectionism starts from a feeling of not being enough, and it is rising fast, above all the belief that others demand perfection. It doesn't improve performance: perfectionists work hard until they fail, then withdraw and procrastinate. Organizations get more from "good enough", room to fail and leaders who set direction rather than micromanage.

- Perfectionism grows from "a feeling that we are not quite enough", hidden behind excessive standards. Three kinds: self-oriented ("a burning need to be perfect", \~30–40% inherited, high in the US but less so in Europe); other-oriented (imposing those standards on others); and socially prescribed (a sense that everyone "expect Flawless performances"), which has "the strongest correlation with serious mental health issues".
- Socially prescribed perfectionism is rising "on an exponential curve", from around 2005 by Tangen's reading. Curran's causes: the crash and recession, fewer opportunities and more competition for college places; a policy response of "lower interest rates money printing" that pushed up asset prices, rents and house prices; and the iPhone (2007) and social media, "a scrambled reality" of curated perfect lives. Perfectionists rarely seek help and push harder after setbacks, so Curran calls perfectionism a kind of anti-resilience.
- The "perfectionistic Paradox": perfectionists work harder and longer at first, but in lab tests, after a failure, they withdraw effort while others "put a little bit more in". Avoidance and procrastination manage the anxiety. Meta-analyses show "zero relationship" between perfectionism and work performance, because the focus "is on avoiding failure rather than shooting for Success".
- Other-oriented perfectionism is Tangen's "boss from hell"; Curran cites Steve Jobs. It can build "a culture of exceptionally high standards but it does so by fear". Tangen cited a stat that "70% of people who leave the jobs" do so because of their immediate boss; where mistakes are punished, people stop taking risks and being creative. McKinsey reportedly hires "insecure overachievers" (Tangen); Curran says that can serve a firm but is a short road to burnout for the people.
- Tangen's fixes at NBIM: encourage "Half Baked ideas" and "call things a pilot if we think it may not succeed" (the podcast began as one), which cuts the embarrassment of failure and, Curran adds, "calibrates expectations from the very start". Curran cites Google experimenting to "pay people to fail" and Atom Bank testing a four-day week to see whether "we get more from less".
- Aim for good enough: any project has "hundreds of ways that are good enough" but "there is no perfect way", and editing until midnight can even compromise quality. Ship once it meets the client's and the firm's criteria, then move on. Tangen: the last 10% "takes a lot of time a lot of money", and even perfect work will be disliked by "10% of people per definition".
- Tangen thinks it helps a CEO not to be a perfectionist: the job is "deciding which mountains to climb and rally the troops" through motivation, not micromanagement. Citing Sam Altman, he avoids overly specific targets (give people the task and see what they come up with), or "you destroy people's creativity" and their ownership. Managing means seeing the person and when they are "in the flow", not just the results; Curran calls this "a bit of a paradigm shift".
- Coping: put the work out anyway. Curran tried to make his book "bulletproof", some people still disliked it, and that was fine; criticism shows you were "brave enough to put something in the world". Swap self-attack ("how could you be so stupid") for self-compassion. Grit helps, but "everything in moderation": when a project heads for a dead end, "know when to quit".
