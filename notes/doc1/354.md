### 354. Vinod Khosla — Betting on the improbable, and a future of free expertise, labor and energy (NBIM, Oct 2024)

*Co-founder of Sun Microsystems (1982) and founder of Khosla Ventures (2004), interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on In Good Company about how he picks bets and where technology is heading.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2VfNXLvPJ8w)

**Takeaway:** Experts extrapolate the past, so the changes that matter are "improbable" and driven by entrepreneurs. Khosla backs fast-learning founders on low-probability, high-impact bets, accepting a 90% chance of failure for a 10% chance to change the world. He expects AI to make expertise and labor nearly free, which he calls "deflationary in a good way".

- Expert forecasts extrapolate the past: experts said Russia's invasion of Ukraine would be over in two days, and Tesla beat in 2016 the number of electric cars the US Department of Energy had forecast in 2010 for 2035. "it's entrepreneurs specifically who invent the future they want".
- Khosla committed to OpenAI in 2018. The timing of a breakthrough was unpredictable, but the trend was exponential and they were probably on its flat early part. The 2017 paper that made AI take off came from Google researchers, and OpenAI scaled it. Incumbents rarely lead: Google, not a carmaker, leads self-driving.
- What he looks for: a plausible path to something experts call impossible, a founder who asks "why not" and persists, the right timing, and a technology breakthrough (mobile phones made Uber and Airbnb possible).
- His best bets were contrarian. In 1996, at Kleiner Perkins, he backed Juniper on TCP/IP becoming the internet's protocol when no telco believed it: "we got a 2500x investment" over about five years, $7bn on $3m. Google was "a couple of flaky PhD students".
- Judging founders: talk to them for a month or two and watch how fast they learn. "fast learning is more important than experience", and "experience is a set of biases which causes you to extrapolate the past". First-principles answers are a good sign; "I've done this for 30 years" is a bad one.
- Expertise will be free: AI is close to matching the median oncologist, primary-care doctor, teacher or structural engineer, and can be copied without limit. If 90% of the $300–400bn the US spends on physician salaries goes away, the world becomes "deflationary in a good way". He argues India should offer free primary-care AI, as it already offers free payments.
- Labor will be free: two-legged humanoid robots within about five years, an industry he expects to outgrow autos within 25 years, though "nobody's planning for that large a change". With seven billion people running 10 agents each, 70 billion AI agents will do everyday tasks; one fitness company went from 10–20 to 150 clients per trainer. Expect a decade of supervised AI interns first.
- Policy sets the pace (5, 10 or 25 years). Societies that protect jobs, as Hollywood's Screen Actors Guild did by opposing AI-generated content, will lose the economic race; he worries China may push it through with a Tiananmen Square-style approach.
- Medicine and food: drugs designed for one person's cancer (portfolio company N1 Bio \[?\]) in 10–15 years; greenhouse robots already grow premium Korean strawberries, and robots spray weeds one at a time instead of whole fields.
- Transport: only \~200 of the 4,000 cities that should have public transit have it, because it is capex-heavy and runs infrequently. Khosla's startup has won contracts in four California cities for point-to-point, two-person self-driving pods, and he bets over 50% of cars in most cities are gone by 2050.
- Energy: since he backed Commonwealth Fusion in 2018 a dozen credible fusion startups have appeared. He expects fusion to be proven viable within five years and scaled in the 2030s–40s by retrofitting coal and gas plants with fusion boilers. Solar in India contracts at 1.7 cents per kWh, a tenth of what many places pay, but it is not dispatchable.
- Minerals: copper, lithium and cobalt are where oil was in the 1960s, when peak oil was predicted. Nobody has yet used AI or sensors to look a kilometre or two down, so in 20 years "we will be talking about a mineral glut".
- Method: "among a thousand improbable some improbable is very probable", so take more shots on goal; he doesn't mind a 90% chance of failure if there is a 10% chance of changing the world.
- If AI lifts GDP growth from 2% to 5–6%, there will be enough abundance to afford something like universal basic income, but the transition will be hard.
