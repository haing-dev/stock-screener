### 355. Rob Vinall — Investment process: start from the entrepreneur, then hunt for disconfirming facts (Good Investing Talks, Apr 2019)

*Founder of RV Capital and manager of its concentrated Business Owner Fund, walking through his process from idea generation to selling.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C3A8wxawlao)

**Takeaway:** Which of the 10,000+ listed companies you start looking at decides most of the outcome, so Vinall starts from a passionate, aligned entrepreneur rather than a cheapness screen. He then tries to disprove a "wonderful business" hypothesis over months or years, and sells when he is wrong, not when the stock looks expensive.

- He used to screen like Graham (net-nets, discounts to net book value, very low P/E, 52-week lows) but stopped years ago. On average such stocks are below-average businesses: "the market is not completely stupid if it's saying something is worth only four times earnings".
- His starting point now is "a very passionate aligned entrepreneur running a business", tied to it as a major shareholder or, ideally, as CEO. Referrals and press articles bring the names.
- Five-minute kills: no one he could trust; a business he can't understand (an early-stage cancer biotech); a CEO who "has changed you know three times in the last two years"; no major shareholder; a business likely to change a lot. Country, sector and size don't matter, but he needs rule of law and protected property rights.
- Debt: he is "quite allergic" to it. An industrial or service company should typically have zero debt and most likely net cash, and a lender an equity ratio "multiples higher" than peers, so it is the "last man standing" in a crisis. Cyclicals are fine in principle but hard to value when profits go 100, then 50, then 75.
- Reading: the best case is a long, thoughtful CEO letter, which shows both the business and how the manager thinks. The hypothesis is always a wonderful business that will flourish for ten years; he reads for disconfirming facts (an exhausted growth runway, an obvious competitive threat) and drops the idea as soon as one appears.
- "the numbers in isolation really don't mean anything": they are the scoreboard, and what counts is the play (how the company sells, makes money and creates value for customers). He prefers primary sources: annual reports, customers, suppliers and employees, or being a customer himself in consumer businesses.
- Time: ideally he follows a company for years before buying, since a month of work misses risks. He met Grenke's founder in 2005 when the valuation was too high, then bought in 2008 when investors dumped anything financial; it was still one of the largest positions (Apr 2019).
- Trupanion took \~6 months from first look to purchase, his "absolute minimum": desk research takes a few weeks, then calls and a visit that takes at least a month to plan. He has swapped conferences (\~25 companies a week) for one to three days at a single company.
- Concentration: ten holdings, the largest 20% or more and the smallest \~5%. Detail matters because the best buys come when everyone else is panicking, and he only finds the courage if he knows the company better than anyone.
- Key drivers: every investment comes down to one or a few metrics. At Credit Acceptance, founder Don Foss's insight was making the car dealer share the loan's risk; CEO Brett Roberts added the scoreboard, economic value added per loan after its capital cost, with capital going first to the most profitable loans.
- Selling: mainly when the thesis proves wrong (less growth, a less engaged manager); he sells fast and has never regretted it. Selling a great business only because it looked expensive has often been a mistake, since surprises at great businesses tend to be positive, so he now gives price less weight than business and people.
- He keeps following sold companies he might buy back, and wants sold stocks to keep doing reasonably well, or he would doubt his analysis. His watchlist holds \~70–100 companies.
