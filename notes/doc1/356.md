### 356. Bernard Looney — BP: from oil company to integrated energy company, transition returns and the windfall-tax debate (NBIM, Feb 2022)

*CEO of BP for two years and with the company since 1991, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen a week after a BP strategy update, two years after the Feb 2020 strategy launch (uploaded May 2023).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xdFD6dJVj_A)

**Takeaway:** Looney is turning BP from an international oil company into an integrated energy company: hydrocarbon volumes fall 40% and their cash funds five transition growth engines. His answer to shareholders who fear low-margin capital is that BP's assets, brand and customers let it earn 15–20% returns in businesses such as bioenergy, with steadier earnings than oil and gas.

- Strategy in three parts: "resilient hydrocarbons", convenience and mobility (BP sells 150 million cups of coffee a year at its fuel stations) and low-carbon energy. Hydrocarbon volume falls 40%, emissions with it, and that cash funds "five transition growth engines" such as hydrogen, bioenergy and EV charging.
- Why change: the industry's record "over 10 or 20 years in a value creation sense it hasn't been that great", while EV charging and bioenergy grow 10–20% a year. BP sold 4.5x more EV-charging electricity in 2021 than in 2020; in China it sold more in December than in the whole previous year.
- Returns: Tangen asked whether BP was moving capital from high-margin oil and gas into low-margin businesses. Looney expects 15–20% returns in bioenergy, "not less than what we get in hydrocarbons", with less volatile earnings than a commodity business: "transition equals enormous opportunity".
- Bioenergy edge, in the three steps a startup would struggle with: feedstock through BP's trading arm (a 10-year deal with Nuseed to develop carinata, a non-food cover crop); plants next to its refineries, sharing power and utilities for capital efficiency; and sales of sustainable aviation fuel through Air BP, present at 46 airports in Norway alone.
- EV-charging edge: 550 million people live within 20 minutes of a BP station, 60% of visitors to BP stations in Britain come for convenience rather than fuel, and BP has decades of ties with carmakers such as Mercedes. Four out of five new cars bought in Norway are electric.
- Pace: over 40% of BP's capital will go to transition growth businesses within a few years and over 50% by 2030, when they will hold 40% of capital employed. "some people may wish that were a light switch", but he calls this pace "pretty damn fast".
- Windfall tax: "when prices are high we make more money than when prices are low we make less money". Only 12 months earlier BP posted its largest-ever loss, $20bn, when oil and gas prices were low ("negative at one stage"). He opposes the tax, citing 35 LNG cargoes delivered to Europe and a pledge to invest £2 in Britain this decade for every £1 earned there, mostly in offshore wind, hydrogen, EV charging, net-zero power and carbon capture.
- Crisis timing: the pandemic oil-price collapse hit just after the Feb 2020 launch and sent the shares to a 25-year low. BP debated pausing but kept going: year one set the direction, year two brought the biggest restructuring in its 112 years, done virtually. Now "the only thing is to focus on execution".
- Restructuring, which he thinks a company should do every 7–8 years: assess every role for necessity, take out two layers of management and widen spans of control. Staff within five layers of the CEO rose from 20% to over 50%, the top 120 executives are almost all new (over 40% female), and 38 executives came from outside in 18 months (Google, Tesla, Macquarie, Boots, Tesco). Over 7,500 people work in agile teams.
- Stakeholders: "we are owned by our shareholders", and Climate Action 100+ investors "challenge us they hold us to account"; a court "is not a place" to set transition targets. With governments "we avoid politics" and serve each country's energy needs, which kept BP in Egypt for over 60 years through many changes of government.
- Deepwater Horizon (2010, the largest spill in US history; NBIM sued BP over it): people died and the company was "torn apart" on live TV. The one good result was humility; BP starts every meeting with safety under the line "we will never forget".
- Leadership: say "I don't know" now and then. Where a leader must always have an answer, "people make stuff up" and the truth stops reaching the top. He prizes openness, authenticity and what he calls empathetic edge: caring about people while still holding them to account.
- Screen implication: an oil major can swing from a record loss to windfall profits within a year, so judge it on cycle-average earnings and 10-year returns; while it reshapes its portfolio (40% less oil and gas volume), its own history is a weaker guide to normal earnings.
