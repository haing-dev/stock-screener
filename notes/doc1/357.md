### 357. Catherine MacGregor — Engie: the Russian gas shock, "smart electrons" and decarbonizing the molecule (NBIM, Jul 2025)

*CEO of Engie, the French utility and one of Europe's energy giants, who took over as COVID struck and faced Europe's gas crisis a year later; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen in Paris.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-jMUNASY1Q)

**Takeaway:** MacGregor runs Engie as a low-risk utility: every investment should be backed by contracted income and earn about 200bp over its cost of capital, and Engie skips projects that don't. Electrons can already be decarbonized competitively; molecules (biomethane, hydrogen) will take longer because of cost and over-strict EU rules, and cheaper power needs renewables plus storage, grids and paid flexibility.

- Business: \~150 years old (Gaz de France, Suez, International Power and Belgian roots), spanning power generation, infrastructure that transports, stores and distributes energy (traditionally France's gas-infrastructure operator) and customers. It has \~100 GW of power capacity, 50% renewable; the 2030 goal is 95 GW of renewables and batteries, plus growth in power networks and in selling 24/7 renewable power.
- Gas crisis, one year into the job: Europe relied on Russia for 40% of its gas, which went to zero; Engie for 20% of its supply. Prices rose 10x, in some spot markets 100x. The risk was financial as well as physical, because long-term customer commitments rested on long-term supply contracts, so her first demand was a team that could tell her "at any given time the exposure we had". Engie used the months before deliveries stopped to buy more spot gas, LNG and Norwegian gas, and Europe learned to require minimum storage before every winter.
- Electron vs molecule: Europe's 2050 plan lifts electricity from 16–18% of final energy to 50%, so the other half needs decarbonized molecules. Biomethane (the same CH4, made from waste) replaces natural gas "without investment"; hydrogen and its derivatives (ammonia, e-methane) add cost and lose efficiency at each conversion. "Decarbonizing the molecule is a bit more challenging" than the electron, which is already competitive.
- Hydrogen is slower: green hydrogen costs more than grey, so industrial customers face "a bit of a double whammy" of new capex and then higher opex; electrolyzers must scale; and EU rules are "too strict too early". She wants technology-neutral rules (low-carbon hydrogen from gas with carbon capture, nuclear or renewables) until a market exists, citing a 20x fall in solar panel costs over 20 years. ArcelorMittal cancelling green-hydrogen projects shows the risk of a transition "at the expense of industry".
- Capital allocation: a €22–24bn gross capex plan over 3 years, 90% of it in 10 countries and 85% aligned with the EU taxonomy, on renewables (wind, solar, batteries) and power networks. Projects must create value, on average \~200bp over WACC ("not you know crazy amounts"); Engie won't reach net zero (2045) or 95 GW "by destroying value".
- "smart electrons": no "dumb solar projects" in regions already full of solar, which deepen negative-price hours at midday. Projects must produce when demand needs it, so they keep their value after their 10–15 year CfDs or contracts end.
- Risk appetite: "we are a utility. Our risk appetite is not very high." Engie backs every investment with contracted income: a customer PPA, a government or regulator contract, or a capacity remuneration mechanism (CRM). Batteries are "very difficult to invest in" on merchant revenue alone, so countries need CRMs to get storage built.
- Spain's big outage: plenty of renewables had run in the weeks before without a blackout, so don't blame renewables alone. Complex grids need enough stabilizing capacity (gas, hydro, nuclear, well-programmed batteries) for frequency and voltage, which solar provides poorly, plus strong interconnections.
- Prices and sovereignty: Tangen cites the Draghi report, energy four to five times dearer in Europe than in the US. MacGregor expects renewables plus storage, grids and extended existing nuclear to cut prices; Spanish power prices are already the lowest in Europe. Europe imported €375bn of fossil energy in 2024, while renewables are produced locally; with 80% of solar panels from China (Tangen), Engie is diversifying suppliers.
- AI demand: an AI data center uses as much power as a city of \~100,000 people. Tech customers push decarbonization, some asking for 24/7 hourly matching rather than annual offsets; their new load should bring new green capacity so other customers don't face higher prices.
- Technology mix: "never oppose the lowcarbon technologies". Nuclear has waste, cooling and new-build cost issues, renewables are intermittent and hydro suffers in droughts. A French offshore wind farm (61 turbines, 500 MW, power for 800,000 people) took under two years from investment decision to power, after 10 years of development held up by permits; its 8 MW turbines compare with 15–16 MW in Scotland, so offshore costs are falling. A poll found 84% of French people view renewables positively.
- Owners: the French state owns \~24% but has 33–34% of votes and three board seats; she says it acts as a "normal shareholder" happy with the dividend. Utility investors are "not here for the short term", and the ESG backlash brought no pressure to change because "our whole equity story hinges on" a value-creating transition: "there is a lot of project we don't do".
- Management: with 98,000 staff, set clear priorities, do "not too many different things", and show staff the transition is tied to value creation, because the €10bn a year of maintenance and gross capex "can only be invested over time if you actually generate cash flow".
- Screen implication: a contracted utility earns a thin spread over its cost of capital by design, so judge it on contract cover, that spread and whether operating cash flow funds its capex, not on a high ROIC floor.
