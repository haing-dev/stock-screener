### 358. Darren Woods — ExxonMobil: "a molecule company not an electron company", returns discipline and the case against Scope 3 targets (NBIM, Nov 2023)

*Chair and CEO of ExxonMobil, the descendant of Rockefeller's Standard Oil, at the company since 1992; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen, whose fund owned over 1% of it (NOK 50bn or $5bn).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=immpXKB-4Bk)

**Takeaway:** Woods sees oil's density, availability and cost keeping it hard to replace, so the transition will stay slow and wind and solar are "necessary but nowhere near sufficient". Exxon invests only where its skill with molecules gives it an edge (carbon capture, hydrogen, biofuels) and expects "above average industry leading returns" there; he rejects Scope 3 targets as punishing low-emission producers and LNG that displaces coal.

- Oil's hold: the world uses \~100 million barrels a day even as 80% of Norway's cars are electric (Tangen). Oil is energy-dense, available, affordable and transportable, and its molecules feed many products, so replacing it at the same utility and cost is hard. Progress over 10 years will lag society's goals; wind, solar and electrification are "necessary but nowhere near sufficient".
- "we're a molecule company not an electron company": Exxon's edge is managing and transforming hydrogen and carbon molecules (it has one of the world's largest chemical businesses), so it targets carbon capture, hydrogen and biofuels rather than wind and solar. "if it's just a checkbook", plenty of others can invest.
- Returns test: with a real advantage Exxon "ought to be able to generate above average industry leading returns"; if it can't, it has no unique edge there and should look elsewhere. Solutions and returns are "an and equation", not a trade-off.
- Capital budget: Tangen cites 15% of new investment going to low-carbon solutions. Woods: "we don't start with a budget we start with the opportunity set". Oil and gas are enormous markets of "depleting resources", so investment is needed just to offset depletion; carbon reduction is a tiny business with "no market today" and "very few companies" willing to pay for lower-carbon products.
- Policy as catalyst: incentives can start new industries down the cost curve, but "the government cannot subsidize this business in perpetuity" and markets must develop. He rates Exxon "ahead of a pack" on low-carbon investment.
- Carbon capture: Exxon has "captured more anthropogenic CO2 than any other company on the planet"; the challenge is cost for dilute CO2 sources. Keeping existing infrastructure and removing its emissions is cheaper for society than replacing it, so Exxon is "agnostic" between ending combustion and capturing its emissions.
- Hydrogen and new tech: "fairly color agnostic": blue hydrogen suits the US Gulf Coast with abundant methane and storage; green can make sense in Europe with strong sun and wind. Nuclear is critical; fusion's lab breakthrough is encouraging, but scaling new technology takes "a lot longer than I think people realize".
- Scope 3: useful for whole economies, harmful as a company target (Tangen said NBIM would back an AGM proposal asking Exxon for Scope 3 targets). Each ton of LNG shipped to Asia backs out coal but raises Exxon's Scope 3, so a target means less LNG and more coal burned; and an absolute measure penalizes size. He prefers life-cycle carbon intensity: Exxon's refineries rank among the world's lowest-emission, and if it stopped making fuel "the world demand doesn't change somebody else will meet that".
- US vs Europe: the IRA is "the carrot approach", setting carbon-intensity standards and letting markets pick technologies; Europe's stick is prescriptive and taxes producers, driving industry out. Policy puts "way too much emphasis on the supply side of the equation": while demand holds, cutting supply in Europe moves production elsewhere and raises prices.
- Price taker: Exxon is "at best 3% probably closer to 2%" of the world energy system, so "we're a price taker" that meets society's needs rather than defining them, as it moved from kerosene (replacing whale oil) to gasoline and chemicals.
- Long cycles vs politics: investment cycles span "a good decade". Within six months Woods testified to Congress first under pressure to cut oil and gas investment, then on why Exxon wasn't investing more: "you can't move back and forth" given the investment cycle.
- Portfolio: Exxon left the Norwegian shelf (including licence 001) because others could extract more value there; it sells such assets and focuses where it adds most. Geopolitics takes his time "every day"; Russia's invasion of Ukraine showed energy's national-security weight.
- AI and culture: AI will high-grade staff work towards harder problems solved faster, so Exxon moved IT into its research and technology organization. Its values are integrity, care, courage, resilience and excellence; it learns safety from airlines and nuclear, and positions itself to succeed "irrespective of which way" the transition goes.
- Screen implication: for a producer, capex that only offsets depletion is maintenance, and even the largest oil major is a price taker, so judge oil companies on cycle-average earnings and returns, not one year's prices.
