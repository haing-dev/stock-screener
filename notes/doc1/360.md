### 360. Fatih Birol — IEA: the Strait of Hormuz shock, "the age of electricity" and Europe's three strategic mistakes (NBIM, Mar 2026)

*Executive Director of the International Energy Agency and its former chief economist, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen at the end of March 2026 ("April in two days"), during a Middle East war that had all but shut the Strait of Hormuz; fast-tracked for release.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7FeZdfntWsY)

**Takeaway:** Birol calls the Middle East supply loss the biggest energy-security shock in history: 12 million barrels a day of oil, more than the 1973 and 1979 crises combined, plus more gas than Russia cut in 2022. Stock releases only "reduce the pain"; the cure is reopening Hormuz. Emerging importers will suffer most, and the lasting response will be more nuclear, electric transport and renewables with batteries, and in places more coal.

- Scale: the 1973 and 1979 oil crises each removed about 5 million barrels a day; this one has removed 12 million, "more than two of these oil crisis put together". The gas loss exceeds the \~75 bcm Russia cut in 2022, petrochemicals, fertilizers, helium and sulfur are also disrupted, and 40 key energy assets in the region are damaged, some severely.
- Response: the IEA released 400 million barrels of crude and products on 11 March, the largest release ever and twice the one after Russia invaded Ukraine (Tangen: \~20% of reserves). Prices fell $18, then rebounded. Stocks only "reduce the pain" and buy time: "The cure is opening up" the Strait of Hormuz. Governments should add demand-side measures (working from home, lower speed limits) and targeted, temporary support for the vulnerable.
- Timing: "April will be much worse than March", because cargoes that left before the war were still arriving in March; April's oil loss will be about twice March's. Jet fuel and diesel are the tightest, short in Asia already and in Europe by April or early May, and further stock releases are possible.
- Emerging and developing countries are "hit the worst": importers without hard currency face inflation, slower growth and possibly energy rationing, and he fears a repeat of the 1970s foreign-debt spirals. Among producers, Iraq (90% of government revenue from oil, paying salaries and pensions for \~15 million people), Lebanon and Bahrain suffer most; Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Kuwait should recover quickly.
- Europe's "reflex was a bit slower" than others'. It relies on spot LNG, where Asian buyers now compete, and its power prices follow the marginal cost of gas, so both gas and electricity prices will rise. Support should be targeted and temporary; he fears high prices give "fertile ground" to extreme politics before key elections.
- No return to Russian gas: Nord Stream 1 was sabotaged, Nord Stream 2 is unlicensed, and oil-indexed Russian gas is now expensive anyway. Over-reliance on Russia was a mistake, and "If you make a second time, it's not a mistake." Gas, sold as reliable, affordable and flexible, may suffer lasting reputational damage, and Middle East producers must rebuild their name as reliable suppliers.
- 1970s precedent: the oil shocks brought \~170 GW of new nuclear (\~40% of today's plants), fuel-efficiency standards that cut an average car's use from \~20 to 10 litres per 100 km, and the North Sea boom on tax incentives and fast licensing. This time he expects a stronger nuclear comeback (including small modular reactors), faster electrification of transport in Asia, faster renewables with batteries, and more coal in China, Indonesia and India.
- "the age of electricity": power demand grows twice as fast as total energy demand (AI, data centers, EVs), and grids are its "Achilles heel". Last year four times as much renewable capacity as was connected sat idle for lack of grid capacity. Governments must make grid investment attractive and simplify permitting; cyber attacks on grids are rising in intensity and frequency.
- Coal: China burns \~55% of the world's coal. After years of flat demand he expects an uptick in China, a rebound in the US and even European countries falling back on their own coal reserves.
- Europe's three strategic mistakes: over-reliance on Russian gas; turning its back on nuclear (about a third of European electricity in the late 1990s, \~15% now; he likened France's plan to cut nuclear from 75% to 50% to selling the Eiffel Tower); and dropping the ball on solar, which Europe pioneered 25 years ago but China now dominates. Solar was 75% of all power plants installed worldwide in 2025.
- Technology bets: with a magic wand he would pick batteries, which change both renewables and transport; Europe trails China (CATL alone has nearly 40% of the market, per Tangen) but can compete in advanced batteries. EVs went from 5% of global car sales five years ago to 25% last year; the war will speed this up, and electric trucks come next.
- Competitiveness: Europe's power costs two to three times US levels (Tangen), and some energy-intensive legacy industries cannot compete because "this price differential in terms of energy will not change" soon. Europe should support selected legacy industries, push tomorrow's (AI, clean tech) and lean on strong fundamentals, such as a single market of 450 million.
- AI: "there is no AI without electricity"; one medium-sized data center uses as much as a town of 100,000 households, around the clock. The AI race among the US, China and Europe will be decided by technology and by the availability and price of electricity, where Europe has most work to do.
- IEA method: scenarios, not forecasts, and "Data always wins". It was too conservative on solar 10 years ago (China surprised everyone) but right that oil demand would slow with China's economy and EVs. Its core budget is just €22 million. Norway, a reliable supplier when Russia invaded Ukraine, should be proud, not "guilty".
