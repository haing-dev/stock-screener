### 361. Ignacio Galán — Iberdrola: turning a utility green, slow permits and the myth of free electricity (NBIM, Oct 2023)

*Chair of Iberdrola, the Spanish utility he joined in 2001 after earlier careers in batteries, shipyards and aeronautics, and telecoms; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen in London the day after a tour of Iberdrola sites in Spain.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bwDsLSGTtPI)

**Takeaway:** Galán turned a utility that burned coal, oil and gas into a renewables leader by closing fossil plants and investing in wind and pumped hydro from 2001. He blames the slow transition on short-term politics, shifting rules and sequential permitting, and insists renewable power is not free: its capex and financing must be paid even with no fuel to buy.

- The bet: joining in early 2001, a few years after the Kyoto Protocol, he planned to close all of Iberdrola's coal and oil plants, invest in onshore wind and convert hydro plants to pumped storage. The board approved a €60bn investment plan, "a lot of money" then, and after three more meetings agreed to change the company's colour from blue to green.
- Slow transition: electricity is only \~25% of final energy (75% is fossil fuels), and almost half of that electricity still comes from fossil fuels. Everyone agrees on 2050, but "the short time is always overlapping the long-term Vision", and governments change the rules whenever something happens.
- Europe's crisis: had Europe invested more in renewables instead of depending on Russian gas, the problems of the past two years would have been minimized. Politicians taxed electricity and tried to reform the power market, but "it was not the electricity it was the gas" that drove prices, and on-off taxes and charges hold back investment.
- Permits: national, regional and local authorities (environment, land, transmission) each add restrictions, "one after the other never they in parallel"; an environmental filing alone needs almost two years of studies. He welcomed the European Parliament's move to treat the build-out as a national interest, cap permitting at two years, and pre-approve areas and grid connections.
- Security: Europe's Fit for 55 target would be better framed as self-sufficiency, because "the only way to keep the light on is to have our own electricity" generated from Europe's own natural resources.
- Technology: no single winner; it depends on local resources. Solar makes no sense where it isn't sunny, onshore wind where it isn't windy, or offshore wind far from the coast at high cost: "all Technologies are needed". Fusion now works with some control at very small scale, but he is unsure of its timing or whether it will be competitive.
- No free electricity: people who call it free "don't distinguish" fixed from variable costs. Wind and solar burn no fuel, but capex, financing and operating costs still have to be paid.
- Execution, which Tangen calls exceptional: define every detail before construction starts, and give people ownership so they take pride in delivering on time and cost. Offshore wind teams in France and Germany showed him a foundation-drilling system they invented and a way to protect whales from construction noise.
- Developing leaders: put young high-potentials in charge of a small solar plant with 100–200 people, where they must deliver on time and cost and negotiate with local authorities and vendors; then move them from a 50 million capex project to 200 million and on to a billion. Leadership needs physical contact, not screens; at 73 he travels about 200 days a year.
- Management: "your success is the success of your people and the failure of your people is your failure", so surround yourself with people more capable than you. He asks for "everything for yesterday", and every decision goes through a Monday operations committee in a matrix of country heads and global business heads.
- Learning across markets: Iberdrola's US networks (New York, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine) are run by Brazilians, because their grid design resembles its Brazilian distribution networks. Solar panels are its largest purchase and come mostly from China or from Chinese makers in nearby countries such as Vietnam.
- Screen implication: a utility can be hit by taxes and market redesign in a price spike it did not cause, so regulatory and political risk is a manual check that no margin history captures.
