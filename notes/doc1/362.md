### 362. Patricia Poppe — PG&E: rebuilding after a climate-driven bankruptcy, lean operations and trading expense for capital (NBIM, Aug 2024)

*CEO of PG&E, the Californian gas and electric utility, since 2021, when it was in crisis after its wildfire bankruptcy; an engineer who learned lean manufacturing in the auto industry and previously ran a Michigan utility. NBIM owned over 1% of PG&E (just under $0.5bn).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=03DXkQLyRX8)

**Takeaway:** Poppe treats PG&E's recovery from its wildfire bankruptcy as an operational, not financial, transformation built on a safety management system, a lean operating system and "breakthrough thinking". Her "simple affordable model" cuts operating costs to fund climate-resilient capital, which she says delivers earnings growth while bills rise less than inflation, and she sees EVs and well-chosen data centers raising grid use and lowering unit costs.

- The crisis: wildfires destroyed towns and took lives, made worse by a grid not built for new extreme climate conditions, and "our company went bankrupt"; some call it the first climate-driven bankruptcy. PG&E supplies gas and power in Northern and Central California to 16 million people.
- "make it right and do it fast": advice from GM's Mary Barra, passed on from Warren Buffett, meaning repair the harm and make the system safe as quickly as possible. On her first day she went alone to the union hall, in her pickup and jeans, then drove to Paradise, still "apocalyptic" 3 years after the fire.
- Operational, not financial: her "performance Playbook" combines a safety management system, a lean operating system (bottom-up continuous improvement she took from autos into power plants and utility operations) and breakthrough thinking. She hired an entirely new leadership team (only the general counsel stayed), mostly utility professionals "peppered" with outsiders for a competitive mindset.
- Competing as a monopoly: performance is made visible daily, with cost targets and an operating and maintenance expense reduction goal next to reliability targets; as in autos, "quality and cost could both be improved simultaneously". The executive team reviews yesterday's performance every day at 10:20 a.m.
- Culture: over 2,000 leaders have taken a 4-day breakthrough workshop, and employee injuries are down over 50% (70% in some areas). Her "lead with love" approach is tracked by a joy survey: over 80% enjoy and are proud to work at PG&E (up double digits), and about 50% feel loved at work.
- Safety vs innovation: standard work is the safest known way to do a job; the gap to a breakthrough goal (zero injuries, 10,000 miles of underground lines) is where innovation happens, and then a new standard is set, "what Toyota taught us".
- Capital for expense: undergrounding the 8% of lines in dense tree areas replaces vegetation management that cost $1.5bn to as much as $2bn a year. PG&E can "trade a dollar of expense for $7 of capital and keep a customer's Bill flat", so $2bn a year of tree-cutting could fund $14bn a year of resilient infrastructure; the goal is a lower ratio (say 5 to 1) so bills fall.
- "simple affordable model": invest in climate-resilient infrastructure while cutting O&M (self-insurance, reworked vegetation and inspection programs, small ideas such as faster laptops for new staff), so bills rise well less than inflation and in some cases fall. She says it delivers both "earnings growth and cost savings for customers".
- Why be listed: "investors make us sharper". Rates are regulated and customers captive, but utilities "do compete for Capital", and investors know "safer utilities are more economically viable", so she cares where PG&E ranks against peers. She sees "no tradeoff" between serving customers and investors.
- Cheaper decarbonization: PG&E delivered 100% carbon-free power last year. Decarbonizing its area by 70%, transport included, would cut household energy spending by 20%, because electricity is a more efficient fuel than gasoline.
- EVs and the grid: she calls EVs the best thing ever to happen to the grid. Distribution grids run at \~45% utilization, overbuilt 15–20% for a few peak days; charging EVs on the midday solar surplus and, as bidirectional cars arrive, discharging them at peaks would raise utilization and lower unit costs. Her area has 560,000 EVs (almost 9,000 MW of potential supply), and 28% of new vehicles sold last year were electric, up from 22% (43% in Santa Clara County).
- Data centers: there is "good load and bad load". A gigawatt of data centers needing \~$0.5–1.5bn of grid build-out can cut other customers' bills by 1–2%, because the new revenue exceeds the cost, up to a break-even point. PG&E's area has 3.5 GW of data-center demand, none of it in its earnings guidance, so it is "all upside".
- Nuclear: California extended Diablo Canyon, one of the best-performing US nuclear plants, by five years, and PG&E is seeking a 20-year license extension. Baseload carbon-free power has a role, but PG&E won't build small modular reactors soon: it does not want to be "bleeding edge" and carry "cost overrun risk" while its grid needs the capital.
- Screen implication: even a regulated monopoly can be bankrupted when its assets fail in a disaster, so check climate and wildfire exposure by hand; and a utility that swaps operating expense for capital spends heavily by design, so judge it on bills and regulation as well as free cash flow.
