### 364. Robin Zeng — CATL: 37.5% of the battery market, five-year depreciation and why Europe's battery makers struggle (NBIM, Sep 2024)

*Founder and CEO of CATL, the world's largest battery maker, which he took from zero to 37.5% market share in about 12 years; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5VIXjjw4u9A)

**Takeaway:** Zeng credits CATL's share to "right products" built on deep electrochemistry, a team that has worked on lithium-ion for 25 years and over 20,000 R&D engineers. He thinks rivals fail on design, process and equipment, sees innovation moving so fast that production lines must be depreciated over 5 years, and says grid storage needs defect rates measured in parts per billion, not per million.

- Share: Tangen said \~40%; Zeng corrected it to 37.5%, reached in about 12 years, though his team began on lithium-ion 25 years ago with batteries for phones and laptops. What matters is the "right products", which depend on the right chemistry and materials. CATL makes EV batteries (for Tesla, Chinese and other carmakers) and storage batteries that steady renewable supply.
- Competition: "the competitor is ourself", because CATL still needs new chemistries beyond LFP and NCM, for example for low-temperature performance. He welcomes rivals who invent ("it's not a copycat"), since competition in invention keeps the industry healthy.
- China's edge: Chinese carmakers listen to consumers and design faster than anyone, so CATL trained itself to grow, ramp production and deliver cost and quality fast. It has over 20,000 R&D engineers, several hundred with PhDs, working on materials, future chemistries (using AI for science), battery management and software.
- Talent: Tangen noted that two out of three widely cited battery papers come from China (only 12% from the US) and China has more than 50 battery graduate programs. Zeng says electrochemistry was a low-paid field that US students skip for finance and semiconductors, while many Chinese students took it up; when Germans asked him, during Angela Merkel's visit to China almost 12 years ago, why Germany couldn't make good batteries, he pointed to China's many universities still working on electrochemistry. To catch up, the US and Germany must start with education.
- Hardest parts: understanding the electrochemical system in the real world, predicting a 20–25 year life from accelerated tests, reliability, scale-up and low cost, since batteries are always compared with a combustion engine refined for over 100 years.
- Why European makers struggle: a wrong design, a wrong process and the wrong equipment. At scale that brings yield and utilization problems, then reliability problems and, two or 3 years later, safety problems; a design that tests well for a day fails if it ignores side reactions and future risks.
- Pace: innovation is accelerating as money and people pour in, and new chemistries keep arriving, so CATL depreciates production lines over 5 years. Solid-state batteries sit at technology readiness level 4: solid-to-solid interfaces diffuse about two orders of magnitude slower than liquid electrolytes and need heavy pressure to work.
- Other chemistries: sodium-ion is in small-scale production for carmakers. Its energy density is lower than LFP's, so buyers choose LFP when LFP is cheaper, but a second generation could replace 20–30% of LFP in smaller, shorter-range cars at first. Its condensed-matter battery performs very well but is very expensive today; CATL aims to bring it to high-end cars that need energy density.
- Materials: technology, not hype, picks the winners. Investors once rushed into nickel and cobalt, but CATL cut cobalt from 33% to 3% and then to zero with LFP, which needs no nickel either, so only people who understand where chemistry is going know which mining investments are good. CATL owns a lithium mine (Tangen), and its plants in Germany and Hungary still source mostly from China; he suggests making graphite in Norway with cheap hydropower.
- Energy storage: perhaps half the size of the transport market but harder. Systems must last 20–25 years, and a 1 GWh system holds at least 1.5 million cells and 7–9 million components, like a Boeing 787. At parts-per-million defect rates something breaks every day, so CATL targets "one parts per billion"; makers who call storage easy will "pay a lot of money" 5–10 years later.
- Culture and priorities: refine yourself, enable others and strive for innovation; staff get stock options, and CATL batteries power 15 million cars. His personal priorities are to share his wealth with society, stay fit and find successors, and he builds plants in poor regions so parents need not leave their children to find work in cities.
- Screen implication: in fast-moving hardware, compare depreciation lives with the technology cycle (CATL writes production lines off over 5 years); long asset lives flatter margins and returns.
