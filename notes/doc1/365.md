### 365. Wael Sawan — Shell: a 10-quarter sprint, playing only where it wins, and gas through the 2040s (NBIM, Apr 2024)

*CEO of Shell for about a year, a chemical engineer who spent 26–27 years in the company across upstream, gas, retail and renewables; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen, whose fund owned 3% of Shell (more than $6bn).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=evHLTxDJsJU)

**Takeaway:** Sawan runs Shell as a multi-energy company that plays only where it has an edge (deep water, LNG, trading, marketing and low-carbon molecules) and partners or pulls back where it doesn't, such as pure renewable generation and home energy. He sees gas demand growing at least through the 2040s and oil products peaking perhaps early next decade, and warns that protectionism against cheap Chinese clean tech will slow the transition.

- Advice he took as a long-time insider: approach the job as if "you were an external hire", question "the sacred cows" and "look at the comparators and say where are we actually winning and where are we not".
- 10-quarter Sprint: long project lead times make 5–10 year promises easy, so the new team picked 10 quarters to be measurable and earn trust. The goal is "more value and less emissions": the executive committee went from nine to seven, over 75 operational, financial and carbon targets became eight (four financial, four carbon), and bi-weekly and monthly performance reviews run at the top. The themes are "performance discipline and simplification".
- Where Shell leads: deep water (Gulf of Mexico projects Vito, Whale and Sparta; Brazil); LNG, where it is the largest player among international oil companies, second only to QatarEnergy; one of the world's largest energy trading houses; and marketing, with 46,000 stations.
- Where it is weak: pure renewable generation, which needs no complex systems, carries a different risk profile and doesn't use Shell's project-management and integration skills. Installing solar panels in Africa under Shell's safety rules (two escort vehicles, harnesses) left it "dis synergizing on the cost structure", so it now partners; it also changed course on selling power to homes when "that didn't work out for us".
- Green electrons: in the US, where Shell is one of the largest gas and power players, business customers want green power that is scarce. Shell develops renewable projects, dilutes to a 20% equity stake and takes a disproportionately larger share of the output. Offshore wind suits a deep-water company better than onshore.
- Multi-energy: lower-carbon oil, the most LNG among the majors, Europe's largest green hydrogen plant (being built in Rotterdam), the world's largest biofuel blending, carbon capture (Northern Lights in Norway) and nature-based carbon credits. EV charging fits its retail sites; biofuels fit shipping, aviation and road freight.
- Shareholders: Tangen noted that markets have rewarded both renewables pushes and later retreats. Sawan says shareholders want "investable business models" with "cash flow growth" and "the appropriate shareholder returns". Shell won't be "the distillation of all the asks of everyone"; it aims to be "the investment case through the energy transition", not a pure oil bet nor a wind or solar company.
- Outlook: fossil fuels are over 80% of the energy system, new sources so far add to rather than replace old ones, and 700 million people still lack electricity. Gas has a growing role "at least through the 2040s", replacing coal and backing up intermittent renewables; oil products demand grows through this decade and could peak as early as the next. Generative AI and crypto mining add new demand.
- Policy: the US Inflation Reduction Act is critical; it created "fear of missing out" elsewhere and strengthened the EU's Fit for 55. With long investment horizons, a significant change in fiscal or regulatory rules "could undermine confidence in these Investments".
- Geopolitics: a polarized, protectionist world will slow the transition, because cheap Chinese products are its biggest enabler: over 50% of EVs sold and of renewable generation deployed last year were in China, with EVs under $10,000. Local production costs more and competes worse with oil and gas, energy corridors may shift towards allies (Red Sea disruption), and intermittent renewables will raise energy price volatility.
- Scope 3 and the climate lawsuit: roughly 90% of a car fuel's emissions come from its use, less than 10% from the whole supply chain. Shell owns its Scope 1 and 2 emissions, has Scope 3 targets and works with customers (EV charging, biofuel blends, lubricants), but argues on appeal that one company shouldn't carry obligations its customers don't, and "it's not right for a court to actually determine the strategy of the company"; it expected a ruling towards the end of the year.
- Culture: honesty, integrity and respect, shaped by the 2004 reserves crisis, but Shell can be "complex bureaucratic". He wants leaders "tough-minded but kind-hearted". Shell cut its \~1,700 safety and asset standards by 70% and is tailoring risk frameworks to each business (deep water differs from fuel retail), though everyone, the CEO included, still holds the handrail.
- People: staff rotate roles about every four years to learn the whole company, though external-facing and R&D roles need tenure; retention is among the industry's highest, and the top people metric is the quality of team leadership.
- Screen implication: an oil major's mid-cycle returns depend on staying where it has an edge, so check whether management prunes ventures that dilute returns (Shell's retreat from pure renewable generation and home energy) rather than chasing every transition business.
