### 366. Rasmus Errboe — Ørsted: offshore wind's boom and bust, a US stop-work order and choosing value over volume (NBIM, Aug 2026)

*CEO of Ørsted, the world's largest offshore wind company and a former Danish oil and gas group, for about a year and a half; a lawyer who joined in 2012 as the youngest employee in the legal department. Interviewed by Nicolai Tangen.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lf9vFXK0III)

**Takeaway:** Errboe says Ørsted set the bar too high in 2023, chasing "volume rather than value" just as offshore wind's costs rose \~50%, and stepped back from the US too slowly; then a US stop-work order hit an 80%-built project in the middle of an €8bn equity raise. His turnaround narrows Ørsted to bottom-fixed offshore wind in stable markets, and he argues that Europe's fossil import bill and repeated energy shocks make a mostly renewable system the cheapest for decades.

- Stop-work order: on Friday 22 August 2025, within 10 minutes of a call from the general counsel, Ørsted received a one-page US order to stop building a project that was 80% done. It came 11 days after Ørsted announced an €8bn equity raise, the biggest ever for renewables and in the Nordics. Like a bridge stopped 50 m short of the toll booth: "You have it all built but you don't get your cash flow." Ørsted sued the US government on 4 September, having spent 7–9 years getting its permits.
- History: the world's first offshore wind turbine went up in Denmark \~35 years ago. Ørsted, formed by a 2006 merger, turned from a fossil-heavy company into the offshore wind leader and sold its oil and gas business (2017, per Tangen), "a story around execution". Denmark led because its framework has been "relatively robust for decades". Europe still has only \~38 GW of offshore wind.
- Cost cycle: the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) fell \~70% from 2015 to 2020, then rose \~50% from 2020 to 2025 with higher interest rates, supply-chain costs and tender rules that made Europe "a test lab", while oil and gas companies piled in and made competition one "that was not healthy". Many tenders were won but never built. Economics are now stabilizing, and he expects costs to fall again.
- Mistakes: at its June 2023 capital markets day Ørsted "set the bar too high" and was too focused on "volume rather than value". It wrote down Ocean Wind in the US that autumn but did not step back fast enough; in hindsight it would have been "better off without" its US projects (Ocean Wind, abandoned; Sunrise, still being built). In Europe it skipped tenders and delivered its core business.
- Political risk: he now prefers markets with bipartisan support and regulatory stability, and develops projects so that Ørsted can afford to step back at any stage.
- Owners: the Danish state, which owns 50%, put up \~€4bn of the raise, alongside two other large shareholders, global investors and more than 200,000 Danish retail investors; governance is at arm's length through the board. Equinor owns \~10%, and he won't discuss a merger. He admits the risk of now being too cautious (Ørsted lost the latest Danish tender) but must deliver for the investors he "looked in the eye".
- Turnaround: a three-year plan, six quarters in, "very much on track" with "firm ground under our feet". Ørsted now does only bottom-fixed offshore wind, where it is most competitive: it left floating wind (LCOE "significantly higher"; it will come, but later than people expect) and green fuels, and sold its European onshore business. Staff will fall \~25%, announced early for clarity.
- Energy security: events in Iran earlier this year set off a second energy crisis and showed that Europe's close to €400bn a year of fossil-fuel imports is unsustainable. Climate urgency is unchanged, but the case is now affordability too: a largely renewable system is Europe's cheapest for decades.
- Momentum: on 26 January nine states committed in Hamburg to tender up to 300 GW of offshore wind by 2050, with more clarity on how; Denmark just tendered \~2 GW, and the Netherlands, Belgium and the UK have tenders coming. His one ask is that governments deliver on their stated intentions.
- Demand: European electricity demand will likely close to double by 2050, and "AI doesn't need molecule". Even at 1–2% growth a year for 15 years Europe needs solar, onshore and offshore wind, batteries and peak gas; offshore wind has a higher load factor, sits near industrial load centers and complements solar.
- Scale: 1 GW of offshore wind costs DKK 30–40bn. Ørsted's biggest farm, in the UK, covers 135 times Central Park with turbines over 200 m tall, one rotation powering a family for 24 hours; Hornsea 3's turbines are close to 300 m. It has more than 1,500 turbines spinning, and decades of turbine data show no trend of less wind (this year is above the norm).
- Hard parts: hardly a week passes without a project off plan somewhere. Wind farms take four to five years to build, and tenders won now deliver in 2032, so managing timelines and commitments is the core risk; his first-year priorities were a new strategy, a new team, getting the biggest decisions right and resetting communication.
- China: Ørsted is not active in China. The industry already buys monopiles, cables and vessels from China (Ørsted uses Chinese monopiles), but Chinese turbines are not part of the mix under European frameworks, and Europe's supply chain has invested €14bn in factories, ports and vessels in the last 3 years.
- Screen implication: a long-dated contracted asset can turn into a liability overnight if its permit is pulled, and capacity targets pursued while unit costs rise preceded Ørsted's write-downs; regulatory stability and value-over-volume discipline are manual checks.
