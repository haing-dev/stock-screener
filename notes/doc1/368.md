### 368. Patrick Doyle & Josh Kobza — Restaurant Brands International: franchisee returns first, Burger King's execution fix and Tim Hortons abroad (NBIM, Mar 2024)

*Executive chair Patrick Doyle (formerly CEO of Domino's Pizza) and CEO Josh Kobza of Restaurant Brands International (RBI), owner of Burger King, Tim Hortons, Popeyes and Firehouse; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6XzayLv65ls)

**Takeaway:** A franchisor earns a royalty off franchisees' sales, so the business is only as healthy as the franchisees' cash-on-cash returns. RBI (\~40% operating margins, capex \~10% of EBITDA) says Burger King's problem is execution and dated US restaurants, not food, and sees Tim Hortons and global coffee as the long runway.

- Industry: "a very stable industry", since people eat "three times a day 365 days a year". The "formalization of the restaurant sector", especially in developing markets, is "a decades long" source of structural growth. RBI's brands sit in the four largest quick-service segments globally.
- Economics: "something like 40% operating margins" and "pretty low Capital intensity", with "something like 10%" of EBITDA going into capex.
- Doyle on franchising: income comes mostly from royalties, "off the top line as a royalty as a percentage of sales". So "the health of the business comes down to the cash on cash returns for the franchisees": with a great return "they're going to build more" and reinvest, which speeds up the brand's growth. The best business "is the one that gives the best return to the franchisee". Kobza says franchisee profitability was one of RBI's biggest focuses over the last year.
- Demand (Mar 2024): in Q4 2023 traffic was positive at Tim Hortons and at Burger King, the latter "for the first time in a while". The driver to watch is jobs: employed people, especially two-income households, value prepared meals, so "tell me what employment levels look like in markets". Job cuts would be "more of a concern".
- Weight-loss drugs: "nothing that we can see yet". Their quick math: if 10–20% of people take them and cut calories by 10–20%, calories consumed fall 1–4%, "a negligible effect" on the industry.
- Burger King plan: focus on the Whopper (launched 1957), flame grilling ("we're the only large player that does flame grilling") and "have it your way". The Reclaim the Flame plan, \~$400 million announced about 18 months earlier, modernizes US restaurants ("we fell behind on that"), puts tens of millions of dollars into equipment together with franchisees, and adds training for a consistent "gold standard" Whopper.
- Doyle: Domino's problem was that "people didn't think the food was very good"; RBI's brands already have "the best-in-class food" in their categories. The gap is execution: service speed at Popeyes, and at Burger King US, where "half of them really need to be remodeled" (far less of an issue abroad). "it's more an execution issue here than a food issue".
- Tim Hortons is RBI's biggest business by cash flow, "bigger than Burger King". Coffee is "a very routine based business" with daily visits, loyalty and high margins. In Canada it's "love yes penetration no": growth comes from afternoons and evenings, cold drinks, and restaurant count growing at least with the population (\~1.5% a year). Abroad, China and India excite them most.
- Plant-based has not taken off in the US (a "low single digit percentage" of sales), but some European markets are at 20–30% of sales, higher in the north than the south.
- People: "north of 500,000" work across the system, mostly in their first job. A US restaurant can run "up to 200% turnover" in crew vs below 100% in a better one; the restaurant general manager makes the difference. "you hire attitude you teach skills": "the interview you have with that person is the best they're ever going to be". Smaller franchisees who are in their restaurants every day tend to do best.
- Doyle (60) on mentoring Kobza (37): Kobza is "right 99.5% of the time", so the chair's job is often to say "just go". Speed is "how you create competitive advantage", and it rests on trust with franchisees: "if the trust level is high you move twice as fast"; broken trust "throws sand in the gears".
- Doyle's diagnostic: ask any 10 people what the company should be better at and "80 or 90%" give the same answer; then ask why it isn't being done. At Domino's it was the food, so they fixed it (Dave Brandon was CEO, Doyle ran the US business and hired Russell Weiner as CMO, who oversaw the ads admitting it).
- Culture comes from what leaders do more than from systems: "if we wear suits and ties you end up seeing it in the restaurants". Kobza says Doyle taught him to be open with investors, franchisees and staff about "the good the bad".
