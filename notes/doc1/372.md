### 372. Jesper Brodin — IKEA: democratic design, climate work that cuts costs and a late but life-saving move online (NBIM, Jun 2025)

*CEO of Ingka Group (IKEA), who joined IKEA in 1995 and was Ingvar Kamprad's assistant from late 1999; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F9COKcZr9co)

**Takeaway:** IKEA's edge is "democratic design": function, quality, form and sustainability at a low price, which takes scale and product engineering that rivals struggle to copy. Brodin says climate work has cut costs rather than added them (energy bill 29% lower than before the Paris agreement), and that a late shift online saved IKEA in the pandemic.

- Stores leave "nothing um left to chance": a person greets you and hands you a bag (Kamprad's insight: you start shopping sooner), food comes early because "you don't make good business deals on an empty stomach", kids can play in Småland, and low-price "breathtaking items" are on show. Per Tangen, stores get almost 900 million visitors a year.
- "our job is not really to be cheap": IKEA serves "people with thin wallets", so a low price should come with the story of how the product was made, its function and its quality.
- Democratic design has five parts: function, quality (only sofas with washable covers), Scandinavian form, sustainability and low price. Doing all five needs scale and "a lot of knowledge from an engineering point of view", which is why it is hard to copy: "you don't have to be rich to be smart".
- Range: 9,000–10,000 products per market. About 20% change each year: roughly half are colour or quality updates and "at least 10%" are entirely new. About 20% are "top families" (Billy, Pax and other classics), also renewed: the Pax wardrobe now clicks together in 5 minutes instead of taking two people 25.
- Flat pack: Kamprad and designer Gillis Lundgren took the legs off a side table (Lövet) and saved "approximately 80% of the air", cutting both price and climate footprint.
- Kamprad (founded IKEA in 1943 at 17, per Tangen) was thrifty (he kept his old Volvo for life), "a true entrepreneur" with vision but "not so good at strategy". He loved the store and factory floors, "detested the boardrooms", and "understood how to scale business and create value through scale". His values outlived him because they are built into recruitment and ways of working, not slogans.
- Sustainability pays: customers rate the environment highly but won't pay extra (a single mother of two in Belgrade: "I don't have the money"). Yet "everything we have done in IKEA um to become climate smart has actually made us save money", through less or circular material and renewable energy: "Our energy bill in IKEA is 29% lower than before Paris agreement." It needs investment and "your whole scale behind it otherwise you don't get the financial benefits", and it strengthened the brand "massively".
- Online: IKEA was late. Customer behaviour changed around 2012–15, and about 8 years ago customers warned that without IKEA online "I'm going to start to deselect you". It re-engineered its logistics backbone, and the shift "saved the company during the pandemic"; otherwise "We would have been in massive red numbers".
- City stores: big-city customers want an IKEA about "10 minutes away". After "so many mistakes and so many miscalculations" over seven years, the format is a smaller big store that sells everything. London's Oxford Street store, "one of the biggest investments we've done", was second in visits in its first week, but "we need to give it a year" to judge it financially (Jun 2025).
- Circularity: take-back service and second-hand sales in all stores, plus a customer-to-customer platform started in Norway and Spain, though second-hand is not yet IKEA's biggest carbon cut. The printed catalogue was dropped as "a waste of resources".
- Geopolitics (Jun 2025): after decades of easier multilateral trade, the last \~10 years got harder. Quoting someone at Davos, "we have left one world order and we haven't yet established a new one". Governments now move faster than companies; IKEA keeps investing long term and mitigates trade restrictions as they come.
- AI is already "revolutionizing" goods flow and saving cost, and helps customer service at scale and home planning.
- Speed: "one of the biggest risks for us is that we are too slow and too bureaucratic", so he cuts meetings and pushes people to take risks. His "banana card" co-signs a colleague's mistake in advance. From his first job building textile sourcing in Karachi (1995), applying IKEA's social and environmental values to suppliers also strengthened its finances.
