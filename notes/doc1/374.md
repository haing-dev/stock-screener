### 374. Niels B. Christiansen — LEGO: momentum growth, a 50% yearly product refresh and family ownership's long view (NBIM, Sep 2025)

*CEO of the LEGO Group since 2017, an engineer who earlier ran Netcom and spent years at Danfoss; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mf5EuG0Sm30)

**Takeaway:** LEGO is a momentum business: fast growth pays for the investment that drives more growth, but about 15% a year is the most a company that builds its own factories can sustain. Christiansen pairs a creative engine (half the range renewed every year) with an identical-factory operating model and a yearly productivity drive, and uses family ownership's long view to build the brand in China.

- Growth: per Tangen, close to 15% a year for the last few years in "a pretty flat industry" (Sep 2025). "it's really a momentum business": a decent growth pace pays for the forward-looking investments that drive growth again. A consultant found "The best projector of high growth is that we grew fast the year before".
- The limit: 15% is "probably at the limit" LEGO can sustain long term, since it makes all its own products, so growth means new factories and distribution centres every year. The risks are complexity growing too fast and capacity not keeping up.
- Past stalls: five to seven years around 2000, and two years around 2016–17 when LEGO lost momentum. Creative people "can create a lot of complexity in very short term", so his job is balancing creativity with a structured operating model. Success is itself "a big danger": it breeds complacency.
- Product engine: about 600 designers, all in Billund, get a free day every two weeks, and "every year we change 50% of our product line" (\~400–450 products). "we're no better at any point in time than the creativity we are coming out with right now". A structured series of "P meetings" tests ideas against trends and hot IPs.
- IP partners (Formula 1, Star Wars, Pokémon, Super Mario): knowing "what the movie slates are" a year or two out improves prioritization. Evergreens such as the Titanic stay for years; the biggest sets have 10,000 pieces.
- Platform: there are 500 billion to 1,000 billion LEGO bricks in the world, and he calls the brick "the ultimate platform or the ultimate modular system". Backward compatibility means a new set works with grandparents' bricks, so each set adds to what a child already owns.
- LEGO competes for children's time, not just with other toys. Screens make it "harder to stay relevant", so it is in games, social platforms and digital IP while staying authentic. It is now a brand for everybody: balanced between boys and girls, and selling to teens and adults.
- Family ownership (93 years, same family) lets LEGO "take decisions with a slightly longer perspective", carry investment programmes through and ride out quarterly "blips or hiccups". It helps when brand, retail and product must be built at once, as in China. The long-term vision runs to 2032 or 2039, when the most active owner turns 60.
- China playbook: start in the biggest cities with brand campaigns and LEGO-only stores, which work better "than a branding campaign", then roll out to tier-two, -three and -four cities, and add local manufacturing. LEGO has more than 1,000 stores (500 in China) and 11 LEGOLAND parks; China's first opened in Shanghai a month earlier. India is "probably 10 years back on that journey".
- Execution: the "partner for productivity" programme squeezes out significant savings every year, because "productivity is what's paying for all the super exciting things we can do". Its six factories "look exactly the same" and run the same way, so an operator from Billund could work in the Czech Republic or Vietnam the next day.
- Simplicity: a month into the job (\~8 years ago) he attacked too many meetings with too many people. A "commercial triangle" (chief commercial, operating and marketing officers) now makes day-to-day decisions, and the leadership team meets 1–1.5 hours a month plus three days four times a year. For a rich Scandinavian company, consensus culture is "a bigger danger".
- Failure: after-action reviews run roughly daily, with at least four a year big enough for his attention, e.g. Hidden Side, an augmented-reality theme that never took off. After testing 600 materials, LEGO dropped bricks made from recycled PET bottles: "it did not lower CO2" and compromised quality.
- Planning: a detailed four-year plan (three felt constant, five too far), matching a two-year product cycle; the 2027 range gets approved in a couple of months. Better to execute something "80% right" rigorously than to perfect the last percent; sometimes "it makes sense to go slow to go fast".
- Leadership: clear guardrails let many leaders decide, while micromanagement means "you use one brain instead of many". At Netcom (\~1999–2001) he launched the world's first Bluetooth product, which failed, and saw revenue jump from DKK 1 billion to 3.5 billion in one to three years, a lesson in how fast growth strains execution.
