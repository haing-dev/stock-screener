### 376. Russell Weiner — Domino's: the 2009 pizza turnaround, a gold standard for taste and technology as the enabler (NBIM, Jan 2024)

*CEO of Domino's Pizza, who joined as chief marketing officer in 2008 after 11 years at Pepsi; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x19sUe7BwFA)

**Takeaway:** Domino's 2008–09 turnaround fixed a product that cost cuts had slowly degraded, but marketing mattered more: tests showed the brand itself made the pizza taste worse. Weiner now holds every change to a "gold standard" and, after years of being seen as a technology company, wants Domino's to win on "deliciousness", with technology as the enabler.

- Scale (Jan 2024): Domino's is in 94 markets, and pepperoni, cheese, sausage and mushroom make up 40–50% of US sales. Staff spot-check stores two or three times a year, alongside customer feedback.
- The 2008 low: Weiner joined in September 2008 as Domino's headed for "our third negative year of same store sales in a row". The stock fell from \~14 in September to "below $3" by November, and "a good number of our franchises were running below break even".
- Box-swap research: Domino's lost blind taste tests, but more striking, "we put our pizza in somebody else's box and all of a sudden it tasted better", and the rival's pizza tasted worse in Domino's box. So "the marketing was the bigger bigger piece of it": it had to change "50 years of of perceptions".
- The campaign: "new and improved" got 14 million Google hits, so it meant nothing. Domino's paired its brand's tension (a pizza company whose pizza didn't taste very good) with society's in 2009 (banks failing, carmakers seeking bailouts, nobody telling the truth). The honest ads showed customers' criticism and then surprised them with the new pizza; Domino's came "three days away from running out of pepperoni".
- How quality drifts: each cost-saving tweak may "statistically taste the same", but "five generations later this product does not taste like this product". Now Domino's has "a gold standard", and any change must beat it.
- Franchisees were brought along with a road show and a blind test of old vs new pizza, where only one person preferred the old one. The ad was pre-tested and had a backup: be bold, "jump out of that airplane but have the parachute", and "take educated risks".
- Chairman Dave Brandon approved the risky campaign while planning to leave for the University of Michigan, "with nothing to gain"; most CEOs would have run a safe "new and improved" ad worth maybe +1%. Weiner also credits former CEO Patrick Doyle.
- Digital: pizza chains pioneered online ordering and are "upwards of 80%" digital, but aggregators now let everyone from mom-and-pops to burger chains catch up. The next edge is big data and AI on customers and operations.
- Technology as enabler: Domino's was once complimented as a technology company that is "a marketing company that just happens to be a pizza company". But the five-year strategy shown to investors on December 7 (2023) leans into "deliciousness", because "when we spend too much time talking about technology even though we have the best pizza in the business we don't get credit for it".
- Operations: an auto-stretcher cuts the time to learn stretching dough from about 20 shifts to about two. COVID's volume surges and staff shortages forced a redesign of store workflow; boxes are now folded once instead of three times. 70% of box material is recycled.
- Pizza is a shared occasion, so rivals now add single-serve sandwiches and other items to head off the "veto vote", as Domino's did earlier.
- International: Italy failed because of the partner and a product made to taste "more like an Italian pizza" rather than like Domino's: "if you don't love who you are why should you expect anyone else to love". China is now "on fire" after a change of partner.
- Leadership: treat people as you want to be treated. Investors "should want a CEO and a leadership team that sometimes doesn't care what they think as long as it ends up in a better result". He calls himself "a heart holder in Domino's Pizza". His Pepsi mentor, Cie Nicholson, released him for a brand-manager job four months into working for her, and later got Domino's to interview him for CMO.
