### 377. Tony Xu — DoorDash: order density, a three-sided marketplace and a thousand days of fundraising (NBIM, Mar 2024)

*Co-founder and CEO of DoorDash, which he started with three co-founders as a Stanford student in 2013; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-70xtYBgLG8)

**Takeaway:** DoorDash grew from a merchant problem (refused delivery orders) and a bet on order density: restaurants have the most stores and the most frequent orders, so a network built on them could later deliver anything. Xu says it survived 2016–18 on a product with high retention and order frequency, built with no marketing money, and now pairs fast experiments with patience on new businesses.

- Origin (2013): the four founders interviewed hundreds of local merchants. A one-person macaron shop in Palo Alto showed them a booklet of delivery orders she had refused, money that could decide whether she made payroll. Outside pizza places and a few cities such as New York, almost no one in America offered delivery.
- Three tests before starting: do consumers want delivery from stores that never offered it; will stores partner and pay DoorDash to win new customers; and can it recruit drivers "for a wage that we could afford".
- Why restaurants: a delivery network needs "the most amount of order density", which takes frequency and coverage. Restaurants have the most stores (about a million vs a couple hundred thousand grocery stores); the bet was that their order frequency would build a last-mile network for every other category. "if you can deliver ice cream cold or pizza a hot you basically can deliver anything".
- Minimal product: on January 12, 2013 the founders put up paloaltodelivery.com, built in 45 minutes with eight PDF menus and a Google Voice number. The first order came about 45 minutes later; they delivered it themselves and took payment on a Square reader. The founders were "the human algorithm" until Y Combinator, and DoorDash launched officially on June 21, 2013.
- Scale (Mar 2024): deliveries now run "in the billions" a year. His YC application guessed \~$100 million of revenue by year seven; he was "off by a couple orders of magnitude".
- Three customers: consumers, merchants (now all of retail) and Dashers, and "it has to work for all of these audiences". 2013–16 proved it could work for all of them "and make a dollar doing it", in places of every socioeconomic background.
- Near death: from 2013 to 2018 the company "almost always felt broke", and the CEO's top job is "to make sure we never run out of cash". 2016–18 were "about a thousand days" of "wandering in the desert asking for money". With no marketing money, survival came from "a product that had higher retention and Order frequency"; close to a quarter of the \~250 staff left.
- Experiments: "the bias for Action is the way we solve and settle debates", with "hundreds to thousands of experiments per week", most of which never reach customers. He runs two management systems: forecastable scaled businesses, and new ones where you stay "stubborn about" the destination but flexible on the path (about five businesses now).
- "consumers are never satisfied": nobody credits faster delivery than last year or asks for higher prices or less selection, so DoorDash must keep improving the core while inventing new businesses.
- The "war on atoms": organizing information that isn't online, such as the last parking space downtown or the last bottle of Coke at a given price. AI (Mar 2024) helps with catalogues, customer support and fraud detection, but "we're very small percentage of the progress bar".
- International: DoorDash is in about 30 countries (29 outside the US) after teaming up with Helsinki-based Wolt, whose founder Miki Kuusi leads DoorDash's international business.
- Culture: "very high ownership High accountability", because delivery is "more of a utility service versus an entertainment system": a failed order is a lost sale for a thin-margin merchant and lost work for a driver. Hiring signals: executives who open with reasons not to hire them; bias for action (first COO Christopher Payne did deliveries with his son the evening after their first talk); detail (Prabir Adarkar turned a coffee chat into a 4-hour review of unit economics with a five-year model); and followership, i.e. how many people follow someone when they change jobs.
- On Meta's board he admires its willingness to fail despite its success and its habit of building leaders from within. Advice: "be proud of the person in the mirror" and "bet on the things that don't change".
