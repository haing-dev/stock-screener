### 378. Ana Botín — Santander: safer banks, Latin American growth and the "DVD to Netflix" shift (NBIM, Sep 2023)

*Executive chair of Banco Santander and the fourth generation of her family to lead it (eight years at JPMorgan in New York before joining); interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ya6zCI53P5A)

**Takeaway:** Regulated banks are far safer than before (three times the capital), but European banks are held back by slow growth, a fragmented market and a once-in-a-century change in how customers use financial products. Santander's answer is Latin American growth, group scale and rebuilding products around the customer.

- Purpose: "I care as much about how we make the numbers as well as doing the numbers". Doing it the right way is "the best thing for our shareholders" because it keeps a 165-year-old business sustainable. Where there is no manual, staff apply "simple personal and fair" (coined when she ran Santander UK).
- State of banking (Sep 2023): regulated banks hold "three times more Capital", from two trillion to six trillion in the global banking system, under much stricter supervision that in Europe reaches down to very small banks.
- European banks still suffer, and so do their valuations: growth over the last decade was "a third of what it's been" in the US; there is no single market, "a big tax on banks"; and "a one in a hundred years disruption" lets new business models overtake incumbents, who are often "the last ones to see it".
- Tangen asked whether banks are still needed now that PE takes corporate credit and consumer companies take deposits. Botín: yes, but in the form customers want. A current account looks unchanged to a banker but is "a totally different product" to a customer, like a DVD vs Netflix, and "pay later is the same as consumer lending it isn't".
- The board's first job is keeping the bank safe for depositors. She says Santander has consistently come out best in stress tests and has one of the lowest CDS \[?\] spreads in the world, "very close to JPMorgan".
- Where the digital shift stands (strategy told to the market in Feb 2023): done in the corporate bank, wealth management and payments, where staying "at the heart of our customers payments" is "existential to our business model". Good progress in the auto and digital consumer bank; most work is left in retail and commercial banking, "more than half of Santander".
- Growth comes from Latin America: Santander is the #1 bank in the region and #1 in auto finance across the continent. It added 40 million customers in the last eight years and targets 40 million more in the next three; Brazil and Mexico have 340–350 million people. Lending risk sits mostly in Europe and the US, but "a lot of our growth and our higher profitability" is in Latin America, where each bank is profitable.
- Rates (Sep 2023): "most of the trends right now are inflationary" (digital and AI are the deflationary exceptions). She expects the ECB and the Fed to err "on the side of going a bit too far"; 30 years in Latin America taught her "it's difficult to kill inflation without having positive real rates". Hence "higher rates now for longer the same way we had lower for longer".
- Culture after fast growth in the 1990s: when she took over, Santander was "quite far away from having a common culture"; nine years on it has one, while country leaders keep local accountability.
- Escalate early: a big US bank's CEO told her that problems people try to fix before escalating "become huge" by the time they reach the top, so she pushes staff to flag issues first.
- Decisions: "when you make a mistake fix it fast", and when someone is not up to the job, "Mission first". Her father asked her to leave the bank after the Central Hispano merger: "he did the right thing for the bank". Her grandfather, who made Santander a national bank by buying a much larger competitor, answered "time" when asked about his success.
- Women: Santander targets 20% to 30% women in senior leadership within five or six years. The barriers she cites are that women apply for a role only when fully qualified while men apply at 30–50%, and the "maternity tax".
