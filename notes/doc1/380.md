### 380. Andreas Berger — Swiss Re: diversification, pricing discipline and rising catastrophe losses (NBIM, Mar 2026)

*CEO of Swiss Re (relatively new in the role) and former head of its Corporate Solutions unit; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7OtEaqQ3c6M)

**Takeaway:** A reinsurer earns its returns from diversification and pricing discipline: Swiss Re's natural-catastrophe book returns 8% on capital alone but 40% inside the group. Rising catastrophe losses come mainly from people and assets moving into exposed areas, so Berger's answer is data, loss prevention and refusing to grow where rates are not adequate.

- What reinsurers do: they insure insurers ("the central bank of the insurance industry", though "Technically not 100% correct"). Local insurers buy cover to protect their balance sheets and borrow Swiss Re's global diversification, because their risks are not correlated. NBIM owns 1.6% of Swiss Re, \~$800 million (Tangen).
- Diversification is the economics: three largely uncorrelated units (Life & Health Re, P&C Re, Corporate Solutions for large corporates and their captives). Natural-catastrophe risk earns an 8% return on capital stand-alone but 40% at group level.
- Catastrophe losses: insured losses exceeded 100 billion in each of the last six years, rising \~5–7% a year in both frequency and severity. In 2024 economic losses were 318 billion, of which 137 billion were insured. The main driver is population growth and more assets "in the nicest areas", which are often catastrophe zones; climate (sea levels, temperatures) adds to it.
- "It's not an insurability problem. It's actually more than an affordability problem if we don't take care of loss prevention." $1 spent on mitigation (dikes, land use, building codes and materials) saves $10 of rebuilding; in the Jan 2025 California wildfires some houses survived while everything around them burned. Owners must take "ownership of risk", and where a problem is too big, governments share it (New Zealand relocating communities, UK flood public-private partnerships).
- Data is the edge: 162 years of loss data, 50 researchers working on natural catastrophes and \~200 models updated with each new event, plus a "digital twin" of each client's assets stress-tested against hurricanes and temperature scenarios. He wants to move "from morality to materiality".
- Pricing discipline: Tangen notes you can build "the biggest reinsurance company in 10 minutes by just taking on all risks at the wrong price". Berger tells underwriters "You're forced to grow the bottom line", not volume; capital is allocated by line with accumulation controls and budgets by territory, and incentives reward understanding the risk over "growth for just for the sake of growth".
- Cycles: "there's not only one cycle"; each line of business has its own. Swiss Re manages a "target liability portfolio" on a five-year forward view (rates, inflation), limits capacity where rates fall and grows in uncorrelated lines. The hard part is psychological: switching from defence to offence when soft markets "pivot into hard markets".
- Fixing Corporate Solutions: it was over-concentrated, with more than 50% of its business in the US and 90% of its liability business there. The reset: a defined customer segment, less long-tail business, countercyclical credit and surety, and international programmes where it faces 8–10 competitors instead of hundreds.
- Market structure: Munich Re, Hannover Re and Swiss Re hold \~31%, the top five 46% and the top ten \~70%. Primary insurers need tier-1 "lead" reinsurers for highly rated balance sheets, long-term commitment and whole-account views; tier 2 is largely Bermudian and was growing; tier 3 is often reinsurance arms of primary insurers.
- Emerging risks: nobody can yet define the worst case for cyber, so insurers cap it with smaller limits. Cyber first crept into property and non-damage business-interruption covers before the industry excluded it and sold separate cover; AI and algorithm malfunctions may follow the same path. Disputes centre on what a contract intended, so "not only the price is important but actually as you said the terms and conditions".
- Life reinsurance: obesity and new drugs are built into its underwriting manual (LifeGuide, now augmented with AI). A Q3 2023 spike in US claims among middle-aged people, not seen in other OECD countries, is still not fully explained, and contracts run "30, 40, and sometimes 50 years", so wrong assumptions last decades.
- Assets: "north of 100 billion" under management in a "pretty conservative" allocation set by the liabilities and their payout patterns. Swiss Re helped found insurance-linked securities and issues cat bonds, which have returned "between 11 14%", diversify well, but are "an instrument for professionals", not retail.
- AI: the industry's biggest problem is "IT legacy and IT debt", which leaves AI use cases stand-alone with invisible benefits. Swiss Re built a clean, globally integrated "AI-ready" data platform and keeps "the human in the loop".
- Leadership: "close the gap to number one" in every activity; decide at 80% of the analysis and course-correct; "strategic patience" learned from a childhood of coups and revolutions. "More than 80% of the people don't like change".
