### 381. Bill Winters — Standard Chartered: fixing a "connector" bank, tariffs and a splitting financial system (NBIM, Jan 2026)

*Group CEO of Standard Chartered for ten years; earlier at JPMorgan from 1983, then founder of an asset manager he likens to a private credit fund. Interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6edo18ZQUDw)

**Takeaway:** Winters bet in 2015 that Standard Chartered's cross-border "connector" franchise was sound beneath its compliance and credit problems. It was, but the clean-up cost a quarter of book equity and the market only believed the recovery in 2022–23. He expects tariffs to settle at 10% (25% for China), a slowly splitting financial system, and settlement of nearly everything on blockchains.

- What the bank is: "we're a connector bank", more than an emerging-markets or trade bank (it is Asia's second-largest trade bank). Hong Kong and China are "by far our biggest profit source", Singapore the operations hub. Two-thirds of the business is corporate and institutional clients, almost all multinational ("If they're not multinational, they don't really need us"); one-third is retail, all in Asia, the Middle East and Africa. NBIM owns more than 2%, worth \~$1bn (Tangen).
- The 2015 clean-up: the compliance problems (mainly with the US) were known, but "I don't think anybody was aware how substantial the problems were" on credit, despite his due diligence. "We wrote off a quarter of the book equity of the bank", refilled it with a rights issue, and the shares ended \~75% below their peak. In 2012 the bank had been "the darling of the city" at a multiple of book; it fell to around book and then to 30% of book.
- Regulators came first, "even at the expense of shareholders", because "regulators give you a license to operate". After the 2012 US financial-crime violations, removal of the US licence was considered, which for one of the largest clearers of US dollars would have been existential.
- Team: integrity first, after "some bad examples of selfserving behavior". One executive was kept; half the core team were insiders promoted a level or two and half came from outside, starting with a new chief risk officer.
- His biggest mistake: not seeing that risk appetite "had already collapsed before I arrived". He hit the brakes anyway: "the balance sheet shrunk by the better part of a third in my first year"; "Probably half of that was unnecessary", and the risk-averse mindset then took much longer to undo.
- The re-rating lag: 2015–16 was "severe medicine" and 2017–18 rebuilding, including closing a technology deficit. 2019 fell a little short of target because rates fell, and 2020's zero rates were "very bad for any bank". He felt the bank was fixed in 2019; the market came round only in 2022 or 2023. A large asset manager told him in 2018 he would have to outperform "for 2 or 3 years before anybody really pays attention and then people will crowd in"; unlike HSBC, it is not a stock investors must own.
- JPMorgan lessons: Lew Preston decided "we will follow our clients" into capital markets, so ask "Are you there to serve your clients or are you there to push products?". Winters rode the swaps business from 1987 for 25 years. JPMorgan later sold "at a huge premium to a company whose stock was hugely overvalued" (Chase); Jamie Dimon then fixed the combined bank: "a highly disciplined, very focused manager".
- Tariffs: he was in Vietnam on Liberation Day (Apr 2, 2025), when it was hit with 46%, and advised its prime minister to concede on trans-shipment of Chinese goods. His unchanged view: tariffs "would end up at 10% for the world, 25% for China", since neither side wants self-harm, but the US-China rivalry will last his "entire lifetime". Even so, "we've never been at a time where there was less major conflict than right now".
- Two financial systems: "I fear that we are" heading there. China is building an alternative to dollar infrastructure, not to displace the dollar but to keep trading if its dollar access is cut, as Russia's was. India, Brazil, South Africa and the Middle East won't choose sides, and as bridges they keep the two systems working together.
- China (Jan 2026): long-term optimistic on its technology (catching up fast even in chip design). The property bust has not caused a financial crisis "so far", but it has "completely sapped consumer confidence and investor confidence". Growth of \~4.8% is below potential, consumer stimulus could be three or four years away, and exports are being shut out of the US and increasingly Europe.
- India is "a powerhouse", with 8% GDP growth in the latest quarter after Modi's reforms, though it still needs infrastructure and easier rules for foreign investors. The Middle East is turning from an "oil and creditor economy" into a "hugely capital consumptive" diversified one; most wealth moving to Dubai is Indian or South Asian.
- Digital assets: "eventually all things will settle on blockchains" (securities, money, then property, art and wine), because settlement is cheaper, easier, more transparent, traceable and real time. The obstacles are no longer technical but regulatory: stablecoins move deposits out of banks into government bonds, and regulators including Andrew Bailey say a system that "drains deposits out of the banking system basically to fund government deficits is an unstable system". Standard Chartered has invested in this for seven years.
- AI has already sharply improved fraud and anti-money-laundering detection, yet banks "still only capture 3% of the financial crime" they think is committed. Code generation is a productivity gain; the big changes (agents in product development and customer service) are at an early stage.
- Culture: collaborative but slow across 55 markets (plus 120 served from outside). Speed targets: onboarding an asset manager from 60 days to six, then six hours. He is "tolerant of errors of judgment" but "completely intolerant of errors of principle". Its venture lab screened 2,000–2,300 ideas, materially commercialised 40 and fully commercialised five, with a good return on invested capital.
