### 382. Brian Moynihan — Bank of America: read the consumer by what they do, capital rules that overshot, AI at scale (NBIM, Aug 2025)

*Chairman and CEO of Bank of America since 2010; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast (NBIM owns \~1.5% of the bank).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_mL9yLKKtmY)

**Takeaway:** Moynihan reads the US economy from what Bank of America's 70 million households and business clients actually do, not from surveys: in mid-2025 consumers were spending more and held record cash, while small businesses on floating-rate credit lines were held back by rates and policy uncertainty. He argues bank capital rules overshot and pushed lending to less-regulated non-banks, and that technology let the bank cut headcount 30% while the business grew.

- The big driver (Aug 2025): US policy is changing on four fronts at once: trade and tariffs, immigration, tax and budget, and deregulation. Bank of America's research team (a "half billion dollars a year" investment) had the world growing a little under 3% and the US at 1% (Q4 2025 vs Q4 2024), with "no recession" and the Fed not cutting.
- Watch behaviour, not sentiment: "look at what they're doing, not what they say they're feeling". Its 70 million consumer households will send \~$4.5 trillion out of their accounts this year, up 4.5–5% in the first part of 2025 (June: 4–4.5%), and their cash is "at record highs". Survey confidence fell after Liberation Day (Apr 2); spending did not.
- Why the consumer holds up: unemployment of \~4.1–4.2%, wage growth running ahead of inflation, US oil independence that cushions energy prices, and 2021 stimulus money that was never spent down; consumer "credit is very strong". His other indicator is initial and continuing jobless claims, "a leading indicator of what businesses are really doing versus what they're saying".
- Where rates bite: large companies borrow in the markets and lock in long-term funding, but a company with $50–200 million of revenue borrows on credit lines whose base rate went from 25 basis points to 5% and "now down to fourish". Such firms use their credit lines less than before the pandemic and delay equipment purchases; their top worry moved from rates and inflation to finding workers as immigration tightens.
- Supply chains: COVID showed they were efficient but "weren't extremely resilient". Tariff gaps (10–15% vs 30–40%) decide who wins the same store shelf, so production is spreading to favoured partners such as Vietnam and Mexico.
- Branches as relationship centres: down from 6,000 to \~3,800 (while opening in new cities), and consumer staff from 100,000 to \~60,000, as routine transactions moved to mobile and ATMs. The remaining staff have "twice the relationship capacity", handling mortgages, estates and first investments; some 350,000 people still visit a branch in a day.
- Regulation (Aug 2025): the pendulum has "swung past the point". After Dodd-Frank, 7–8% capital ratios were deemed adequate (already a 25–30% increase); now "10 11%'s adequate" although industry risk has fallen, and capital is up 25% since 2018–19. His asks: settle capital rules, implement Basel III on a fair basis, remove overlapping liquidity rules (as with the supplementary leverage ratio change), clarify stablecoins, and allow faster mergers among the US's 4,000 banks.
- Regulate by activity, not charter: "if you make loans or take deposits you're a bank", whether a stablecoin issuer or a private-capital lender. In the US "half of every asset class" (commercial and consumer loans) sits outside the banking system; spreading risk is fine, but not if non-banks can lend against the same credit at higher leverage: "why can't we do it?"
- Growth must be organic: US law bars a bank holding over 10% of deposits from buying another bank unless it fails. Bank of America's highest market shares are only in the low teens (10–14%), so it grows organically; it sold its asset manager to be a pure distributor ($4.5 trillion of wealth assets) and buys payments businesses. A $100 million push into 401(k)s beat an acquisition, given a $65–70 billion expense base.
- International loans grew from \~$20 billion 15 years ago to $130–150 billion. He likes the Middle East (in Dubai for 60 years), Australia, Japan's "new found" shareholder discipline (good for investment banking), India (60+ years) and Europe, where 1% growth still leaves "lots of market share to gain".
- AI in practice: Erica, the virtual assistant it began building 10–12 years ago on its own small language model, answers \~200 million questions a quarter from 20 million customers, each of which would have been a call, text, email or branch visit. 18,000 programmers use AI coding: of 21 project steps, five or six benefit, saving \~30% on those and \~10% overall. It spends \~$4.5 billion a year on new technology and announces tools only once they work.
- Headcount: from 285,000 in 2010 to 212,000 today, "30% less people", while the deposit book is almost three times bigger. AI targets come from a chart of pay vs AI susceptibility vs headcount (well-paid, high-volume, susceptible roles first); only 25,000–30,000 processing staff remain. He manages hiring to avoid layoffs, and notes US employment doubled from \~80 million in 1969 to 160 million despite computers.
- Toughest decisions: the post-crisis clean-up, including a quarter with a $20 billion lawsuit settlement and a cut in mortgage share from one in five loans to \~5%. Downsizing must "start from the top": running the first post-crisis integration, he eliminated his own job.
- Failure modes he guards against: complacency, bureaucracy, and either missing a trend or getting so far ahead of it that "you can bet the company" (AI). To stay "a learning machine" he avoids curated news feeds and recommends Brian Grazer's A Curious Mind.
