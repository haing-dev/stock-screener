### 383. Amanda Blanc — Aviva: sell what isn't top three, buy scale, and execution over strategy (NBIM, Oct 2025)

*CEO of Aviva, Britain's leading insurer, since July 2020; previously at AXA and Zurich. Interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tfo88esmZwk)

**Takeaway:** Blanc turned Aviva around by selling sub-scale businesses (eight for £8 billion) to focus on markets where it is top three, then buying Direct Line for £3.7 billion for scale, cost and capital synergies. She argues diversified insurers beat monolines on capital and on spreading AI investment, and that good execution of an 80% strategy beats a brilliant strategy left on the slides.

- What Aviva is: insurance "sells a promise to pay". Aviva serves 25 million customers (21 million in the UK) and has a balance sheet of over £400 billion, invested in UK infrastructure, gilts, private assets and corporate debt. Its edge is breadth: from a child's Junior ISA to an equity-release mortgage, which no other UK insurer offers.
- First decision (she joined the board in Dec 2019, six months before becoming CEO): focus the portfolio. From Henri de Castries at AXA she took the rule "you need to be top three in the market that you're in" to get the benefits of scale. Aviva sold eight businesses for £8 billion within 12–24 months, cut debt, returned cash to shareholders, and the core UK, Ireland and Canada businesses "really started to shine".
- The hardest part was investor confidence: investors asked why they should trust a company that had made "three dividend cuts between 2010 and 2020". The culture was also slow to decide. COVID helped: selling businesses over video calls was faster than flying around to meet buyers.
- Direct Line (£3.7 billion; bid Nov–Dec 2024, completed 1 Jul 2025): Aviva believed it could run the business better with its scale, technology and existing AI work. Expense synergies, capital synergies and customer benefits made it "a no-brainer"; the PRA, FCA and CMA approved it within six months.
- Diversified beats monoline: in 2020 many said monolines were "the way forward". She disagreed: lifelong customer data matters, "the diversification benefits for capital are significant", and AI investment can be spread across 20 product lines. Over five years "diversified businesses have actually done better".
- Regulation (Oct 2025): the post-Brexit Solvency UK rules have been positive for insurers. The FCA's Consumer Duty was "a lot more expensive than everybody thought it was going to be" and more bureaucratic. The regulator now wants more people advised: "only 9% of people in the UK actually take advice" on retirement.
- UK consumer (Oct 2025): Aviva customers show "really good resilience". Insurance is "the last thing that people will sort of drop", and unlike during COVID nobody is calling the contact centre to say they can't afford cover. People who can't save are struggling, but always have been. Aviva runs the UK's biggest wealth platform (over £200 billion).
- Pensions: future retirees will rely on defined-contribution pensions, and "15 million people will not be able to retire well" in the UK. Her fixes: auto-enrolment from age 18 instead of 22, contributions raised from 8% to 12%, education from school, and investing beyond the default fund in private assets for better long-term returns.
- AI: \~150 use cases. Machine learning has priced motor insurance for over 10 years, and "98% of our retail products are priced using AI". In claims, models predict why a customer is calling and run fraud checks beforehand, saving the first 5–10 minutes of each call.
- Climate: in Canada last year Aviva had "six cat events within about four weeks" (wildfires, hail, floods), so it must price and model them, manage exposures and limit the zones and capacity it underwrites. The transition will happen later than assumed in 2019–20, with "much more focus on energy security".
- Uninsurable risk: UK flooding became uninsurable where houses were built on flood plains. Flood Re, an insurer and government-backed scheme, covers such homes but not those built after \~2001 (she wasn't sure of the date), when the risk was known. Elsewhere insurers make customers keep part of the risk (e.g. not covering the first 100,000 for some businesses) and push owners to rebuild with flood-proof materials.
- Execution: "strategy is great, execution is better"; executing an 80% strategy well beats a brilliant one without follow-through. That means clear objectives for everyone down to the contact centre, regular performance reviews with the CFO ("Are you doing what you said you were going to do? If not, why?"), and opening every executive committee with a customer complaint or compliment.
- Tangen cited research that company results deteriorate after a CEO is knighted or made a dame, as the CEO turns statesman and hesitates to cut jobs. Blanc says nothing changed for her: Aviva did better last year than the year before, and the tough decisions continue with the Direct Line integration.
