### 384. Jane Fraser — Citi: simplify to the cross-border network, globalization rewired, Europe on the defensive (NBIM, Jan 2024)

*CEO of Citigroup and the first woman to lead a major US bank; Citi is NBIM's global custodian and NBIM owns almost 1% of it (\~$600 million). Interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast, recorded the week after the Nov 2023 APEC summit.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gZFiEPDBaMI)

**Takeaway:** Fraser is shrinking Citi to what it does best, moving money across borders for large institutions, and flattening it from up to 13 management layers to a median of eight. She argues globalization is changing rather than reversing (Citi's flow volumes grew faster than GDP), central banks were late on inflation because their data lag, and Europe is losing competitiveness by playing defence.

- Big picture (late 2023): a "desynchronized" world whose common theme is "building resilience". Corporate "balance sheets are pretty strong", but companies are rethinking business models and supply chains for a world with "an additional s in ESG and that is security". The number she watches most is rates: how fast they come down, the curve, and inflation.
- Central banks were late to see inflation. Banks saw it in real time in rents, supply-chain and energy costs and tight labour, while "the data that they rely on is much more lagging". After COVID jobs came first, and "in the states we over stimulated" a well-performing economy. Powell then acted decisively, but the steep rise will hurt "for a while" as companies and individuals refinance "at materially higher cost".
- Why banks still matter: in March 2020 banks kept making markets and supporting clients for "a good six weeks" before central banks and treasuries stepped in; without that many asset managers would have faced life-threatening trouble. Private equity and private credit lack a dealer's capital base to support markets under stress, and unregulated DeFi and crypto ended in "a lose lose".
- Citi's edge: it is the leading bank for institutions with cross-border needs. It operates in 160 countries, holds banking licences in almost 100, and moves $4 trillion a day in cash management, FX and supply-chain flows for the world's 5,000 most important multinationals. The largest fintech moves about a trillion a year; Fraser calls Citi "$1,400 times bigger".
- "globalization is changing it's not de globalizing": that network's revenue "grew 30% last year", half from higher rates and half from pure volume growth, "much faster than GDP". Supply chains, financing and energy flows are being rewired (the Middle East linking to Asia; Brazil now more connected to Asia than to Latin America), with more "just in case" redundancy and less "just in time".
- Versus JPMorgan: some banks came out of the financial crisis stronger "and they fully capitalized on it". Citi underinvested in data and infrastructure for about a decade and is now investing heavily to leapfrog peers; two big "regulatory consent orders" push the same risk-control and operations work.
- The restructuring ("org simplification"): Citi's structure fitted a universal bank in 60 countries. Now five business lines report directly to her and serve one client segment with cross-border needs. Co-heads go, layers fall from 13 in places to a median of eight (starting with the layer below her), and a new chief client officer pushes for "a fast no rather than a slow one". It covers 240,000 people and is being done in about six months.
- Directness: "if you're not on board with the direction we're taking the bank then it is time to go and find another place". She sees no trade-off "between excellence and empathy", and a board member told her a CEO must listen harder every year because people tell you less and less.
- Lesson from the financial crisis, when she ran strategy and M&A: Citi "had become a bit of a financial Supermarket" in the 1990s and early 2000s and had to sell businesses and refocus on being a bank. The same discipline applies now: "not pretending we can be all things to all people".
- AI: the opportunities outweigh the threats, but she worries about cyber, fraud and social media fuelling political division. She likes a description passed on by Franklin Templeton's Jenny Johnson: AI is "a bit like an intern", good for gathering information but not for making the decisions.
- US-China (Nov 2023): engagement is improving, and she prefers the word diversify to de-risk. "there will be technology decoupling for sure", and some in financial flows, but the two economies stay interdependent. Restarting military communication matters most, because accidents at the front line can escalate.
- Europe "is losing some competitiveness" after a labour- and energy-cost shock, and struggles against China's speed of technology adoption. Europe "plays defense rather than offense": the US Inflation Reduction Act is a carrot, Europe's taxonomies and metrics a stick. "do not bet against the American entrepreneur": Wes Edens of Fortress spotted a divergence between gas and oil prices and built a successful LNG business.
- From Latin America she learned "the love of the rule of law": where it is undermined, investment flows "have to have higher risk premium".
