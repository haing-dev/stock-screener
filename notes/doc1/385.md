### 385. Jamie Dimon — JPMorgan: culture by follow-up, private credit's slipping standards, inflation risk and Europe's unfinished union (NBIM, Apr 2026)

*Chairman and CEO of JPMorgan Chase, in a live recording of NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast with Nicolai Tangen in front of a conference audience.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m5THZW1HR5s)

**Takeaway:** Dimon builds culture through relentless follow-up, shared information and honest comparison with better competitors. On risk, he says private credit is not systemic but underwriting has slipped, so the first credit recession in years will be worse than people expect; he puts the odds of higher inflation and a bond crisis above the market's and wants Europe to finish its single market (its GDP has fallen from parity to 70% of the US's).

- Culture is driven, not declared: "every meeting, everything you do, every trip you make, every person you hire, every person you fire". Much of Wall Street runs on "compensation schemes"; "They're not serve a client scheme". After the 2004 Bank One merger he held back his How We Do Business book until people had experienced it in practice.
- Follow-up: he reads customer complaints because "we might be doing the exact same thing to a million people", and keeps "a list of people who owe me something" (answers he is waiting for) plus a folder per business. A meeting that ends "We'll pick it up again next week" is "usually a bad meeting"; it should end with a named owner and an action.
- Bureaucracy: "bureaucracy complacency and arrogance will take down a company", and bureaucracy is "the petri dish of politics". Fixes: share all information before meetings, ask people who "admire a problem" "if you're king for today what are you gonna do?", and use small, dedicated, fully authorised teams (like Navy SEALs) on a common platform. A project that is "1% of each person's job it will not get done".
- Honest benchmarking: management meetings ask "what does the other guy do better?": "Stripe kicked our butt. PayPal kicked our butt." And: "we're the number one FX trade in the world, but we're number seven in Vietnam. Why is that?" Focus on inputs (training, technology, more bankers in Oslo) because "the input will drive the business for 20 years", and remove the jerks for whom "It's all about them".
- Governance: his board meets without him at every meeting, senior executives give their own views even when they disagree with him, and the lead director (David Novak) sends handwritten coaching notes: "it made me do a better job".
- Private credit (Apr 2026): at 1.7 trillion it is the same size as high yield and bank syndicated leveraged loans, against 13 trillion each for investment grade and mortgages, so "It's not systemic." But standards have slipped a little across "a wide spectrum": aggressive assumptions, higher leverage, "too much ratings arbitrage", weaker covenants and more PIK. Of more than a thousand private credit firms, "not all thousand of them are" brilliant, and because "we haven't had a credit recession so long", the next one "will be worse than people think", for some banks too.
- Stress tests: when he arrived, JPMorgan's scenario was stocks -10%, G10 currencies 3% and credit spreads +40%. He made it stocks -50%, currencies 10% and the worst credit ever (the worst for high yield was 17%), which exposed traders "putting puts in your balance sheet", booking profits "until it blows up". JPMorgan now runs "hundreds a week" against the Fed's annual test; the worst is usually the financial crisis, when nobody had modelled "home prices being down 40%".
- "I don't guess what's going to happen" to markets: he makes sure the bank survives low or high rates and oil anywhere up to $200, because "I will not be put in a position where if that happens, we're in deep trouble".
- Inflation (Apr 2026): not his base case, but stagflation stays on the list and the odds are "higher than the market thinks". US inflation has been over 2% for five years, and the Iran war, remilitarisation, infrastructure needs and deficits all push up: "I don't know how the world running deficits like this isn't inflationary". Companies assume debt rolls over at maybe 100 basis points more: "why not 500 basis points more?" A 50–75 bp surprise would hit 10-year bonds, and when "something we think should be stable is moving a lot, it's uh pretty scary".
- Debt and geopolitics: "there will be some kind of bond crisis and then we'll have to deal with it"; better to fix deficits beforehand. His top risks are cyber (AI such as Mythos will help attackers find vulnerabilities) and geopolitics (Ukraine, Iran, NATO), not the US economy, which is "like the weather". "the worst thing we can do is fragment" the Western world, which "is precisely what Russia and China wants".
- Europe: 25 years ago its GDP matched America's; "Now Europe is 70% of the US levels" because the union "never got finished": no real common market, capital markets union, common bankruptcy rules or cross-border bank mergers, plus bureaucracy and anti-business taxes. Of the Draghi report's 300 recommendations, seven or eight are done. At 60% or 50% of the US, its companies cannot compete with American firms and the "juggernauts" China is building. His offer: if Europe implements Draghi, "one big beautiful free trade bill" from the US, pushed first by the big countries (Macron, Starmer, Meloni).
- AI: JPMorgan has used it for 13 years with thousands of staff, in risk, fraud, marketing, hedging, prospecting, note-taking and AML/KYC. It belongs in every business review, together with defence (network segmentation; no payment-system access even for the CEO). AI may displace skilled jobs faster than society can adjust, so firms should redeploy people with training, income support or early retirement; one day the developed world may work three and a half days a week with GDP of $200,000 per person.
- Read numbers critically: "If you have companies aggregate stuff, they're hiding something." A new branch is expensed and earns nothing for three years, then makes $1–2 million a year, so it is an investment, while software would be capitalised. "Don't believe the number": "There are good expenses and bad expenses. There are good revenues and bad revenue", and in banking, bad business is something "you will pay for it".
- Office work: remote work doesn't suit many jobs, and "if it doesn't work for the client we're not going to do it"; almost no one quit over the return to the office.
