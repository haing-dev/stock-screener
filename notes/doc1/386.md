### 386. Sergio Ermotti — UBS: why Credit Suisse failed, crisis mergers and Europe's banking handicap (NBIM, Mar 2024)

*CEO of UBS from 2011 to 2020 and again since its Mar 2023 takeover of Credit Suisse; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast about a year after the deal.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ycBFo2DvSr8)

**Takeaway:** Ermotti says Credit Suisse failed because it could not earn its cost of capital: too much capital sat in an investment bank earning below its cost of equity, and lending was used to buy relationships. UBS's rescue was "a kind of merger you can never do in peace time", and his first priority was to repay the state's support quickly to prove UBS could carry the risk on its own capital and liquidity.

- Career: he entered banking through an apprenticeship as a plan B to football, traded convertibles at Citibank, and at 27 built Merrill Lynch's Swiss franc capital-markets business from scratch (1987), trading by day and running the back office by night, which would be "Unthinkable today" under current regulation and governance.
- Credit Suisse (Mar 2023): as chair of Swiss Re he watched from the sidelines. He had long seen that it "didn't have a sustainable business model" and was surprised it waited so long for a radical step (a sale or a break-up), even after its October capital increase "didn't really you know go through well with investors". In the end external shocks such as the US regional banking crisis forced the outcome.
- Post-2008 rules worked: recovery plans and capital buffers made a private solution possible, and a wind-down "would have not costed the taxpayer any money", though it would have caused turmoil because markets would quickly have asked who was next. He was relieved that a private solution won over a public one.
- Why he came back: called the Monday after the deal, he decided within 48 hours, partly from duty to UBS and Switzerland. His logic: if he said no and it went badly he would feel co-responsible; if it succeeded "I would feel stupid for not having do it". So "I had absolutely no choice".
- First priority, as he told the board: repay "as quickly as we can the emergency liquidity support of the government" and hand back the state guarantee on the problem portfolio, to show UBS could absorb the risk with "our own Capital our own liquidity". It took three to four months.
- Why Credit Suisse failed: "there is nothing more dangerous than a large bank or a bank not making money or losing money". A wealth-management, asset-gathering bank with a strong Swiss universal bank should cover its cost of capital; CS did not, because it put too much capital into an investment bank earning below its cost of equity and used credit as the lever to win clients. Lending should complete a wealth offering, not be the way in. CS became "way too entrepreneurial and way too risk-taking" and "lost completely the disciplines on risk reward and return on Capital".
- Crisis mergers: "it's a kind of merger you can never do in peace time", and "in banking all the mergers that creates any value are coming at moments of stress"; US banks built their positions by taking over failing banks in the financial crisis. Crises come "every so often so it's the waiting game". Many mergers fail not on industrial or financial logic but on chemistry, governance and roles; in a crisis there is a winner and a loser, which takes the emotion out.
- Integration: staff were angry or grieving after 170 years of rivalry. His pitch: the two banks fought for the national championship, and "now we are the Swiss national team". The priority in 2024 is merging the parent banks, UBS AG and Credit Suisse AG; Credit Suisse brings \~600 legal entities.
- Why US banks beat Europe's: after the crisis US regulators wanted their banks to stay the leading force and let them grow, while "Europe did everything they could have done" to stop banks becoming bigger or successful. With no capital markets union or banking union, and "parochial thinking" about national champions, Europe produced no strong alternatives; UBS competes as a David against Goliath through wealth management and asset gathering.
- Switzerland punches above its weight in banking, pharma, food and universities: Swiss banking moved from secrecy to professional services, backed by political stability, the rule of law, a strong franc, heavy investment in education and a flexible labour market.
- Culture: since 2011–12 UBS scores people separately on what they achieve and how they achieve it, and leaders must be good at both. Tangen quotes him: "we need teamwork but we cannot be friends all the time"; shared goals matter, and friendships that run too deep can bias a leader.
- Succession: he will stay until the integration is done, at minimum to end-2026 or early 2027. Finding "more than two or three credible candidates is not easy"; he wants executive-board members who could do each other's jobs, makes managers and staff name two jobs outside their comfort zone, and reckons building a leader takes \~20 years (James Gorman's figure too).
- Leadership: lead by example and use authority sparingly, so "people smells the coffee" before he has to insist; he steps in when debate goes on "forever without any meaningful outcome". His regrets are always about people: not bringing in the right person earlier, or leaving someone in a job too long.
