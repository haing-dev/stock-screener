### 387. Tan Su Shan & Nicolai Tangen — DBS: diversify everything, journeys and AI nudges, plus a reverse interview on forecasting (NBIM, Nov 2025)

*CEO of DBS, Southeast Asia's largest bank, about six months into the role (15 years at DBS, after building its wealth business); interviewed by Nicolai Tangen in Singapore for NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast, after which she interviews him.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z8UOnqhZHvs)

**Takeaway:** Tan positions DBS as a stable, rules-based haven for an Asia that is diversifying away from the US in trade, supply chains, currencies and banking relationships. DBS's edge is managing through customer journeys with live data, which lets it measure what AI earns (\~$750 million of incremental revenue in 2024). In the reverse interview Tangen says his friends' annual market forecasts were 80% wrong, so organisations should build agility, speed and resilience rather than predict.

- DBS began as the Development Bank of Singapore a few years after independence in 1965, financing ports, Singapore Airlines and multinationals' factories as Singapore moved from manufacturing to services; it is now "the largest commercial bank in Southeast Asia". Singapore's lesson: be "clean and transparent", steady, open for business and rules-based, an "island of stability" in a fractured world. Temasek's part-ownership gives stable ownership plus a network of talent and technology, which she calls a Temasek halo.
- Diversify everything (Nov 2025): tariffs punish companies that sell only to the US or buy only from China ("China plus one"). Wealthy families now want accounts in the US, Europe and Asia, hold gold, and are moving beyond US dollars. Trade outside the US, which DBS calls "totus" and puts at 89% of the total, "has room to grow": intra-Asia, Asia–Gulf, EU–Asia and within ASEAN.
- Why markets recovered after Liberation Day (which hit just after she became CEO): ample liquidity, especially in Hong Kong and Singapore after "rates crashed in Asia", flowed into financial assets, even though "the economic slowdown is real". Real commercial asset growth is "quite tepid", trade front-loaded before the tariffs is slowing, and investment dropped when "the pause button got pressed".
- Career lesson: hired to build the private bank, she asked to be demoted to report to the consumer-banking head, so DBS could build a "wealth continuum" from retail to private clients; "a title isn't that important", impact is.
- Building a wealth business: understand Asian clients' specific needs, combine personal relationships with a strong digital platform, and run open architecture, because "if you manufacture and you sell your own product it could be a conflict of interest". Asian wealth is younger and concentrated in the founder's business (IPOs, real estate, shipping, tech), so the adviser must flag "a lot of correlation risk". Succession creates demand for liquidity (an IPO or trade sale), while the next generation wants digital assets and is heavily concentrated in the Magnificent 7.
- Culture: "We are a stayer": DBS does not leave a market "when the chips are down". Any employee may WRECKON (pronounced like raccoon) a boss's idea, and each meeting has a MOJO, the meeting owner plus a "joyful observer" who can stop it going down the wrong path. The bank has 40,000 staff.
- Managing through journeys: since building its data lake (ADA) in 2016, DBS organises around customer journeys (opening an account, applying for a loan, supply-chain finance) instead of product silos, with live dashboards in every meeting room tracking turnaround times, complaints and pricing. 14–15 years ago it was "the worst bank for service"; it learned from Singapore Airlines (RED: respectful, easy to deal with, dependable) and hired retired SIA staff.
- AI payoff: A/B-tested nudges to customers feed the dashboards, so DBS measures AI by the incremental revenue it brings: 750 million last year, "hopefully over a billion this year". Business owners must own the wireframes and journeys themselves rather than leave them to the digital head.
- AI and jobs: in Taiwan, where DBS bought Citi's wealth and cards business, service calls are turned into text and scored by a model for wealth potential, and high scorers are called and converted. As AI bots take over service calls, service staff can become relationship managers for some of its 200,000 clients who never had one. The goal is "the best AI enabled bank with a heart".
- Leadership: "Tell the bad news up front" and give a quick no; think as much about "what we don't want to do" as about what to do. Reverse mentoring matters because "you want to identify the future trend, look at your teenagers"; she is learning about gaming (Twitch, Discord) to design banking for gamers and teenagers.
- Resilience: after losing her job, her home and a relationship in her mid-to-late 20s she learned to meditate, and took away "when things are very good, don't lose your head. When things are very bad, don't lose your heart".
- Reverse interview, Tangen on forecasting (Nov 2025): his group of friends records 12-month predictions each year, and last year's "were 80% wrong". Nobody predicted the tariffs, the US–Europe rift or India moving closer to China, and had they known, they would have expected stocks "down 20 30%", yet markets rose. So "you cannot predict anything": build agility, speed and resilience (cyber, balance sheet, redundancy).
- Tangen on the long term: NBIM aims for 50–100 years, but "One of the toughest things in life is to be longterm", because "the ability to sit still is very very hard". The Ministry of Finance mandate spares NBIM big asset-allocation calls. A CEO is the "chief energy officer", and ambitions are high enough only "if they don't start laughing they are not high enough"; five years ago staff laughed at the goal of being the top large investment fund.
