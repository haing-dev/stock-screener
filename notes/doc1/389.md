### 389. Bob Iger — Owning the stories: Disney's IP, staying relevant without losing core values, and managing creatives (NBIM, Nov 2024)

*CEO of The Walt Disney Company (CEO for 15 years until 2020, then back two years later); interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast. NBIM owns more than 1% of Disney.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s5rHIot8hkk)

**Takeaway:** Disney has lasted a century because it owns its stories and keeps its core values, while changing how they look and reach people (computer animation, streaming). Iger manages creativity with empathy and patience, not committees. On his return as CEO he put creative accountability back at the centre of the company.

- Owning the IP: asked whether one company should own culture-defining characters, like a music back catalogue, Iger says "owning is critical", because it lets Disney "monetize or or capitalize financially on great creativity". But Disney has not "cornered the market": technology makes almost everyone a creator, so "there's more competition than ever before".
- What makes the franchise: stories people share with each other, which "compounds the impact"; consistent values, so "you know what you're going to get when you see the name Disney on a product"; and products "enjoyed by multiple Generations all at once", as at the theme parks.
- Longevity: Tangen cites a figure that "only 45 companies out of 1 million survive 100 years". Iger has studied great brands back to the 1920s. His conclusion: to stay relevant in a fast-changing world, companies "abandon core values and that becomes the death of the company".
- The fix is to keep the values but modernise how they look and reach people. Pixar's computer animation looked modern to new generations, while "handdrawn animation looked old". Disney+ streams Snow White (1937) and a Mickey Mouse short from 1928: the "same story but distributed in modern technology".
- Technology: Walt Disney saw "opportunity instead of threat" and treated technology "as an asset not a liability". Iger sees three uses: better stories, better distribution and customer relationships, and a more efficient company. On AI he urges caution, since "there's more that we don't know than we know", and he won't accept that AI will destroy human creativity.
- Managing creatives: "empathy is the biggest key". Creativity is "not something that's reduced to math or science", so accept failures without pointing fingers. He gives second chances for "Honest mistakes" but not for a breakdown of integrity, and stays patient, since sometimes "right after a big failure comes a huge hit".
- No committees: "I don't think you can create by committee at all nor can you create by checking boxes". One creator's voice should rule. Iger calls himself an editor, not a creator.
- Creativity is not dying: technology lets more people create, even if quality is "diluted a bit". His 2024 examples are Inside Out 2 (a sequel to a film made nine or ten years earlier, and a commercial success), the series Shogun, and Deadpool & Wolverine.
- Return as CEO (2022): the board had removed his successor, so first he calmed the company. A restructuring had taken "accountability and responsibility away from the creative side", so he restored it on day one and streamlined the organisation so it was clear "who was responsible for for what". The hardest part was patience, "starting from a very low point".
- Leadership: balance heritage and innovation, with "a foot in the present and a foot in the future". Be an optimist, because "people don't want to follow pessimists", with a dose of realism. Be decisive: slow decisions cause "so much destruction when it comes to productivity".
- Staying in tune at 73: he spends time with his grandchildren and with staff 50 years younger, and watches short-form video and social platforms daily, to track "disruption in terms of people's tastes" and how people spend their time. He rises around 4am for quiet planning and a workout of about an hour.
- Advice: be curious and patient. Opportunities come to "people that are prepared for them"; he benefited from bosses retiring just as he was ready for their jobs.
