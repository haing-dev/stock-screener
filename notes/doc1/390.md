### 390. Daniel Ek — Spotify: streaming saved music, discovery is the product, and Europe's founder mindset (NBIM, Jan 2024)

*Founder and CEO of Spotify (17 years in the job), interviewed by Nicolai Tangen before a live audience in Oslo for NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AoYAEOr2R_s)

**Takeaway:** Ek says streaming, with Spotify as its biggest player, saved the music industry from piracy and took it back to an all-time high. Because more artists now compete, a smaller share of them succeed. Spotify's roadmap targets problems that listeners and creators share, above all discovery, and he expects its recommendations to beat any playlist a user could build within 5–10 years.

- Why music looked like "a terrible business" when he started: piracy. Napster and Kazaa let anyone download any song for free, which paid nothing back to the industry, was slow and could carry viruses. Sweden's prime minister said it was OK, and the Pirate Party "made it all the way to the EU".
- "streaming saved the music industry and Spotify is the biggest player in streaming". The industry is "back at all-time high", and more artists can live off music than ever before.
- Why artists still complain: anyone can now make music on a laptop, so more people try, and "on a percentage basis there are actually fewer people succeeding" (more succeed in absolute terms). An artist paid "$15" for "a million streams" may be one writer among many sharing that song, and a million streams "isn't that much" next to a hit such as Blinding Lights.
- Attention: asked how long people give a song before moving on, Ek says the average is now "about three or 4 seconds" (he would give a song Tangen recommends at least 20). Songs have gone from long intros to hooking at once, yet two- or three-hour podcasts also work, so short and long form coexist.
- Two music trends: tastes are going "hyperlocal" (gospel and funk are among Brazil's biggest genres) and global (Latin artists are now among the world's biggest). He expects Bollywood and African music to go global within 5–10 years.
- Roadmap rule: Spotify serves two constituencies, consumers and creators. Problems "true for both of them at the same time" go to the top. The listener's top problem is finding what to hear and the creator's is finding an audience, "two sides of the same coin".
- Recommendations: today a user who puts in the work can still build a better playlist than Spotify. In 5–10 years he expects Spotify to beat even a full working day of effort, becoming a "trusted friend" that hooks you on things you thought you'd hate. Most businesses, he says, move people "from point A to point B with lower and lower friction".
- Podcasts: there are two million in the world, and listeners "listen on an average of 6 and a half hours per week".
- A rights business: Spotify has "more than 200 lawyers" because music IP rights are complex. The head lawyer has to teach the team to "try to find Solutions rather than just being the stop", so the company can still take risks.
- Delegation: under the "Scandinavian Leadership Model" he is "probably the least powerful person in Spotify" and makes the fewest decisions. To get to a decision-maker, influence the people around them: at 23 he won a standing Friday meeting with a record-label CEO through his assistant, until "eventually he agreed" to a deal.
- Founder-led team: he is "a bit too optimistic at times", so he surrounds himself with people "more levelheaded than me" who see risks he misses and are realistic about deadlines.
- Startups in Europe: the ingredients (light administration, capital from VCs and angels, and talent) are all there; what is missing is "mindset" and role models. Just starting puts you "in the top 1%", and Spotify changed how it pursued its idea many times. Alumni of Spray (which "crashed and burned") and Stardoll went on to staff Spotify.
- Money: his advice is to "BET on yourself", since you know your own skills and limits better than "a random stranger". He made his first fortune in his early 20s from equity in companies, retired at 23, and then found he would do the work even unpaid.
