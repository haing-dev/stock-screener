### 391. Ed Catmull — Pixar's system for repeatable creativity: director authority, a Braintrust without power, hiring for potential (NBIM, Sep 2024)

*Co-founder of Pixar with Steve Jobs and John Lasseter, and author of the book Creativity, Inc.; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast. Tangen notes Pixar has won 27 Academy Awards.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-UeG4Z77jTc)

**Takeaway:** Pixar's run of hits came from a management system as much as from technology. The director holds creative authority, films in progress are judged by the health of the team, candid notes come from a Braintrust that cannot overrule, and people are hired for potential. Catmull's premise is that "we are wrong more than we think we are", so strong people who disagree are the asset.

- The founding goal was to make the first computer-animated film. Over time it became a management and cultural vision, learned by watching other companies' mistakes. From his teachers he took two ideas: Alan Kay's, that things would change at an exponential rate, and Ivan Sutherland's, that whatever the big vision, "you got to solve the problem now".
- Creative authority sits with the project leader, not executives, because the person at the top "often doesn't know exactly what what's going on". Jobs, as chairman and investor, never told Pixar how to make films or which technology to use. The aim: "you want them to own the problem of making the film".
- Judge work in progress by the team: early on, every project has problems, so "the measure is the health of the group not not whether or not the Project's working". A team that is laughing and struggling together will probably solve them.
- The Braintrust: you lose objectivity in your own project, so you need an outside force with "a vested interest in your succeeding". The first was Tom Schumacher at Disney; later Pixar's own leaders took the role. Its key rule: "this group couldn't override the director". It gives notes, and the film team picks its own solutions. Catmull's job in the room was to watch whether people were being honest.
- Board dissent: Jobs changed between 1991 and 1995 and became empathetic. Pixar's 1995 IPO was his first success since being forced out of Apple. In Pixar's 10 years as a public company he fired two directors because they never disagreed with him: "if they don't disagree with me they're not bringing any value to the company".
- Failure: keep the word for real failures. When a team loses confidence in its director, Pixar first adds people or makes changes, but "we will replace and have replaced the director on the film", since "I can't tell ahead of time" who will rise to a new role. On Ratatouille, Brad Bird took over from the stuck original director and realised the original pitch.
- Silos form naturally, so design against them. The Pixar building put the theatres, eating area, meeting rooms and bathrooms at the centre, so everyone "had a reason to cross" and "inadvertent encounters" happened. Animators could paint, or even cut holes in, their walls; the only limit was fire code.
- Talent strategy: Pixar published all its research while some rivals kept secrets, because "it's all a talent play". Being the place people want to work brings in the best people. George Lucas backed this, and so did Jobs, though he was known for secrecy.
- Hire for potential: on A Bug's Life (the second film), leaders hired people who could already do the job, and it had more hiring failures than any other film. A real-work intern programme followed: seven of the first eight interns joined after graduating, and later there were \~10,000 applications for \~100 places.
- Humility: he reckons only about half his decisions were good, so bring in people who know what you don't. Believing your success is your own doing is "a poison in the body".
- The record-time Toy Story 2 crunch was "too insane", and later films needed limits on overwork because "we were in this for the Long Haul".
- Compute: the number that matters is not Moore's law but the "compound annual Improvement of performance per price": 40% a year for 60 years, and still going with GPUs. World computing rose a millionfold in the last 10 years and will again in the next 10, and a single device's computing power will rise 40 to 100 times. AI will change animation in "highly unpredictable" ways, so values and how people are treated still matter.
