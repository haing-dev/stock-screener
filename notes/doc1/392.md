### 392. Ian Charles & Doc O'Connor — Arctos: minority stakes in sports franchises as a scarce, low-leverage, uncorrelated asset (Capital Allocators, Dec 2021)

*Co-founders and managing partners of Arctos Sports Partners, a private equity firm that buys minority stakes in pro sports franchises. Charles spent 15 years at secondaries firm Landmark Partners; O'Connor spent 32 years at the talent agency CAA, then nearly 3 years as CEO of Madison Square Garden. Interviewed by Ted Seides, who discloses he is an investor in the Arctos fund (recorded \~Dec 2021, going by the host's holiday-season ads; posted on YouTube Jul 2022).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cjtIYJbeFiY)

**Takeaway:** North American leagues opened up to funds (2019) because few buyers can pay \~$2bn+ for a team, minority holders need liquidity, and teams can't borrow to fund growth. Arctos sees franchises as scarce, legally protected local monopolies that also own a share of their league, with very little leverage and little correlation to other assets. The sector compounded 12–14% a year over 20 years, "about a 500 basis point premium to the S&P 500".

- The fund (Dec 2021): founded \~2 years earlier, Arctos raised a $2.1bn first fund and a SPAC (with Theo Epstein). It bought stakes in the Boston Red Sox, Golden State Warriors and Sacramento Kings. Its market: minority stakes in the big five North American leagues (MLB, NBA, NFL, MLS, NHL) plus top global sports platforms, "about $400 billion" in total.
- Three deal types: liquidity for minority and control owners; growth or operating capital; and acquisition financing, to build multi-team platforms or help a new control owner buy a first team.
- Why the leagues opened up: the average team is worth \~$2–2.2bn, and few people can pay that. Minority partners need a source of liquidity, as the secondary market gave private equity. And teams can't borrow much, so a "high ROI pipeline" could only be funded from operating cash flow or new equity, even though "95% of these businesses have positive operating income".
- What you buy, in three layers: (1) a 1/30–1/32 share of the league, and each league alone is worth "between 25 and 150 billion dollars"; (2) a "protected geographic monopoly" in one of North America's 30 best markets; (3) local platform assets such as the arena, surrounding real estate, parking and hotels. The league generates "40 to 80% of the economics for each team", a "beta factor" shared by all teams.
- Economics: media (local and national) is the largest revenue stream, then ticketing and game-day revenue. Talent is "the single largest cost center", and more rational collective bargaining agreements have cut its cost over time. Over the last decade, 60–65% of new control owners came from tech or finance.
- Record: over \~115 years, US inflation ran at \~3.1% a year and US public equities compounded at \~5.9%, while equity in the New York Yankees compounded at 9.7% a year. Over 20 years the sector returned 12–14% a year, with a correlation to traditional assets of -0.2 to +0.3. Charles: "I do know 50 years from now, there will be a World Series in October."
- Leverage: leagues ban using a team as collateral and cap franchise debt, so average loan-to-value across the big four leagues is \~13–14%. Past forced sales by institutions (newspapers; Disney with the Angels and Ducks; Fox with the Dodgers) and by highly leveraged owners left "bad prints" that suppressed prices. That is why the leagues barred institutional owners "for the better part of a decade or more".
- Why most PE can't play: some leagues require 10+ years left in a fund's term and $500–700m dedicated to that league, with no leverage, no collateral and no governance control. For a buyout fund, "the L doesn't work. And the B doesn't work." You must be "a passive partner over a long horizon with no leverage".
- Sourcing: almost $25bn of deal flow in 22 months. Arctos has a database of 1,200+ limited partners across 180 platforms, like holding the only copy of the secondaries market's investor directory in 1998–2001. Its Arctos Insights unit sends owners content and tools to keep a dialogue going, and 17 senior advisors cover sponsorship, ticketing, media rights, real estate and betting. Access to deals and information is "one of the moats".
- Diligence: league-level work is done once and updated quarterly; each club gets a line-by-line, probabilistic underwrite. Arctos also diligences the control owner, management and "the likelihood of a control transaction in the next say 5 to 20 years". Anything about players is walled off by an MNPI-style compliance system.
- Winning matters less than market, fan experience and owning the venue. Results drive revenue more in European football, baseball, hockey and MLS than in the NFL and NBA, where national revenue is high. O'Connor's Knicks were the NBA's worst performers of the 2000s, yet the Garden stayed at or near capacity and led league revenue until the Warriors' new arena.
- Growth drivers: sports betting, a sponsorship category "that didn't exist 5 years ago" that also lifts engagement and media values; the move to direct-to-consumer streaming; international expansion (the NBA leads); platform consolidation; and new venues plus surrounding real estate, a "20 to 50 billion-dollar opportunity over the next 5 to 10 years".
- Risks: league media contracts, though MLB, the NHL, the NFL and some European soccer leagues repriced theirs in the last 18 months, de-risking 7–12 years (MLS and the NBA were next); venue safety (COVID meant games without fans); and player collective bargaining agreements.
- Charles' pet peeve: "something like 90% of the managers in alternatives don't create any excess return. Net of fee and carry", yet many GPs don't know alpha from beta.
