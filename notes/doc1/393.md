### 393. Kenichiro Yoshida — Sony's turn from hardware to creativity: exits, image sensors and owned IP (NBIM, Nov 2023)

*Chairman and CEO of Sony Group (CEO since 2018, earlier its CFO); interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast. NBIM has been a Sony shareholder for 25 years.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VNe1lOhE6Pc)

**Takeaway:** Yoshida turned Sony from a maker of devices that played content into a company whose purpose is content and creativity. It exited PCs, kept only premium TVs and phones, sold its battery and display-panel businesses, and invested in image sensors and in IP across games, music and film. A failed general streaming service taught Sony to go direct to consumers only in niches, and otherwise to supply other platforms as an independent studio.

- Origins: over 77 years Sony grew from sound (its name comes from the Latin "sonus") into electronics, entertainment and semiconductors, through CBS/Sony Records (1968), Columbia Pictures (1989) and PlayStation (mid-1990s).
- The shift: entertainment content "used to be our method" to sell packaged products such as CD and Blu-ray players; now "entertainment is now our purpose". Sony exited PCs, went premium-only in TVs and smartphones, and sold batteries and display panels to focus on CMOS image sensors, used mainly in smartphone cameras. It invested in games, music and pictures, led by the purchase of EMI Music Publishing, plus direct-to-consumer services in specific areas.
- Culture drives "execution which is more important than strategy", and Sony values not fearing failure. Its 2015 general-entertainment streaming service (PlayStation Vue: live TV, movies, sports) won few subscribers. Sony learned that "broad entertainment requires a high level of capital" and closed it within 5 years. The lesson: serve communities in specific areas, such as the anime service Crunchyroll.
- Film: Sony owns no general-entertainment streaming service, so its studio aims to be "a strategic supplier", picking the best partner to distribute each title. It sells direct only in niches such as anime, and shares that data with creators.
- Gaming: games will be playable wherever there is computing. PlayStation stays the core while Sony expands to PC, mobile and cloud. All-you-can-eat subscriptions are worth less than in video because "people usually play one game at the time", so Sony runs a hybrid of subscription and paid content. On Microsoft–Activision: healthy competition "is necessary for the game industry to grow".
- AI: the convergence of computing and entertainment is "a Mega Trend", but entertainment is "a people business". Content is "Creator generated and copyrighted", so Sony treats AI as a tool for creators, not a replacement; for example, LLMs will streamline game programming.
- Long-term thinking: Yoshida calls planet Earth the most important stakeholder. He balances steady profits against long-term creative bets through communication with staff and shareholders. His formative job was president of So-net: after taking it public he renamed it and removed the Sony brand to make it independent.
- Advice: "always challenge yourself". Many of his ventures in online games, network services and animation failed, but they became assets for him.
