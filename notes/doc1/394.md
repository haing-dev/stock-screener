### 394. Robert Kyncl — Warner Music: breaking through the clutter, catalogue value and streaming's pricing headroom (NBIM, Apr 2025)

*CEO of Warner Music Group, after senior roles at HBO, Netflix and YouTube; interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0zNfoQtxWXc)

**Takeaway:** Digital distribution lets anyone publish, so a label's value has moved from distribution to getting artists heard: "in a world where anyone can publish, no one can be heard". Kyncl sees catalogues as durable IP that new platforms keep reviving, and music subscriptions as under-penetrated next to video. After 15 years of one-size-fits-all pricing that lagged inflation, he expects price rises in mature markets.

- Career lessons: a 1990s movie co-financing firm taught him "how to value content how it's paid for and how it's distributed". Netflix had \~500,000 subscribers when he joined and began focusing on streaming in 2005 to "disrupt our own disruption". YouTube had \~$450 million of revenue in 2010 and was being "sued by lots of different people"; Eric Schmidt told him to stop them "sending us paper" (lawsuits) and send them money instead.
- Why music: everyone has a relationship with it ("art decorates walls but music decorates time"), and through the industry's ups and downs it is "resilient and gets through all the downs".
- The label's new job: with digital distribution "anyone can publish", so labels' distribution role has shrunk, but "in a world where anyone can publish, no one can be heard". Value "decreased on the distribution side and increased on breaking through the clutter", which takes data, creative work and global infrastructure. That is why "all the big stars are signed with large music companies": "it takes a village" for repeatable success.
- A&R as a system: one young A&R executive looks for an artist's "connection with the audience before I even listen to their voice", watching crowds at live shows for tears, shrieks or laughter; for female artists, teen girls are the earliest adopters. Audiences are then grown gradually through small tours guided by data, not half-empty big venues; Teddy Swims, not a star five years ago, built his core audience with small shows in Europe. Kyncl says his own hit predictions are poor because he has no system.
- Streaming is efficient distribution, with no inventory, returns or breakage, and it has grown the business. Yet music subscriptions still lag video subscriptions, and the gap is bigger outside the US and in developing markets, so there is "a lot more room to grow".
- Pricing: music is "probably the best deal that consumers get in anything". Streaming prices have stayed flat since launch: "Nobody has moved price up despite you know whatever inflation there may may have been." That was right while hunting for subscribers. Now the plan is to keep "hunting" where penetration is low and start "harvesting on price" where it is high, with "two sizes" instead of one strategy.
- Catalogue value: old songs come back through licensing to platforms, where users attach them to videos (Fleetwood Mac took off on TikTok), and through remakes, such as David Guetta's "Forever Young" with Ava Max and Alphaville. Linkin Park, whose first album with Warner came out in 2000, saw new-album pre-sales "the highest in Warner's history", and "their catalog just shot through the roof": "it just underscores the value of IP despite whatever happens on a macro level".
- Buying catalogues: "it depends what the price is". His wish list is Michael Jackson and Queen, but "competition has them".
- Copyright tools: at YouTube he helped build Content ID, which fingerprints content so rights holders can block, track or monetise uploads, with a negotiated revenue split. AI is "basically just UGC on steroids", so the same framework can apply.
- AI: background or mood music with no artist attached "may be under attack", since AI can make it at scale. Artist-centric music touches identity and livelihood, so its use must be "very permission based". WMG works with platforms, AI model companies and governments (e.g. on the NO FAKES Act), but does not rely on slow legislation.
- Running WMG: he calls it a "15 16 17 18 billion startup" depending on the market cap. Roles close to artists stay entrepreneurial; the centre is structured. Media companies run many separate P&Ls and infrastructures and could be much simpler. Each direct report sends a one-page note every Friday: what got done, what comes next week, and what they need from him.
- Advice: "nobody will remember the reasons why you failed", only results; stay "two steps ahead of your boss"; and saying "I'm doing my best" after a bad result means "there's no more upside from you".
