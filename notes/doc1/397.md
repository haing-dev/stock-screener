### 397. Zak Brown — The McLaren turnaround: culture and leaders first, then sponsors, then the car (NBIM, Nov 2025)

*CEO of McLaren Racing since the end of 2016, interviewed by Nicolai Tangen on NBIM's "In Good Company" podcast while McLaren led the 2025 Formula 1 season.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J1hSsHa99Sk)

**Takeaway:** Brown took over a team at a record low in results and sponsorship, with a toxic culture but a great brand. He replaced almost the whole leadership team, rebuilt sponsorship to pay for drivers and wind tunnels, and got 1,400 people rowing the same way; McLaren has now won the constructors' title two years running. Team values have soared: McLaren was valued at £560m five years ago and has been reported at \~3.5 billion, and he puts the floor for an F1 team at about two billion.

- Starting point (end of 2016): McLaren was at "a record low in sponsorship in results on track", with unhappy fans and "a pretty toxic work environment". But it had "a great brand in a great sport with great history", and people with 20–40 years at the company; most staff had been there through both the winning and the terrible years.
- Excuses vs causes: everyone blamed the Honda engine. With a Renault engine "you get a little bit better", which raised the question "now what are the excuses?" and became the wake-up call. The real problem was teamwork and culture.
- The sequence: first the leadership team, all new but one (the CFO was promoted from within): "the first thing I learned was I need to change my leadership team". Next the commercial side, his strength, where belief in the brand brought new partners whose money paid for drivers and wind tunnels. Then he changed the top three people in the F1 team.
- The value chain: great fans need great communications and marketing; the commercial team then has to "monetize that great brand". Finance must focus on ROI because "we live in a cost cap world". HR builds the team, and only then does it reach the car. The culture is transparency, hard work, incremental improvement and "park your feelings at the doors".
- How winning becomes value: prize money from F1 rises with results, "fans love winners", and more fans bring more sponsors. The goal is to stay in the top four and always be "a threat to win". McLaren once fell to ninth, and "we can't go back there".
- Team values: five years ago McLaren was valued at £560 million, and "it's been reported that we are around 3.5 billion in value" ("not a million miles off"). The new 11th team "had to pay almost $500 million just to get an entry" and will spend "a billion or two" before it turns a profit. So he sees two billion as the floor for an F1 team, with the top at four to six.
- Ownership: Bahrain's sovereign fund Mumtalakat has owned the team since about 2007. When it was spending heavily on the automotive group, it chose outside investment for the racing team over living within its means, "which was a slow death". After a great 5-year run "they bought out the minority shareholders", and Abu Dhabi's CYVN came in as partner.
- Netflix's Drive to Survive delivered what F1 lacked when Liberty bought it: North America, and a more diverse, more female and younger fan base. The US went from one race to three, F1 signed "a new huge TV deal with Apple", and "75% of our new fans are women and a younger audience age 18 to 34".
- Fans: "Without fans there's no McLaren". "Only 1% of our fans will ever go to a race", so McLaren pays for free events (40,000 people over two days in Trafalgar Square), and it brought back its papaya livery because fans asked for it.
- The car: there are "six drivers that can world win the world championship out of 20, but they have to be in two of the best cars". "2% separate the best from the worst", so the work is a hunt for milliseconds, mostly in aerodynamics. With 2026 "a clean sheet of paper", his priority is stability: Red Bull slid amid "culture turmoil" and recovered with the same car once a new team boss settled things.
- Two equal drivers (Lando Norris and Oscar Piastri, one point apart with four races left) raise the odds in both championships, push each other, give two sets of car feedback and appeal to sponsors and fans. The cost: they take points off each other, and since 1984 every team-mate title fight "has ended in tears".
- Decisions: decide fast only when you must, as at a pit stop, and never in an emotionally charged moment. After Austin he called a crash "some amateur driving" on live TV, then saw the replay and apologised. McLaren runs hot debriefs right after a race and cold ones later; "All I ask is you don't make the same one twice".
- Roles: the CEO runs the business, brand and board, and team principal Andrea Stella runs car performance: "I work for the racing team". Without technical training, Brown judges engineers with "a good sniffer": who delivers on promises, and who backtracks at the second question. His instinct is right "90% of the time", and slowing down halves the 10% he gets wrong.
