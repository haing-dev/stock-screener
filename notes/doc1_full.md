## Investment philosophy & markets

43 talks on how great investors think. Most agree on durable moats, long holding periods and avoiding big losses; macro (Bridgewater) and activism (Elliott) are the counterpoints.

### 1. Li Lu — Columbia Business School lecture (2006)

*Founder of Himalaya Capital.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-jF5au0-JiY)

**Takeaway:** Value investing suits a minority temperament (\~5% of assets). The work is investigative journalism, and the big money comes from a handful of deep lifetime insights you're willing to bet on heavily.

- Graham's three attitudes are owner mindset, a big margin of safety, and treating Mr. Market as separate from you. All three follow from one fact: you own a small piece you can't control.
- Be comfortable alone. "You're right because your reasons and evidence are right, not because others agree."
- The job is mostly research. Read Value Line page by page, starting with the new-lows list, until you can "smell" an opportunity in minutes.
- Checklist: Is it cheap? Is it a good business? Can I trust management? What did I miss? Why is this being offered to me?
- Timberland (1998): \~5x earnings, no analyst coverage, a 98% family vote. He read every court filing and checked the family's character. The stock rose \~7x in 2 years; he sold when 50–60 analysts filled the room.
- Korean holding company: market cap $60M against \~$70M cash plus \~$100M of listed stock. Think in whole-company numbers, not per-share.
- When you've done the work and see no downside, bet big rather than in 25–50bp positions.
- His biggest mistake was an omission: monthly-return pressure kept him from buying a company that later rose 50–100x.
- Expect 5–10 deep insights over a 50-year career; cheap Graham stocks are the "bread and butter" at 10–15% a year.
- Rarely sell rare compounders: deferred tax is an interest-free loan from the government.
- Moats like Bloomberg's come from learned workflows and network effects, and once an inflection is crossed the winner takes all.
- Shorting forces a trading mindset. Holding cash is fine, but never let a year pass without learning something.

### 2. Pat Dorsey — The nuances of investing in moats (Capital Allocators)

*Founder of Dorsey Asset Management; built Morningstar's moat framework.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P25Ahug2-eQ)

**Takeaway:** Moats are mostly qualitative. The best investments pair a durable moat with humble, aligned management and a long reinvestment runway, and you can never set the quality bar too high.

- Moats come from intangibles, switching costs, network effects or cost and scale. They were found by studying firms with >15% return on invested capital (ROIC) for >15 years.
- ROIC misleads more today because value now sits in expensed assets such as code and brand. Margins and free cash flow mislead too.
- "Scale economies shared" (Costco) is a moat without pricing power. Abusing pricing power invites competitors: if you take 5 in price, add at least 3 in value.
- Luxury brands are durable signals, while search-cost brands (Coke) are not. Network effects are over-glorified; prefer a narrower moat with a runway over a wide moat with none.
- He shifted from \~70/30 moat/management to \~30/70. "Any idiot can run it" has done harm.
- His management screen is humility. Ask: "What's a do-over?" and "Which direct report would you least want to lose?" Red flags: "I" instead of "we", the CEO owning the leased HQ, moving HQ somewhere sunny.
- Capital allocation is rare. Buybacks are neutral-to-negative on the evidence and M&A is mostly poor. Ask for hurdle rates and post-deal reviews.
- Portfolio: 10–15 stocks, 15% max position, 15% hurdle rate, from a universe of \~300–400. The US has the best regulator and better allocators.
- DCFs undervalue companies that "beat the fade", so use multiples first and the DCF as a cross-check.
- Most sells come from being wrong. Take the 12–20% loss early. "Weird isn't wonderful"; investing is a craft.

### 3. Dev Kantesaria — Exceptional businesses for the long run (The Investor's Podcast, Dec 2024)

*Founder of Valley Forge Capital (\~$2.7B).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nt5jnCCW2iM)

**Takeaway:** Own 8–12 monopoly or duopoly "toll collectors" that combine growth and predictability. Hold them for a decade or more and treat market timing and precise valuation as noise.

- Quality means growth *and* predictability. Young software is unpredictable and Nestlé is slow; both underperform.
- Test: would you be comfortable owning it if the market closed for 10 years? He avoids biotech despite 18 years in biotech VC.
- Entry points: about a third come from drops on misunderstood issues and two-thirds from "market neglect" (FICO six years ago).
- Overpaying by 10–15% hardly matters for a great compounder. Pricing power on an essential, low-cost product is the hallmark of a great business.
- Duopolies need rational competitors. Boeing/Airbus, UPS/FedEx and potash fail the test.
- He is wary of big tech on AI: commoditization, exploding capex, and winners that are hard to pick. Not owning big tech 10–15 years ago was his largest omission.
- "99.9% of active management is random noise minus fees"; most people should index.
- Omissions cost far more than bad buys. He bought S&P Global at $17.50 in 2009 on his honeymoon; it's now >$500.
- He avoids serial acquirers and diworsification and prefers buybacks. Low turnover isn't complacency: he keeps re-ranking holdings against the watchlist.

### 4. Sir Chris Hohn — In Good Company (NBIM, May 2025)

*Founder of TCI and the Children's Investment Fund Foundation (CIFF).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M01NZc2QlDk)

**Takeaway:** Barriers to entry trump growth. Buy the \~200 businesses with durable moats, hold \~8 years, and let intrinsic-value compounding beat whatever multiple you paid.

- Moat types:
  - irreplaceable assets ("they'll never build a second Madrid airport");
  - complex IP (no new jet-engine entrant in 50+ years);
  - installed base;
  - network effects;
  - switching costs.
- Growth without barriers is worthless: airlines grew \~5% a year for a century with minimal profit.
- Pricing 1% above inflation at a 20% margin grows profits \~5% faster than revenue. Price-led growth beats volume growth.
- Regulation: barriers too low invite competition; too high invite regulators. The ideal is weak, rational competition.
- Avoid list: banks, autos, retail, insurance, commodities, airlines, telecoms, media, traditional asset managers.
- Average holding period is \~8 years. He bought Moody's at 10x earnings in the financial crisis and now holds it at \~$400–500.
- Edge = fundamental, long-term, concentrated (10–15 stocks) plus intuition. "Very few things matter."
- Hardcore activism rarely pays now, and shorting is brutal: Wirecard rose 20–30x before it collapsed.
- CIFF gives >$500M a year to climate and child health.

### 5. Sir Chris Hohn — Investing & philanthropy (Money Maze, Nov 2025)

*TCI and CIFF.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wPNs8DZ0FvE)

**Takeaway:** Start with risk, not return. Competition and disruption are "the whole thing," so own businesses with several barriers to entry and an essential need, and hold them like a private-equity owner.

- Most of a company's NPV lies 20–30 years out, so ask whether it will still exist then.
- "Good companies stay good and bad companies stay bad," but only if you judged quality right.
- Moats are being worn down faster by AI.
- The thesis must be obvious. Avoid fast-changing tech and discretionary goods ("never understood what makes one handbag better").
- He prefers engagement to activism. GE invited TCI's analyst to address its top 300 managers.
- A great manager can't fix a bad business, and arrogance is a killer.
- "Do you need to change your wife every year?" Investors chase what's new; Peloton went to $50B and then nearly zero.
- Philanthropy: pick high-leverage, underfunded causes (contraception at \~$10 per averted unwanted pregnancy) and look past KPIs to root causes.

### 65. Pat Dorsey — Competitive advantage masterclass: Moats 2.0 (The value investing channel, Apr 2022)

### 66. Terry Smith — Characteristics of a quality company (The value investing channel, Apr 2022)

*Founder and CIO of Fundsmith; came to fund management from running businesses.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B_-YBTI_EYw)

**Takeaway:** A quality company needs a high return on capital in cash, secular growth to reinvest at those returns, and a moat against mean reversion. Such returns persist for decades, so how good a business is matters far more than whether it looks cheap.

- Return on capital in cash (cash delivered in a period divided by capital employed) is "the single most important thing". His portfolio was \~25% at the time of the talk (\~2022).
- Munger's point: a business earning 6% on capital for 40 years returns \~6% even if bought at a huge discount. One earning 18% for 20–30 years wins even at an expensive-looking price.
- High returns are wasted if the company is landlocked in a small niche or geography. Growth lets it reinvest cash at those same \~25% returns.
- Test for secular, not cyclical, growth: each cycle peak and trough should exceed the previous peak and trough. Growth needn't come every year.
- Returns persist: on ROE since 1966, the high group sits \~20% and the low group \~12%, still apart half a century later. "Good companies don't become bad companies by and large or vice versa."
- Persistently high-ROIC sectors (1963–2004 and the latest 20 years): pharma, household and personal goods, software, media, commercial services, semiconductors, health care, food, consumer services.
- Persistently low: utilities, telecoms, transport (\~7–8%), energy, materials, retailing. Airlines never earn an adequate return on capital.
- The MSCI World quality subset (high returns on capital, high margins, good cash conversion) beat MSCI World in every rolling 10-year period since 1996.
- Moat ranking: brands and installed base outrank patents, which expire. Otis's \~1858 patent lapsed by the 1870s, yet Otis is still number one.
- Installed base is where the money is: elevator buyers sign the maker's maintenance contract 75% of the time. Embedded software (payroll, reservations) works the same way.
- Control of distribution is a moat. Diageo, Pernod Ricard and Bacardi keep new vodkas out of bars; Nestlé and Danone own emerging-market dairy cold chains.
- White space drives growth: \~2/3 of people needing vision correction lack it, >80% of world transactions are cash, and \~60% don't use toothpaste.
- Pets are his top long runway: US diet pet food is $10bn a year (a \~5-year-old figure), and a vet-diagnostics holding benefits. Aging, premiumization and emerging-market consumers also drive growth.
- Cheapness can't make a bad company good: "It may give you a short-term opportunity, but it certainly doesn't give you a long-term one."

### 67. Peter Thiel — Competition is for losers (The value investing channel, Feb 2022)

*Co-founder of PayPal and Palantir, founder of Founders Fund; a \~2014 lecture for Stanford's How to Start a Startup course, introduced by Sam Altman.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nSc0GoVRqtE)

**Takeaway:** A company's value is the value it creates (X) times the share it captures (Y%). The two are independent, so only monopolies keep lasting value, and durability, not growth, dominates that value.

- Airlines vs Google (2012): US airlines had $195bn of domestic revenue vs Google's \~$50bn. Yet cumulative airline profits over \~100 years are \~zero, and their market cap is about a quarter of Google's.
- There are only perfectly competitive businesses and monopolies, with "shockingly little" in between. "capitalism and competition are antonyms".
- Monopoly's financial signature: very high profit margins and cash piling up year after year (Apple, Google, Microsoft, Amazon). Screen on profitability and net cash, not revenue size.
- Market definitions lie: Google (\~66% of search) calls itself \~3.5% of \~$500bn advertising, while non-monopolists shrink their market to look unique. Measure share in the actual market.
- Four monopoly traits: proprietary technology \~10x better than the next best thing, network effects, economies of scale (high fixed, low marginal costs), and brand. Google had all four; now "three and a half".
- DCF math: PayPal (Mar 2001), growing 100% at a \~30% discount rate, got \~3/4 of its value from 2011 onward. Tech firms get 75–85% from 2024 onward (\~2014 view).
- So Silicon Valley overvalues growth rates, which are easy to measure, and undervalues durability, which is qualitative. Ask why it will still lead in 10–20 years.
- Be the last mover, not the first: Microsoft in operating systems, Google in search. "the better framing is you want to be the last mover you want to be the last company in a category"
- Start small and expand in concentric circles: Amazon in books, eBay in Pez dispensers, PayPal with 25–30% of \~20,000 eBay PowerSellers in 2–3 months.
- Red flags: big-market pitches (2005–2008 cleantech, "energy"), crowded categories (the 10th thin-film solar firm) and large existing markets such as \~$1trn restaurants.
- Fast innovation by others kills incumbents: 1980s disk-drive makers were 10x better, then replaced within two years. Consumers won, founders didn't.
- Historically Y ≈ 0 for science, railroads and textile mills (5–7% yearly efficiency gains, 1780–1850, competed away). Money came from vertically integrated monopolies (Ford, Tesla, SpaceX) and software.
- Software suits monopoly because its marginal cost is zero and adoption is fast. In a small market with slow adoption, rivals have time to enter.
- Crowds signal insanity, not wisdom: \~20,000 people move to LA yearly to be movie stars and \~20 succeed. He is skeptical of lean startup; great firms made a quantum improvement.

### 91. Prem Watsa — Fairfax's Graham playbook: guest lecture at Ivey (Ivey Business School, 2011)

*Chairman and CEO of Fairfax Financial, taken over in 1985 at a $2 million market value; book value per share compounded 25% a year to \~$400 and float grew to \~$23 billion (2011).* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N9CCyX-40OI)

**Takeaway:** Buy a dollar for 50 cents, go against the crowd, and stay financially sound enough to survive a 1-in-50 or 1-in-100-year event. Management's long track record is "the single biggest determinant" in avoiding value traps.

- Margin of safety is the key principle: work out what a company is worth and buy it for 50 cents on the dollar. Price decides the outcome: "what you pay makes a huge difference in relationship to what you get".
- Banks: "Banks Book value is not a bad first measure especially if you trust management". Fairfax bought Wells Fargo below book in the 2008–09 crash, citing its long management record.
- J&J was \~$60 in both 2002–03 and 2011 while EPS doubled ($2.25 to $4.50–$5), so the P/E fell from 25–30x to \~12x. It had hit 12x only \~five times in 60 years, so expect 10–15% earnings growth plus a "double whammy" if the P/E reverts.
- Seagram: Fairfax bought below net cash (\~$70 of cash per share vs a $65 stock), getting plants and inventories "for free". The stock doubled and tripled; management did waste the cash, but \~25 years later.
- Nortel was 40% of the Toronto index (TSX) at huge P/Es in 1999–2000, so Fairfax skipped it. Its bonds later traded at 40 cents with \~$4bn of cash covering \~$4bn of bonds, and went to 100.
- Value traps: "the single biggest determinant for us is management". Check a long record and prior employers, avoid the two-year CEO promising great growth, and give value 3–5 years to be recognized.
- Courage to average down: write down the five or six reasons when you buy at $10. If it falls to $5 and they still hold, buy more, even with 20 analysts saying it's worth $5–6.
- Survive the tail: as insurers plan for a Category 5 hurricane, ask "what happens if things go down 50% can we survive it". Debt is what kills: nine of Japan's top ten life insurers failed after guaranteeing 3–4% returns.
- Crisis playbook: worried about US housing in 2003–05, Fairfax held government bonds and cash and avoided AAA structures entirely. In late 2008–early 2009 "you were getting paid to take risk", so it bought corporate and junk bonds at 10–12% spreads.
- Consensus is right in engineering but already priced in markets: if ten oil analysts are positive, you'll be disappointed. So each of Fairfax's \~6 investors runs his own pool, and positions can build to \~$400m as others add.
- Crum & Forster, bought in 1998 for $680m as a turnaround, took 5–6 years to fix instead of 2–3. By end-2010 it had paid $1.5bn of dividends and still held $1.2bn of capital.
- Alignment: Watsa's salary has been fixed at C$600,000 since 2000 with no bonus or shares, and Fairfax discloses the negatives. A tiny holding company over five to seven units avoids bureaucracy and "unleashes talent".
- Macro (early 2011): after markets rose 80–100% from the lows, Fairfax hedged its portfolios through the S&P 500. US total debt relative to GNP exceeded 1929's, with Japan after 1989 (Nikkei down 75% in 20 years) the warning.
- Bubbles (early 2011): copper, gold and cotton were on a "parabolic curve" like silver in 1980. Facebook's $50bn private valuation showed emotion and greed, and housing euphoria "does come down it's only a question of time".

### 92. David Einhorn — Critical thinking, poker and the bubble basket: Oxford Union Q&A (OxfordUnion, Dec 2017)

*Founder of Greenlight Capital, which he and a colleague started \~20 years earlier with $10,000 each in 130 square feet; avid poker player.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qvz5LS9pIjs)

**Takeaway:** Greenlight's edge is critical thinking that, a few times a year, finds an important difference of opinion with the other side of the trade. Those become a few large, unlevered positions, valued imprecisely and sized as if the whole stake can be lost.

- Three layers, as in poker: what you know (price, sales), what you can surmise (CEO motivation, strategy, competition) and unknowable events within a range. Combine them and ask if this is "a good place to commit a fraction of my capital".
- The facts matter least "because everybody knows them"; the edge is inference and judging the range of outcomes. Once you have "figured out the joke", it's just a matter of watching it happen.
- Imprecise valuation: don't ask whether a $10 stock is worth $11 or $12, only whether it's worth "a lot more than ten dollars". Reassess at $15, $18 and $20 as facts change.
- Shorts need "over valuation combined with some kind of deterioration"; valuation alone is not a short signal.
- Bubble basket (from \~2014–15): value unprofitable companies blind, from financial statements only, and short small if fair value is \~90% below price. Even if off by 100%, a stock 90% overvalued is still 80% overvalued.
- The basket held \~40–50 names: many failed or were de-rated, offsetting 2–4 big losers until early 2017. Then nearly everything rose in 2017; he drops any name that starts "showing real profits".
- Losers: the presumption is "we might have missed something here", so reassess constantly, then reduce, exit or hold. Adding to a loser happens "very rarely".
- Risk: no fund leverage, the best idea is capped, and market risk is taken knowingly. A $10 stock has "ten dollars of downside", not $1, because any stock can go to zero.
- Value at risk (VaR) holds capital for 95–98% of outcomes and none for the tail, which invites unlimited tail bets. The 2008 tail proved "much more likely and much more correlated".
- Time arbitrage is "one of the best inefficiencies in the market": investors skip stocks that won't rise within six months. "Dead money" stocks nobody wants are "sometimes... just the best investments".
- Value vs growth (late 2017): the market paid for social desirability and CEO charisma, as in the "eyeballs" era, and de-rated profitable companies. "it's not different this time and at some point this will revert", though results "have certainly suffered".
- Machines win at short horizons, so Greenlight hunts what is "widely misunderstood... by a large margin". It stays in developed markets, since local insiders and rules are hard to beat from New York.
- Activism is "very rare" and only for changes that help in the short, intermediate and long term. Many shareholder–management fights are about agency, such as managers protecting their pensions, not horizon.
- Post-2008 fixes "solved none" of the problems (2017): too-big-to-fail and rating-agency concentration remain. Derivatives clearinghouses are new, undercapitalized too-big-to-fail institutions.

### 15. Robert Carver — Ex-Man AHL, now trades solo (Odds on Open, Jul 2025)

*Former head of fixed income at Man AHL; author of* Systematic Trading. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y39Zf3GPpxE)

## Science, education & other

### 47. Saul Perlmutter — Why doubt is a superpower (NBIM, Dec 2025)

*Physics Nobel laureate (dark energy); co-author of* Third Millennium Thinking. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Negpb5iCqTA)

**Takeaway:** Science's real method is a culture: think probabilistically, hunt your own mistakes, seek out people who disagree, and pair doubt (the brakes) with can-do confidence (the accelerator). These tools transfer directly to investing.

- We can now solve planet-scale problems. The leftover problem is how to think together.
- Bet in probabilities (90% on this, 70% on that), not true or false. That makes debate less ego-driven.
- "Individual humility, collective arrogance": \~95% of an experimentalist's life is hunting errors, yet it takes confidence to stay on a hard problem for years.
- **Blind analysis** beats confirmation bias: hide the result until every error check is agreed. Personal version: pick which sources you trust *before* seeing what they say about your case.
- For an investment decision:
  - Collect independent views in writing *first*, then aggregate. (Norway's Supreme Court lets the senior judge speak first and has the lowest dissent.)
  - Then do scenario planning: pick two unrelated driving forces and test all four extremes.
- The best investments and the best science are both counterintuitive. His team set out to measure the universe slowing down and found it accelerating.
- Gut feel is real but also sees patterns in noise. Vet intuition with statistics, and feed it with sustained focus.
- AI is double-edged: it can fake learning the basics, and it is overconfident.
- It took 5 years to get the first good supernova. Rival teams swapped telescope nights and shared the prize.
- Advice: ignore the doom framing of the news. "This is your moment."

### 48. Venki Ramakrishnan — The science and hype of living longer (NBIM, Jul 2026)

*Chemistry Nobel laureate; author of* Why We Die. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cbaxr5HZKGg)

**Takeaway:** No physical law caps lifespan, but radical life extension is "highly unrealistic today." Aim for health span; the proven levers are boring: eat moderately, sleep, exercise.

- Average life expectancy doubled (public health, vaccines, nutrition), but the maximum (\~120) barely moved.
- Slowing damage yields more centenarians, not huge gains. Partial cell reprogramming is the plausible way past 120, but in mice it is patchy and uses oncogenes, and it hasn't extended mouse lifespan.
- AI is great at patterns (AlphaFold), but going from clues to treatments "happens in the analog world, in real people." Hassabis's "no illnesses in 10–15 years" is over-optimistic.
- Longevity money comes from ageing societies and from billionaires who "solved payments, so think they can solve death." On Brian Johnson: "no control experiment." Cryonics is "nonsense until you revive a mouse."
- His $3B portfolio would fund caloric-restriction pathways, old-versus-young blood, senolytics and reprogramming.
- Doubling lifespan risks stagnation and entrenched power. Great work mostly comes from people under 40, and the rich already live 10–15 years longer.
- Delegating everything to AI risks "making humanity stupider." "Not everything is about living longer."

### 50. Bülent Arman Aksoy — Predicting individualized therapeutic vulnerabilities in cancer (NHGRI, 2014)

*Then a graduate student at Memorial Sloan-Kettering.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UvH3qRepw7Q)

**Takeaway:** Cancer cells that randomly lose one of two redundant enzymes become selectively killable by a drug that blocks the remaining one. Mining genomic data finds these "Achilles heels" patient by patient.

- Normal cells keep a backup enzyme. A drug against the other one kills only the cancer cells, so toxicity is low.
- The pipeline combines pathways, cBioPortal/TCGA genomics, cell lines and drug–target data. It found \~4,000 vulnerabilities across \~6,000 samples; \~20% of tumors have at least one.
- \~40% of these are targetable by FDA-approved drugs. Matching cell lines allow lab testing.
- The vision is to profile a tumor, test its vulnerabilities, then pool patients into "basket trials."
- Limits: it models only single-reaction redundancy, and hit counts scale with sample size.
