## Investment strategies & how to think about markets

The fundamental investors converge on a simple recipe: own a few businesses with durable barriers to entry and pricing power, run by humble, aligned managers, for a very long time, and do the research of an investigative journalist. They differ mainly on concentration and on how much weight management deserves.

### 1. Quality means durable barriers to entry

- **Barriers beat growth.** "Growth without barriers is worthless" (Hohn). Competition and disruption are "the whole thing," because most of a company's NPV lies 20–30 years out (Hohn).
- **Growth × predictability.** Fast but unpredictable (young software) and predictable but slow (Nestlé) both underperform. Test: would you hold it if the market closed for 10 years? (Kantesaria)
- **Prefer a narrower moat with a growth runway** over the widest moat with nowhere to reinvest, like a saturated eBay (Dorsey).
- **Good companies stay good and bad companies stay bad,** but only if you judged quality right in the first place (Hohn).
- **Duopolies need rational competitors.** Boeing/Airbus, UPS/FedEx and potash fail the test (Kantesaria). The ideal is weak, rational, "apparent" competition (Hohn).
- **Returns converge on return on capital.** A business earning 6% on capital for 40 years returns \~6%, even if bought at a huge discount (Terry Smith, via Munger). At 4x book, a 20% ROE fully reinvested lifts a 5% yield on cost to \~10% in four years (Davis).
- **Three tests of quality.** A high return on capital in cash (cash delivered ÷ capital employed; \~25% across his portfolio), a secular runway to reinvest it, and a moat against mean reversion. High returns "landlocked" in a small niche must be handed back (Terry Smith).
- **Growth that needs no capital is worth more.** Coke reinvested \~20% of earnings to grow 12%. Moody's had \~$50M of PP&E and prepaying customers, so each dollar of its earnings was worth more (Goldstein, Greenblatt).
- **Know when ROIC misleads.** Where sales, marketing and engineering are expensed, "investment also happens on the income statement" and ROIC is "frankly meaningless". A falling ROIC can then signal a brighter future (Dorsey).
- **Industry structure drives about half the outcome.** Best is monopoly, then oligopoly, then a fragmented industry you can consolidate; worst is a sub-scale player where a few dominate (Haley). Distrust company-defined markets: monopolists inflate them, also-rans shrink them (Thiel).
- **Two simple quality screens.** Pre-tax return on capital = EBIT ÷ (net working capital + net fixed assets): 14% is "generally that's not good", good starts above 20% (Greenblatt). For pre-profit small caps, use gross profit ÷ tangible assets (Haley).

### 2. Pricing power is the tell

- Pricing 1% above inflation at a 20% margin grows profit \~5% faster than revenue, and price-led growth beats volume growth (Hohn).
- The ideal is an essential, low-cost product that can raise prices a few points above inflation for decades, but never use price to mask volume decline (Kantesaria). Classifieds raising price "5% a year forever even if there's no audience growth" now face AI disintermediation (Marques, May 2026).
- Abusing pricing power enlarges the profit pool and invites rivals: "if you take 5 in price, add at least 3 in value" (Dorsey). Managers often don't understand their own latent pricing power (Hohn).
- In inflation, own businesses whose pricing power passes on costs and protects a high ROIC (Haley, May 2022). Firms selling to the bottom 40–60% of earners struggle to pass on fuel and food inflation (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022).
- Fees that are small against what's at stake hold up: big issuers need both Moody's and S&P, so fees aren't very negotiable (Goldstein).
- Monopoly's financial signature is very high margins and cash piling up "year after year" (Thiel).
- A dominant rival's price cut can wreck an industry: Blockbuster dropping late fees cut gross profit per rental from $2.92 to about $1.69 (Gaines).
- Freight can make a "commodity" price like a franchise. Soda ash held \~$250/t in Western Europe and \~$180/t in the US over 2010–21, and Solvay got positive net pricing in inflationary 2023 (Einhorn).
- Sold-out capacity with long lead times compounds price. Carpenter Technology's prices rose 40% in two years, lifting EPS from \~$4.20 toward \~$10, "almost all of that is price". Its alloy is "maybe only 30 bips" of GE's cost (Advent, Apr 2024).

### 3. Management and capital allocation

- Dorsey moved from \~70/30 moat/management to \~30/70. He screens for humility with questions like "What's a do-over?" and "Which direct report would you least want to lose?" Red flags: "I" instead of "we," and moving HQ somewhere sunny.
- Hohn: a great manager can't fix a bad business, and arrogance is a killer. Fairfax turns away arrogant people however good they are (Watsa).
- Capital allocation is rare. Buybacks are neutral at best and M&A is mostly poor (Dorsey). Avoid serial acquirers and diworsification; the ideal company holds little cash (Kantesaria).
- Beware the "messiah" founder (Gaonkar). Founders aren't inherently better (Dorsey). Polished English made executives *seem* trustworthy, yet their stocks did worse (Xin).
- Alignment is a hard rule: "we don't invest in a single company where ... the incentives of management aren't aligned" (Davis). Stahl's owner-operators run a company "because they own more equity than anyone else".
- Ownership tests: small-cap managers should own at least \~5%, paid mostly in stock (Haley). A stock double should pay managers \~10x their annual cash pay (Goldstein). Exxon's executive pay vests 10 years after retirement (Davis).
- Ask how management chooses between projects, buybacks and M&A. A CFO who can't name a metric or hurdle is a red flag (Haley).
- Over a 5–10-year horizon "businesses are just people"; a CEO's clarity in writing (Bezos's 1997 letter) is a telltale (Sundheim).
- Boards are overrated in \~95% of cases, but a bad board can destroy a company; directors owning no stock is the bigger conflict (Davis).
- Keep the leaders who built it: "The reason they got to 2 billion in ARR is they did something right." Thoma Bravo's 2021 Medallia buyout swapped in an operator and "lost the innovative capability" (Bravo, May 2026).

### 4. Hold for a long time

- TCI's average holding period is \~8 years versus <1 year for typical institutions. Intrinsic-value compounding outweighs the multiple paid: Moody's was bought at 10x in the financial crisis and is \~$400–500 today (Hohn).
- Don't sell rare compounders: deferred tax is an interest-free loan (Li Lu). "Our favorite holding period is as long as the thesis holds" (Dorsey).
- Low turnover isn't complacency: keep re-ranking holdings against the watchlist (Kantesaria). "Do you need to change your wife every year?" (Hohn)
- Valuation sales are the classic mistake. Davis sold Costco bought at \~14x at 18–27x; it compounded on. Gotham sold Moody's up \~50%, then it rose 6–7x (Davis, Goldstein). "The biggest mistakes are selling the Costcos too early" (Sundheim).
- Fair value is a broad range; inside it, do nothing (Davis). Sell when the company stops executing, not at a price target (Haley, 2019).
- Li Lu's four reasons to sell: a realized mistake, a clearly better risk-reward, bubble-level valuation, or forced redemptions.
- Give value three to five years to be recognized, sometimes longer. Fairfax's 25% compound book-value growth included long stretches of only 3–4% (Watsa).
- Let appreciation pick the biggest positions: "The only way you achieve it is to leave it alone." Stahl held Texas Pacific Land from 1984 until his death in 2026.
- Switching compounds error. A PM right 70% of the time who sells A to buy B has 0.7 × 0.7 = 0.49 odds, "black versus red" (Stahl).

### 5. Valuation: roughly right beats precisely wrong

- Overpaying 10–15% hardly matters for a true compounder, and forward multiples mislead for special situations (Kantesaria). Ask whether a $10 stock is worth "a lot more than ten dollars", not $11 or $12 (Einhorn).
- Use multiples first and a DCF as a cross-check. DCFs undervalue firms that "beat the fade" back to the cost of capital (Dorsey).
- Think in whole-company numbers and do the maths mentally, about 5 minutes a page (Li Lu). Use free-cash-flow multiples and expense stock-based pay (Gaonkar).
- Value = incremental return on capital × the capital that can be deployed at that return (Gaonkar).
- Invert the P/E for a starting return and assume no rerating; 14–15x is "fine" (Davis).
- Reverse-engineer the price: some of the Magnificent Seven needed \~20% growth for a decade at >50% margins, achieved by \~0.1% of S&P 500 companies (Davis, Jul 2025).
- Use EV and pre-tax earnings, with EBIT = EBITDA − maintenance capex; EBITDA means nothing unless capex is \~zero (Greenblatt).
- Never discount at less than 6%, even with a 4% 10-year bond: 10x EBIT is \~6% after tax (Greenblatt, 2005).
- Normalize earnings and check that a low P/E isn't a cyclical peak (Greenblatt, Li Lu). Look through GAAP: Amazon reported losses yet produced cumulative free cash flow (Davis).
- The other side: most of the money comes from re-rating when doubts about a business model prove wrong; Priceline grew 40% a year at 9x earnings (Sundheim).

### 6. Research is investigative journalism

- Li Lu reads Value Line page by page and read every court filing on Timberland. His checklist: Is it cheap? A good business? Trustworthy management? What did I miss? Why is this being offered to me?
- Primary research methods:
  - building a dropshipping site to understand Shopify (Xin);
  - 100–120 management meetings a year (Pilecki);
  - niche trade shows and surveys of AI developers in non-tech industries (Gaonkar);
  - decade-long relationships with executives (Xin).
- Attach each thesis to an unbiased tracking data point, and spend as much time on what consensus could get wrong (Gaonkar).
- "Social arbitrage": spot cultural change, translate it into economics, find the expectation gap and time it before institutions notice (Camillo).
- **Invest, then investigate.** Take a meaningful but modest starter and give analysts 2–3 weeks; a wrong idea exits near flat (Druckenmiller). D1 starts buying before its memo is done (Sundheim).
- Alta Fox speaks with at least 15 CEOs or CFOs a week, and asks operators where they'd invest their own money (Haley).
- Qualitative work is the edge because quantitative data is crowded: talk to customers, ex-employees and ex-salespeople (Dorsey).
- At least 80% of the time the work shows a stock isn't cheap enough; it still pays when a stock later gets hit (Greenblatt).
- Study the stock, not only the business. Eminence spends 20–30% of research time on the "setup": the narrative, who holds and trades it, and what sell-side clients ask (Sandler, Eminence).

### 7. Where opportunities come from

- **Neglect.** About two-thirds of Kantesaria's entries are quality sitting in plain sight. Generalists ignore financials (Pilecki). Timberland had zero analyst coverage (Li Lu).
- **Positive change the market under-reacts to** gives you time to buy with confirmation (Pilecki).
- **Big change with a wide spread of outcomes,** where research can produce a differentiated view (Xin). The same idea from the quant side: Paddock trades paradigm shifts.
- **Crises.** In late 2008 "everything got cheap" (Singer), with good companies at 3–4x earnings (Watsa). Crises are great if you aren't forced to sell (Lebron). Crisis alpha arrives roughly every two years (Chan).
- **Misunderstood issues:** about a third of Kantesaria's entry points.
- **Situations the market can't easily value:** a new CEO or business line, complex segments, spin-offs, post-bankruptcy equity, IPOs, no comps. Combinations are best (Haley).
- **Durable franchises priced as fragile:** banks at 10–12x against utilities at \~20x in late 2024 (Davis).
- **Profitable small caps with operating leverage.** Of Alta Fox's 104 multibaggers (2015–20), 88% were EBITDA-positive five years earlier, with mid-40s gross margins and \~10% EBITDA margins, from \~10x forward EBITDA (Haley).
- **Temporary problems in cheap stocks.** Ask "temporary problem, or is it permanent?" (Greenblatt, via Pzena).
- **A shareholder-base gap.** Teva at 6x earnings was pivoting to biosimilars under a new CEO; value holders sold before growth investors arrived, and it re-rated to \~12x (Druckenmiller, 2025–26).

### 8. Activism and engagement

- Activism exists because index funds hold a plurality of equity and fewer investors act like owners. The best setup is an "open door," and \~70% of Elliott campaigns lead to meaningful action. "We're dealmakers, not crusaders" (Singer).
- Hohn has moved from hardcore activism to engagement: "the business always wins," and passive holders don't vote.
- Short-term activism can be destructive: activists would have fired Bezos in 2002, a "sugar high" (Davis). Activism matters more as passive funds stay silent (Haley).
- Friendly owners get deals. Fairfax may own 20–30% of a company but never goes hostile, and "companies want to do business with us" (Watsa). Einhorn says pushing an agenda is "very rare".
- Governments can do the activist's job. Korea's Value Up program forces governance reviews, buybacks and dividends, "mirror\[ing\] the experience that happened in Japan" (Marques, May 2026).

### 9. Indexing, hedge funds and retail

- "99.9% of active management is random noise minus fees"; most people should index (Kantesaria).
- Hedge funds sell uncorrelated, beta-stripped returns, so judging a market-neutral fund against the S&P is a category error (Tom). Retail's edge is freedom from LPs and benchmarks (Camillo).
- Pod shops: their virtue is that they can't stay wrong, but at \~4–6.5% pass-through fees and with crowded positions they create "air pockets" in sell-offs (Orr, Zach). Concentrated, long-term directional bets remain one of the few ways to get very rich (Matei). Pods cut risk when a PM is down "more than 3%," so "they're selling stocks when I'm buying them" (Mike Alfred, Alpine Fox).
- For most savers the "behavior penalty" of buying after rises and selling after falls swamps any stock-picking gain (Davis).
- For those who can't value companies, Greenblatt suggests \~30 magic-formula stocks held through long lagging stretches.
- Markets got less efficient as passives, retail, quants and multi-managers replaced mutual funds; short-term moves overshoot intrinsic value (Sundheim).

### 10. Special situations: get paid for complexity

- **Breakups stay mispriced even on the front page.** Analysts stop covering "until the smoke is cleared" and fund managers skip messy names. After Barron's laid out Sears's sum of the parts in 1993, Greenblatt's \~$5 stub reached $30 in two months (Greenblatt).
- **Value the pieces, not their quotes.** "Tear apart the company" conservatively: Sara Lee at $23 against $36 of parts was worth pursuing, against $27 it wasn't (Greenblatt). A listed stake worth \~80% of NAV can halve (Davis).
- **Read corporate-speak.** Sears's "no intention of disposing" of Allstate meant it would. Parents sell ≤20% before a tax-free spin, usually because the unit "didn't work out" (Greenblatt).
- **Use options only when you know something.** A call is stock plus a put plus a loan, so an in-the-money LEAP is a stock substitute. On Pzena's Lear thesis an $8.20 call could become $25–35 (Greenblatt, Oct 2005).
- **A distressed bond is a way to buy the equity.** Grupo TMM notes at 60 cents implied \~5x EBITDA for a railroad a strategic buyer valued at >8x, and doubled in \~18 months (Matt Mark, Jet Capital).
- **Good formulas lag for years.** Ranking on EBIT/EV and EBIT/tangible capital made 22.8% a year vs 12% for the top 1,000 stocks (1988–2004/05), yet lagged in \~6.5–8 of 18 years (Greenblatt).
- **Bonds covered by cash have a floor.** Nortel bonds at 40 cents, with \~$4B of cash against \~$4B of bonds, went to 100 cents. Fairfax bought again near 20 cents on a 40–50-cent recovery estimate (Watsa).

### 11. Short only when something is breaking, and size shorts for squeezes

- Overvaluation alone isn't a short: "we've always insisted on over valuation combined with some kind of deterioration" (Einhorn).
- Einhorn's exception was a "bubble basket" (from \~2014–15): 40–50 small shorts in profitless firms valued \~90% below price from their statements alone. He exits any name "showing real profits".
- Lookalike shorts don't hedge. In the 1998 Russian crisis Sandler's small- and mid-cap longs at Fusion fell while his large-cap lookalike shorts didn't. Eminence now shorts meme valuations, earnings misses, share losers, "melting ice cubes" and frauds.
- Volatility is asymmetric: a falling long shrinks, but on a losing short "leverage goes up" (Sundheim, May 2021). Trimming after a non-fundamental squeeze means "locking in negative alpha" (Sandler).
- Pre-size, then let shorts float. None may exceed 2% of capital. A $3–4B stock with 15% short interest in Reddit's top 50 gets at most 85bp, sized to survive a 100% squeeze (Sandler).
- AI may make quality compounders the best shorts: "the best short ideas are actually high quality compounding great businesses" (Marques, Pertento, May 2026). Their "service level deflation" is "not visible today in the near-term earnings".
- Shorting is a "negative carry business" (Alfred), so pay for it. In 2014 Eminence's analyst lists ran five longs to one short. It made its most skeptical analyst a dedicated short seller and double-weighted short alpha in pay (Sandler).

### 12. Index rules and short horizons misprice what patient owners can hold

- Float weighting buys more as insiders sell, "an algorithm for doing the opposite of what everyone knows is the sensible action." Index money also has no sell price: at "a million times earnings, the money would still come" (Stahl).
- Owner-operators earned "the bulk of the return" in the S&P 500 back to 1957, yet carry the lowest valuations: "It shouldn't exist, yet it does" (Stahl).
- Go where indexes can't. Dundee traded at \~C$1.40 against $10 of stated NAV: "It's too small to be in an index." Index capacity is "limited by the least liquid member" (Stahl).
- Price moves mean less. Passive's share of trading went from \~25% to \~50% by 2019 and "60 now"; "price action is much more meaningless than we used to think" (Sandler, Oct 2024).
- Quants bucket on trailing numbers. Zillow sat in "unprofitable growth" after exiting home flipping, but "what quants can't do is look forward" (Sandler).
- Non-AI stocks became a "source of funds" for AI and meme rallies (Sohn 2026 panel). Falling value stocks have "literally no buyers" (Rosen, Rubric); Reddit fell \~50% in an "AI loser" basket while its posts grew (Lennon, Pleasant Lake).
- The value analysts are gone. Passive replaced "the thousands of people" paid to find cheap stocks, so fine companies with double-digit cash-on-cash returns are more abundant "than at any time in my career" outside bear-market bottoms (Einhorn, Apr 2024).
- Time arbitrage is "one of the best inefficiencies in the market": most investors skip stocks that won't rise within six months (Einhorn). Calendar-year grading undervalues the long run (Stahl).
- He couldn't time the end. In Apr 2016 indexation was "in the late innings"; by Sep 2018 "I have no insight" (Stahl).
