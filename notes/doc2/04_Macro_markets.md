## Macro & markets

For equity investors, macro matters mainly through liquidity and the cost of money, which move valuations and decide which earnings survive. Druckenmiller reads it from inside the market rather than from economists' data; Karen Karniol-Tambour (Bridgewater) reads it from who funds spending, and Rob Citrone (Discovery Capital) from each country's policies. The stock pickers (Li Lu, Sundheim, Davis) don't forecast macro; they make sure their holdings can survive it. Two exceptions, Einhorn and Stahl, argue that debt and the end of disinflation favor gold and hard assets.

### 1. Liquidity and the Fed drive markets more than earnings

- "It's never been about politics, it's always about liquidity." What matters is "not even the level, it's the rate of change" (Druckenmiller, 2018).
- His 2018 gauges: central-bank buying falling from \~$1tn a year to zero, and M2 growth slowing from \~7.5% to 4% (Druckenmiller).
- Zero rates remove capitalism's hurdle rate, so "of course debt is going to explode." From 2010 to 2018 corporate debt rose \~60%, corporate earnings only 27% (Druckenmiller).
- "when you had 11 years of free money ... people do stupid things," and "6-month bear markets preceded by asset bubbles don't exist historically" (Druckenmiller, 2022–23).
- Greenspan's late-1990s refusal to hike was "the original sin." A 1% rate with 9% nominal growth in 2003 seeded the GFC (Druckenmiller).
- Non-bank lending makes policy harder to read. Still, when the Fed is "slamming the brakes on," as in 2022 (1% to 4%), worry about equity exposure (van Eck).
- The Fed kept "their foot on the gas" through 2021, buying $120bn of bonds a month at zero rates, then hiked 500bp in a year. Soft landings since 1950 all followed "Proactive rather than reactive" policy (Druckenmiller, May 2023).

### 2. Read the economy from inside the stock market

- "the best economists I know is the inside of the stock market." Stocks lead fundamentals by 6–12 months; housing and retail lead, capital goods lag (Druckenmiller).
- Dec 2018: the S&P was down only 13%, but autos fell 30% and homebuilders 35% while utilities, staples and pharma rose. He called it "amber" (Druckenmiller).
- Jun 2022: homebuilders were −50% and trucking −40% from their highs, trucking on record earnings. That warned of trouble 6–12 months out (Druckenmiller).
- Credit "tends to lead the economy": in late 2018 GE's CDS went from 50 to 200bp. Unemployment lags, and "most recessions were preceded by a very low unemployment rate" (Druckenmiller).
- His old signals are fading. QE switched off the 10-year, "the most important price in the world," and technical analysis is "about 20% as effective" (Druckenmiller, 2022–26).
- May 2023: trucking, "a Guiding Light for my firms" with a 6–8-month lead, was "extremely weak", and bankruptcies were "astonishingly low". He expected "a lot more bodies coming" (Druckenmiller).

### 3. As a stock picker, accept the macro and own earnings that survive it

- "The macro is what we must accept. The micro is where we can and should make a difference." With no leverage, a 50% drop can't force a sale (Li Lu, Dec 2024).
- Sundheim (D1) doesn't trade macro: it's "a binary bet," and a short-Treasuries trade "might never be right." Davis (Davis Advisors) never goes to cash; he buys "what people aren't interested in."
- Earnings that grow through a recession protect the stock: "if Staples can go up in price in a recession, why can't a company like Nvidia" (Druckenmiller, Jun 2023).
- In a bear market good earnings don't save expensive stocks. In 2018 Alibaba beat every estimate yet fell from 210 to 165, and cloud names de-rated from 9x to 7x sales (Druckenmiller).
- Avoid longs that need a macro outcome: "why in the world would you buy a group that needs rates to go up to own it" (banks, Druckenmiller, Dec 2018).
- In a hard landing profits normally fall 40–50%; he expected only 20–30% in 2023 because the recession was "so anticipated" (Druckenmiller, Jun 2023). His May 2023 definition: profits down "20 percent plus", unemployment from 3.4% to above 5%, and rising bankruptcies.

### 4. Rotate with the cycle, and know when to stand aside

- Buy an industry that is losing money and shutting capacity; beware one announcing expansions. Margins mean-revert within \~3 years (Druckenmiller).
- COVID "over-earners" (brick-and-mortar retail, trucking, then ocean shipping) gave him the best shorting period of his career (Druckenmiller, Jun 2022).
- "The present is not what moves stock prices. Change moves them." Picture the world 18–24 months out (Druckenmiller).
- When AI got "disturbingly heated" in late 2025, he rotated into biotech, down \~4 years with momentum turning. Teva re-rated from 6x to \~12x (Druckenmiller).
- Narrowing leadership is "a yellow light": no bear market has started without it (Druckenmiller, Oct 2024). In mid-2023 the S&P rested on 7 stocks (Gerber, Hudson Bay).
- "My record is as much knowing not when to play as when to play." He saw no fat pitch in Jun 2023 (Druckenmiller). Crises are where returns are made (Li Lu).
- Plan the way out before the downturn. His May 2023 list for after a hard landing was copper, US housing, biotech and AI. He held off copper, since cyclicals fall as a hard landing starts (Druckenmiller).

### 5. Inflation, rates, deficits and debt

- Two "undefeated records" once inflation tops 5%: it has never fallen unless Fed funds exceeded CPI, nor without a recession (Druckenmiller, Jun 2022). In May 2022 real rates were "at the most negative level in the last 70 years" (Einhorn).
- The 1970s template: inflation fell from \~8% to 3%, then rose again. Bonds ignored it until it reached 12%, then "responded in spades" (Druckenmiller, Oct 2024).
- The 10-year can trade to nominal GDP: 6–7% if inflation runs 4–4.5% with 2.5–3% real growth, versus \~4.5% then (Druckenmiller, Oct 2024).
- Deficits of \~7% of GDP at full employment "can't last forever." His guessed trigger is "a failed auction," in "6 months... six years" (Druckenmiller, Oct 2024). In May 2023 he called entitlements and interest a "200 foot tsunami"; the reserve currency's curse is that "markets don't check you".
- Deficits of 6% of GDP are "two feet on the gas pedal." The 40-year fall in rates is over, so long backtests carry a falling-rate bias (van Eck, Jul 2026).
- The 10-year has been below 3% in only two periods since 1871. Shadow-bank leverage breaks if long rates stay higher, starting with commercial real estate (Gerber, Jun 2023). The median regional bank had 43% of loans in commercial real estate, \~40% of that office (Druckenmiller, May 2023).

### 6. The supports behind record margins and low inflation may be reversing (2022–26)

- "everyone's spending is somebody's income," so economy-wide margins grow only when spending is funded other than by wages: borrowing, dissaving or deficits. With COVID checks, "at the same time the economy collapsed corporate profits actually surged to new highs" (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022).
- By Jun 2022 those supports were reversing: "we've already globalized every good we could globalize." After "the peak pro corporate environment", expect higher taxes, more antitrust and wage pressure (Karniol-Tambour).
- Profits alone don't make equity returns. In the 1970s commodity producers were \~30% of the market, so profits "did great", yet stocks had their "worst decade ever"; producers are \~3% now (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022).
- Disinflation rested on "two and a half Miracles": dumped Soviet resources, \~3bn new low-cost workers and multinationals entering newly opened countries. "Those are the Miracles and that's over," so inflation "can last decades" (Stahl, Oct 2024).
- There's no Volcker exit, Stahl argued: private equity is "essentially leverage Equity", and bank balance sheets are "many orders of magnitude bigger" than in 1982.
- "the fed is bluffing" (Einhorn, May 2022). With $24tn of public debt and \~$7tn rolling within a year, each 1% adds \~$70bn to the deficit, so "it has to pick the treasury." "At that point it's best to have some gold."
- Underinvestment makes old-economy profits stick: "higher prices aren't fueling new capacity instead they're fueling buybacks." Einhorn called cement, steel, chemicals, mining and energy "attractive long investments" (May 2022); by Apr 2024 soda ash's excess profits were "normalizing".
- The S&P 500 holds "as a practical matter no tangible Assets," and rebalancing toward them will be "very disruptive in an equity sense." AI capex means "we're returning... to the world of hard or tangible... assets" (Stahl, 2024–26).

### 7. Currencies, gold, bitcoin and real assets

- A peg between diverging economies is "a one-way bet" with cheap carry: the 1992 pound short cost 0.5% for six months. He passed on Brexit, a ±10% coin flip (Druckenmiller).
- In early 2026 he was short the dollar: near the top of its purchasing-power range, with foreigners "way, way overloaded" (Druckenmiller). Karniol-Tambour was short the dollar against the euro and yen in May 2025.
- Gold is "mainly a geopolitical trade" (Druckenmiller, 2026). It is van Eck's preferred hedge for US spending, but in a loss-of-confidence liquidation "nothing's going up, even gold."
- Own Bitcoin in an inflationary bull market and gold in a bear market or stagflation (Druckenmiller, 2022). Bitcoin is gold's immature "teenager" (van Eck).
- Hold 60–70% equities and \~10% real assets, with bonds the rest; real assets now diversify better (van Eck, 2026). Copper is "very tight for the next 8 years" (Druckenmiller, 2026).
- Gold is the reserve asset when trust breaks. Freezing Russia's reserves weaponized the dollar, yet gold's share of reserves was "staggeringly low": India 8%, China and Saudi Arabia 4% (Einhorn, May 2022).
- Stahl called Bitcoin "the most conservative investment there is": supply is capped at 21m, and each four-year halving doubles the cost of a coin. Gold isn't fixed: at $5,000/oz more comes out of mines and jewelry (2024–26).
- Alfred, a holder since 2017, expects $1m a coin "probably by 2033 or 2035", but "we could have a 60 or 80% draw down at some point." It fell from \~125k to \~60k by spring 2026.

### 8. China, Japan, India and the rest of the world

- "I was in love with China until about six or seven years ago." Under Xi he expects "a very undynamic economy" for 10–15 years (Druckenmiller, Jun 2023).
- A government that can "act capriciously" has "a humongous gravitational pull on valuations," so D1 left China around 2022 (Sundheim, Oct 2025).
- China's key variable is consumption: \~40% of GDP, versus 60% in India and >70% in the US. Supply-side stimulus can't fix a demand problem (Li Lu, Dec 2024).
- Japan was Druckenmiller's "biggest value trap in history," but by Jun 2023 it was embracing shareholder value. By early 2026 he held big Japan and Korea positions.
- India has a deregulation "green light" and the fastest growth in 2026, yet is underweighted in indices. Its rupee "is just not dollarized" (van Eck, Jul 2026).
- Tightening breaks emerging markets first: "that's kind of where it always starts" (Druckenmiller, 2018).
- Citrone looks at "countries like we look at companies": management and policies first. Latin America was "left for dead for the last 25 years"; his top pick, America Movil, offered a 10% FCF yield, 10% growth and 7–10% yearly buybacks (May 2025).
- Korea's discount may finally close with Japan-style Value Up reforms. On next year's consensus Samsung and SK Hynix are the world's two most profitable companies, at \~6–7x earnings (Marques, Lennon, May 2026). Lennon allowed it may be "the very... top."

### 9. The current regime (2025–26)

- Three forces drive markets: modern mercantilism, the AI build-out and record capital concentration in US equities. "We're all Brazil now" as fiscal limits bind. Go global; Europe is investable and China diversifies (Jensen, Bridgewater, Nov 2025).
- Markets are "about as risky as I've ever seen": leverage, deficits >6% of GDP, AI "over its skis" (Singer, Elliott, Feb 2025).
- A seven-person group's 12-month predictions were \~80% wrong, so build scenario planning and geopolitical capability instead. Be more circumspect on private equity; favor private credit only with cash yield (Temasek, Sep 2025).
- Post-crisis banks are well capitalized, so no crisis is imminent. Private credit's risks are hidden double leverage and zero recoveries on failed software loans (Pilecki, Apr 2026). It can carry "something much more similar to equity-like risk" (Karniol-Tambour, May 2025).
- AI spending is demand-pulled and power-constrained (Nadella, Oct 2025), so at worst expect price corrections, not contagion (Ullal, Dec 2025; Hoffman, Feb 2026). Debt-financed AI capex is "usually a bad leading indicator" (Sundheim, Oct 2025).
- The economy will get "much stronger" on fiscal stimulus, but valuations are "toward the top of the valuation range historically." "Macro has been dead for 10 or 15 years. I don't think that's the case anymore" (Druckenmiller, early 2026). The S&P is at 26x (Davis, May 2026), with semis and optical \~23% of it (Marques, May 2026).
- Cohen (Point72, May 2025) saw no recession yet but "significant slowing growth": \~45% recession odds and a 14–17% effective tariff rate. With high multiples he expected a sideways market: "Right now the market feels toppy."
- Citrone (May 2025) expected a 5–5.25% 10-year and no Fed cuts in 2025, then a 2026 boom on tax cuts and reshoring: "next year is going to surprise everybody."
- Lenders have repriced software. Refinancing leverage fell from \~7–7.5x to \~5x and spreads widened \~250bp plus fees; lenders now say "No, this is not software" (Bravo, May 2026).

### 10. Process lessons specific to macro investing

- Put big bets only in liquid markets (bonds, FX), so "you can change your mind when you're wrong" (Druckenmiller).
- Bet on asymmetry: in Dec 2018 he argued a wrong hike would cost "five to ten X" a wrong pause. Hedge the macro you don't want, as with short credit against long Treasuries (Druckenmiller).
- Being early costs as much as being wrong: his 2018 short was "three months early." Separate trade timing from thesis (Druckenmiller).
- "When I'm wrong I changed my mind." He faults the Fed's forward guidance because "you eliminate your optionality" (Druckenmiller, Oct 2024). Know your bias: "Bulls make more money than bears."
- General sizing lessons are in Risk, sizing & psychology.
- Swing only at fat pitches; otherwise "keep your gross low be open-minded." In May 2023 he ran \~60% gross and flat net, with Treasuries "off the table" at 3.5% against 5% fed funds (Druckenmiller).
- A thesis needs a time frame: "eventually is not a good time horizon for a hedge fund." Greenlight held no gold for years, until "eventually is now" (Einhorn, May 2022).

### 11. Implications for the screener

- **Rate resilience.** Check interest coverage at today's rates and debt maturing soon; termed-out corporate debt rolls over in 2025–26 (Druckenmiller). Flag high leverage with near-term refinancing (Gerber).
- **Real per-share growth.** Flag EPS growth driven by debt-funded buybacks or M&A: 2010–18 S&P EPS rose \~60% versus corporate profits +27% (Druckenmiller).
- **Recession earnings.** Score revenue and operating-income change in 2008–09 and 2020; sustainable earnings in a recession protect the stock (Druckenmiller).
- **Cyclical over-earning.** Flag margins far above long-run history (Druckenmiller), and check whether a low P/E reflects peak or one-off earnings (Li Lu). Be most wary where sales rely on transfers or consumer dissaving, as record 2022 margins did (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022).
- **Sector sensitivity.** Tag businesses that need higher rates (banks) or depend on office real estate (Druckenmiller, Gerber). High EV/Sales, high-beta names de-rate even without misses (Druckenmiller). A 1.5-point rise in the 10-year cuts the value of a 10-year earnings stream \~15% (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022).
- **Funding risk.** Cash-burning growth companies can suffer "a capital chain break" in a crisis (Li Lu, Dec 2024).
- **Market overlay.** Track cyclicals versus defensives, credit spreads and leadership breadth. Cut forward estimates when oil, the dollar and rates rise together (Druckenmiller).
- **Country filter.** Weight consumption share of GDP (Li Lu), discount markets without due process (Sundheim) and favor deregulating economies (van Eck). Einhorn invests only in developed markets, where insiders and local rules are easier to judge from New York.
- **Tangible assets and supply discipline.** The index holds almost no tangible assets; royalty and land owners need no reinvestment (Stahl). Favor industries returning cash instead of adding capacity (Einhorn, May 2022).
