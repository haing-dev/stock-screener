## Healthcare & biotech investing

The healthcare specialists agree that their edge is a better probability estimate than consensus, not a cheaper multiple: does the drug truly work, what is it worth if it does, and how do you hold it so a miss doesn't sink you? Generalist quality investors mostly skip clinical-stage biotech. Profitable pharma, medical and services businesses can pass quality filters, but only after checks the financial statements don't show: patent expiry, government pricing, royalties and financing needs.

| Investor | Firm and style | How they underwrite | Signature lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bihua Chen | Cormorant: \~$2.5B biotech long-short plus private funds | Causal biology, potency and the data room; \~4x peak sales | "Knowledge is different from judgment" |
| Jim Flynn | Deerfield: $16B across public equity, structured debt, venture and its own labs | Probability-weighted cash-flow models for every instrument | Fail cheap; protect the basis, keep the skew |
| Dave Johnson | Caligan: small/mid-cap life-sciences activist | Approved assets worth more than EV; pipeline as a call option; \~3:1 up/down | "we don't take a lot of scientific risk" |

| Annie, Rod, Eli (GEF panel, Nov 2021; first names only) | Health-tech services; drug company creation; public-private life sciences | Payment reform, base rates, total system cost | "technology is not the issue when it comes to healthcare innovation" (Annie) |

### 1. Drug investing rewards specialist judgment, which is why most quality investors stay out

- Kantesaria avoids biotech as unpredictable despite 18 years in biotech VC. Alta Fox has little in biotech because it lacks "exceptional businesses with durable \[moats\] that we can understand" (Haley).
- The core question is whether a drug works, judged on incomplete information. "Every company will tell you something is causal, and most time it's not" (Chen, Cormorant).
- Credentials and networks aren't the edge. Baskets, university ties and backing a CEO's past success don't work, and a degree is "not sufficient or necessary" (Chen).
- "Science is just a language" that anyone can learn; the real skill is making good probabilistic assessments (Flynn, Deerfield).
- The generalist alternative: Druckenmiller made "a big move into biotech" (2025–26), though the science goes "right over Stan's head." He trusted a specialist team and judged how the market would embrace the change.
- Be "very skeptical" but not cynical: cynics miss huge winners through bias about who looks successful (Chen).

### 2. Value a pipeline as peak sales × a deal multiple × probability of success

- Long-term value is "actually easy": biotech trades at \~4x peak sales of its major drugs. Pharma pays 2–3x to 6–8x, depending on uniqueness, patent strength and exclusivity (Chen).
- Johnson's MorphoSys formula (Jun 2023): peak sales × \~2x precedent deal multiple × 78% probability of success (PoS) ÷ shares. That gave \~45/share against <13 paid.
- Build peak sales bottom up: monthly price × share of newly diagnosed patients × duration taken from the standard-of-care discontinuation curve (Johnson). Duration, patient count and value-justified price all lift peak sales (Chen).
- The edge is a different PoS. "If you think probability of success is 70% other people think it's only 30%," you make money if right (Chen). The sell side used 50–60% versus Caligan's 78% (Johnson).
- Deerfield uses "long-term cash flow based probabilistic models" for everything, "even long-term drug discovery." A genetic association doubles PoS, and antibodies double it again versus small molecules (Flynn).
- Respect base rates. \~90% of drug assets fail (Walmsley). Academic ideas fail \~95% of the time and half don't reproduce, so \~2.5% succeed (Flynn, 2021).
- Ex-Lilly and Novo, pharma trades at \~12x earnings because R&D ROI is \~zero: "The biggest cost is failure" (Ricks).
- Don't build a takeout into the value. Casdin's biggest mistake was overweighting M&A and sizing up: "maybe my valuation was right but the probability I should have assigned to it was much smaller."

### 3. Read clinical data as if the drug doesn't work

- Rank the hypothesis. Inherited genetics is strongest, single-driver cancers are strong, and picking the right cytokine in inflammation is where "you could really go wrong" (Chen). Genetics-linked diseases fall first; brain diseases are "big and complicated" (Flynn).
- Common traps: correlation taken as causation, too little potency in humans (resveratrol), and 99.999% protein binding. Storytelling won't find these; the data room will (Chen).
- Johnson's checks: is the Phase 2 confidence-interval lower bound (about 57%) above the standard of care (about 40%)? Is Phase 3 upsized, and did it enroll \~6 months early while a rival's ran on time? Add 3–4-year durability data and calls to trial doctors.
- There are "two kinds of risks": your drug fails, or a competitor's works better. Add-ons to standard of care must add efficacy without toxicity (Johnson).
- Read rivals' labels. Daxxify's six months was a median at double the dose, from a placebo trial rather than head-to-head (Johnson, Sep 2022).
- After a failure, ask whether the biology was wrong or the technology fell short; the latter becomes "an engineering problem." Distrust "retrospective signal finding" (Chen).

### 4. Structure exposure around binary events

- Buy a floor and get the binary cheaply. MorphoSys hit negative EV in Dec 2022: "you own this option for nothing." By Jun 2023, net cash after burn, approved products and up to $535M of milestones still covered >50% of the price (Johnson).
- Deerfield's favourite structure is senior secured debt plus warrants: one part "protects your basis," the other captures the skew (Flynn).
- Melinta shows why: when COVID hit, Deerfield credit-bid, ran it six years, doubled sales from $60M to $125M and sold for over $300M. A pure lender would have lost most of its money (Flynn).
- Venture biotech "had been bad for decades" because 90% lose, and lose a lot. Deerfield runs discovery at marginal cost and kills fast: "if you create a company around it it's going to want to live" (Flynn).
- The payoff: Nuvalent went from a $2M test-tube proof, funded alone, to \~$10B with two lung-cancer drugs filed (Flynn, Jun 2026).
- Trade the calendar, too. Stocks sit in a "catalyst desert" and wake up \~6 months before data (Johnson). Gerber's first off-floor strategy (1995) was Phase 3 FDA binaries with option overlays.
- Near-term catalysts are usually priced. In Casdin's factor data, high-impact events 1–3 months out were negatively correlated with returns: calling +10 cents was right, but "now it had to be 15 to beat."
- Look for catalysts nobody models. Viatris traded at 6–7x earnings, "100% converted to cash flow", while two drugs it bought from a forced seller, Idorsia, read out in 6–9 months. Idorsia held its trial data back for a journal, so "nobody is valuing this" (Rosen, Rubric, May 2026).
- Only Casdin described a sizing method: each idea is scored on management, market, return profile and risks, so the team knows "why something was 1%, why something else was 5%."

### 5. Financing and the biotech cycle drive short-term prices

- Biotechs are long-dated, cash-burning assets under financing pressure, so interest rates "influence biotech valuation a lot" (Chen).
- Flynn has seen three bubbles in 36 years. Each brings 1–2 years of doubling or tripling, then 1–2 years collapsing "back to the same point." In May 2025: "Things are cheap again."
- In bubbles "People accept companies that are too early, too risky, require too much cash," and they "just have to go away" (Flynn). For real winners, 2020 was a dip, not the price peak (Chen).
- After the 2022 capital drought, "capital allocation has never been more important": "focus on your winners starve your losers" (Johnson). Non-dilutive Pharmakon debt ($125M) funded Evolus to breakeven.
- Bubble-era zero-coupon converts later traded in the low 60s. Buying back at 72 earned a 22% IRR, and MorphoSys bought a quarter at 64 (Johnson).
- Business-model changes create mispricing. MorphoSys sold its 28-program royalty portfolio to Royalty Pharma for $1.4B and its European holders left. It became "a U.S biotech in a German shell" (Johnson).

### 6. Back complete teams, and fund them so they never run out of money

- Accept missing some good teams to avoid the bad ones. About 70% of the teams Casdin judges bad turn out bad, while \~30% he doubts create value anyway (Casdin, Jul 2022).
- Test management with a suggestion. "Oh, that's a good idea" is a red flag; "6 months ago we instituted a program to do that" reassures (Casdin).
- Fill every seat, not just the scientist: chemistry, trial design, regulatory, finance and commercial. "A's hire A+ people, and similarly B's hire C's and C's hire D's" (Casdin).
- Over-capitalize, then scale in from the inside rather than front-load and "fight dilution on the way down" (Casdin).
- Long-duration capital is his biggest edge: with LPs locked in he can "step in" during selloffs, which were "nine out of 10 times" unrelated to the industry (Casdin, mid-2022).
- Invest public and private together: "If you're only investing in public companies and not talking to private companies, you're only hearing half the story" (Casdin).

### 7. Pricing power is capped by patents, payers and politics

- Generics take "98% of volume within a month" of patent expiry (Flynn, 2026). A typical pharma patent cliff cuts revenue \~90% (Johnson). Pharma "replaces itself every decade" (Walmsley).
- Cliffs drive M&A. With \~$140B of pharma revenue going off patent in the late 2020s, boards must "justify not doing deals." The partner facing expiry is the natural buyer, e.g. Incyte with Jakafi in 2028 (Johnson).
- Most of pharma's top sellers were acquired from biotech, sometimes \~$10B pre-approval for a drug selling $5B a year (Chen). Pharma buys biotech \~6–12 months after selloffs (Silver, Maverick).
- Durability without patents exists. Botulinum toxins rely on trade-secret cell banks and avoid the cliff, at lower margins than small molecules (Johnson). Patents are Terry Smith's "least liked" moat because they expire.
- Government payers cap returns. Medicare/Medicaid-dependent companies see "they change the rules" if they earn too much, so demand a larger margin of safety (Greenblatt). Botox can't undercut its government price (Johnson).
- Jeuveau's aesthetics-only label escapes those rules, letting it run \~20% cheaper for patients, \~20% more profitable for practices, with loyalty rewards (Johnson, Sep 2022).
- Flynn is sanguine: "If someone comes up with something transformative, people pay for it," and drugs are only \~20% of costs. He conceded "abuses" in 2021, such as TNF prices that "inch up not down."
- Price risk is rising for me-too drugs: China has ten near-identical PD-1s at a tenth of US cost, "coming here" (Eli). Botched reform could push innovation abroad (Rod).
- Payer coverage can be the margin lever. Many Natera tests are "not getting paid anything at all" until coverage arrives, and >$1bn of R&D plus \~4x the nearest rival's publications won it. Buckley expected profits "nearly two years before consensus" (Duquesne, Apr 2024).

### 8. Where the industry is going

- Druckenmiller called obesity drugs "a razor blade business" because patients regain weight off the drug; he sold Lilly "in the high 700s" (Oct 2024). Chen says the obesity story is "only half written" (2026).
- AI in discovery is early. The first wave "did not deliver" and "the impact has not been felt yet" (Chen, 2026). It helps the earliest stage but not yet the slow, costly trials (Flynn).
- "Turn on a computer and invent drugs" is hype, though an AI fix lifted Lilly's GLP-1 output 8% (Ricks). Cures happen "in the analog world, in real people" (Ramakrishnan). Druckenmiller still calls biotech AI's best use case.
- Life-science tools are AI and reshoring winners "left for dead," with 3–5 consolidators buying $5–10B companies (Silver, Jun 2026).
- In care delivery, data is the moat, not software, which is "hard to invest in" (Flynn). Software will diagnose most people within 5–10 years (Flynn, May 2025).
- The bottleneck is payment, not technology: \~80% of care could happen at home, and hospital-owned primary care raises costs \~10% (Annie). Back products "better cheaper to the system" (Eli).
- Contrarian services trades work. Flynn bought nursing homes when "everyone hates this"; Brookdale occupancy rose from 64% to 84%. Davis owns UnitedHealth for the third time, after what "happened to" it in the year to May 2026, citing "massive mispricings."
- A genome took \~13 years and \~$3bn by 2003; now it takes a day and $500. Casdin argues cheaper biology raises success rates: "The lights are now on." He expected \~20% a year for a decade, but "buckle up because it's going to be very volatile" (Jul 2022).

### 9. What a financial-statement screen can and can't see

- Healthcare can pass quality tests. Pharma and health care are among sectors with persistently high ROIC over 1963–2004 and the latest 20 years (Smith). Healthcare was over-represented among Alta Fox's 2015–20 multibaggers (Haley).
- But Haley warns those winners may enjoy competitive dynamics that "will not persist," because regulation "greatly impacts the healthcare space."
- Clinical-stage biotech can't be screened: the edge is whether the drug works, and comps are the "less value added" part (Chen). Haley's positive-EBITDA filter removed it.
- Check commercial drug companies against pharma economics: 80–90% gross margin and up to \~50% operating margin. Operating margin depends on marketing needs and efficiency (Chen). Diagnostics and tools run \~70–80% gross margins, but "technology is not going to cover up crappy businesses" (Casdin).
- Low multiples can hide a transition. Teva was a "boring generic drug company" at 6x; a new CEO's biosimilars pivot took it from 16 to 32 and \~12x (Druckenmiller, 2025–26).

### 10. Implications for the screener

1. **Bucket pre-revenue biotech separately** rather than scoring it on quality metrics; a positive trailing EBITDA filter does this (Haley, Kantesaria, Chen).
2. **Flag patent-expiry exposure.** Treat revenue from drugs nearing expiry as \~90–98% at risk (Johnson, Flynn), and favour brands, installed base or trade secrets (Smith, Johnson).
3. **Haircut government-payer businesses.** Require a larger margin of safety where Medicare/Medicaid sets returns (Greenblatt).
4. **Red-flag price-led growth in old, crowded classes** (Flynn on TNF inhibitors) and me-too drugs in crowded mechanisms (Eli, Chen).
5. **Benchmark margins by subsector:** drugs at 80–90% gross and toward \~50% operating (Chen); diagnostics and tools at \~70–80% gross (Casdin).
6. **Check runway to the next catalyst.** Net cash must stay positive after burn (Johnson); flag bubble-vintage, cash-hungry IPOs (Flynn).
7. **Screen for a value floor:** EV below net cash plus approved products, royalties and milestones, ideally negative EV, then demand \~3:1 (Johnson).
8. **Track contractual catalysts:** royalty step-downs (+1,000bps at Evolus), regulatory milestones and discounted converts at cash-rich issuers (Johnson). Add payer coverage and drug descheduling (Buckley, Rosen), and fade sharp run-ups into known readouts (Casdin).
9. **Don't buy fallen biotechs mechanically:** \~8 of 10 post-data collapses are justified (Chen).
10. **Add a sector-cycle overlay:** after a 1–2-year collapse to pre-bubble levels "Things are cheap again" (Flynn); Druckenmiller entered after a \~4-year slump as momentum turned.
