## Investing in the AI boom

The tech specialists agree that AI is a real, early S-curve whose value has gone first to compute, chips and hardware bottlenecks. Models are settling into an oligopoly, power and sites have become the binding constraint, and horizontal application software is the first casualty, though domain leaders may still win. They split on price and timing: Whale Rock, Light Street and Maplelane saw estimates still too low in mid-to-late 2026, while Davis and Druckenmiller heard late-1990s rhymes, and even Altman expects a glut.

### 1. Ride the S-curve, but only behind an airtight advantage

- An S-curve inflects when adoption barriers fall: smartphones went from 1% to 50% ownership in 4–5 years. The curve is "a map into the future," answering Buffett's objection to tech (Sacerdote).
- Growth turns linear at \~30–40% penetration. Yet Sacerdote calls selling Apple in 2012, at \~50% US smartphone penetration, a mistake: it kept compounding \~20%. "It's okay to miss the first 100%" if the curve is tall.
- Without a moat "you can be in the best S-curve of all time and still lose out": RIM, Palm and Nokia went to zero (Sacerdote).
- Curves can stall. US EVs hit a wall at \~10–15%, and Tesla went from "10 out of 10" to "zero out of 10" for Whale Rock (Sacerdote).
- Tech cycles run infrastructure (about 5–10 years), platform (about years 6–16), then applications (about years 11–21). As of Sep 2026 AI is "a third of the way through the first leg" (Kacher).
- AI is "an L-curve, straight up": enterprise application AI <1% penetrated, infrastructure \~10% (Sacerdote, spring 2026). The leader takes \~two-thirds of a market, #2 "20% 25% tops" (Kacher).

### 2. So far value has accrued at compute and the bottlenecks

- Infrastructure inflects first and is needed "no matter who wins above," so Whale Rock went "chips and the infrastructure first." It bought Nvidia in Jan 2023 at \~4x its 2025 earnings (Sacerdote).
- Money is made "following that migration of that bottleneck further upstream": GPUs, fabs, tools, even "obscure materials listed on a Japanese stock exchange." The sharpest "revision torque" hits low-margin starting points (Tykocinski, Jun 2026).
- AI workloads grow \~10x a year against \~30% in the x86 era, decommoditizing parts. Celestica, sole Google TPU server supplier, traded at \~8x earnings; PCBs went from 10 to 40 layers (Sacerdote).
- Semis became an oligopoly: Nvidia holds \~85% of AI accelerators, and TSMC can "win no matter who wins" (Kacher, Sep 2026). Wafer-fab equipment could go from \~$120–130bn to \~$300bn in 3–4 years (Shaulov, May 2026).
- Power is the next layer down. "Gas turbines are going to be in shortage for a very long time," yet Siemens Energy traded at half GE Vernova's EV (Sundheim, Oct 2025). Druckenmiller worked down the chain to power, uranium and copper.
- Enablers such as Applied Materials, TSMC, Texas Instruments, gas and copper "make a lot of money whether or not the returns on the capital being spent are good or not" (Davis).
- Memory was cheapest in Korea: SK Hynix at \~6x 2026 earnings vs Micron at \~10x, both "massive cash gushers" (Marques, May 2026).

### 3. Below the chips, power and sites bind now, and a glut will test the operators

- The choke point "hasn't been chips" (Alfred, Jun 2026). Microsoft has chips "sitting in inventory that I can't plug in" for lack of powered "warm shells" (Nadella, Oct 2025).
- US power use has been flat since 2007, so natural gas is "the vehicle of choice" for data centres. Stahl owned LandBridge, Permian land with water and rights of way (Oct 2024).
- Underwrite the operator: "how good are they at taking power and turning into compute." An edge resting on a rising commodity price means "long periods of time where it's not fun to be an equity shareholder" (Alfred).
- Three models compete. Colocation landlords skip the GPUs, IREN and xAI own the compute, and CoreWeave relies on "20 plus external providers." Alfred, an IREN director, bets on owning the stack.
- Delays are a tell. Operators hailed as winners reported delays and guidance cuts that the market called temporary: "No, these are systemic issues" (Alfred, Nov 2025).
- A glut will come: unit cost falls "like 40x" a year, "a very scary exponent from an infrastructure buildout standpoint." "Some people are going to get really burned" (Altman, Oct 2025).
- The defense is a fungible fleet and a short backlog: "fungibility of the fleet is everything for a cloud provider." Microsoft's $400B RPO averages \~2 years, and "circularity ultimately will be tested by demand" (Nadella).

### 4. The model layer: oligopoly or commodity, and who pays for it

- Whale Rock's April 2023 options were winner-take-all, commodity or an oligopoly of 3–4. About 50–60 startups died, leaving Anthropic, OpenAI and Google as a "three-horse race" (Sacerdote, 2026).
- Sundheim expects 4–5 lasting LLMs because "the capital required to get into this business is too great." Gross margins are "quite high," unlike a commodity industry (Feb 2026).
- His model is Netflix plus Spotify: a huge fixed training cost sold at high incremental margins, made sticky by personalization (Sundheim). Druckenmiller saw it the other way in Oct 2024: "they're all going to give you the same answer," so he stayed neutral.
- The real risk is capital intensity "to a degree that we've never seen before in the history of business." With that leverage, the labs can't afford 2–3 years of slow adoption (Sundheim). Altman tempers scaling hopes: "Unfortunately," intelligence scales roughly with the log of compute (Oct 2025).
- If models plateau, open source catches up and "it might be a race to the bottom." Chips would hold up better: "chip companies don't care who's winning tokens" (Sacerdote).
- Hyperscalers split the group. Sundheim sees "a worse business model going forward" as demand concentrates in 4–5 labs that will insource compute. Kacher sees them "playing catch-up," and Sacerdote said Google has "won AI" and is "very cheap" (May 2026).
- Inference decides who pays. Each chat burns "a lot more GPU cycles" than a search, so consumer monetization is "murky," but "enterprise monetization is much clearer" (Nadella, Oct 2025).

### 5. AI as a threat: software first, but not all software

- Whale Rock held 40–50% in software \~5 years ago. Incumbents' AI "products were not very good... Nobody could charge for them," so it was net short entering 2026 (Sacerdote).
- His headwinds: CIO budgets shifting to tokens, no more annual price rises, seats hit by hiring freezes, and a new sales motion. Salesforce has \~$40bn of sales but only \~$500–700m of AI ARR.
- Sundheim had "basically no shorts" on AI victims before 2026. Systems of record (ERP, CRM) are protected "at least for for a few years" but small productivity tools are more exposed.
- The SaaS-apocalypse view is a "huge simplification": "core systems don't tend to get swapped out," as banks' mainframes showed. Selloffs create bounceback trades (Kacher, Sep 2026).
- "It's too broad to say software": horizontal applications are in trouble, while data and infrastructure software like Datadog can win (Shaulov). The labs using Datadog is a "pretty good tell" (Sacerdote).
- Davis sorts the rest into enablers, users, insulated firms (Tyson) and the "walking dead" (Kodak, newspapers). Users have big data sets, cuttable labour costs and willing management (Capital One). "It's still early" to buy a non-tech stock for its AI plan (Kacher).
- "agents are the new seats," as an agent tier replaces the old business-logic tier. High-price, low-usage products have "a little bit of a problem," and AI adds "a real marginal cost to software this time around" (Nadella, Oct 2025).
- Domain leaders deploy "most of the agents being deployed right now," and "our retention rates are holding" (Bravo, Thoma Bravo, May 2026). In software "There never was" much of a moat, so leadership decides.
- Don't bottom-fish: "this is exactly the wrong time to buy a mediocre software company at a bargain price," even at a 7–8% day-one yield (Bravo).

### 6. Bubble or not: the debate extended, 2023–26

The disagreement table pits Ullal, Hoffman, Bloisi and Nadella against Jensen, Singer, Kantesaria and Altman. The new voices add dates and tests.

- **Druckenmiller (Jun 2023):** AI is real, unlike crypto, but "all of AI is not going to make it through." Secular runs last years: Cisco ran \~4 years in the dot-com boom.
- **Shaulov (Apr 2024):** $3–4tn of AI savings or revenue (\~5–8% Fortune 500 efficiency) justifies $500–800bn of spend, "15 to 20% Capital intensity."
- **Li Lu (Dec 2024):** Nvidia is worth more than all German listed companies combined, "despite potential bubbles in its valuation," because it is indispensable.
- **Sundheim (Oct 2025):** \~70% of breakthrough technologies brought a bubble. Watch debt-financed capex, circular financing and bad companies at crazy prices. Nvidia at "20-something times" looked like 1996–97, not 1999.
- **Druckenmiller (early 2026):** AI got "disturbingly heated" from mid-2025 and began to rhyme with 1999–2000, so he rotated into Teva and biotech.
- **Davis (May 2026):** "Similarities are bigger than the differences." Amara's law puts us in "the overestimating phase," and the internet's leaders Yahoo, AOL and Cisco ruined their holders. The S&P was at 26x earnings.
- **Alfred (Nov 2025–May 2026):** bubble talk was "a complete joke" after Nvidia's Nov 2025 results, but by May 2026 "we might be in '97 right now... We might even be in mid-'99." He watches data-center capex, which turned with the stocks in 2000.
- **Maverick (Jun 2026):** dot-com capex ran \~200% of operating cash flow; AI's ran "well under 100 percent." But a multi-trillion-dollar company just issued equity, so it has "more similarities by the day."
- **The bulls (May–Sep 2026):** Nvidia was called a bubble every year for four years; "It's going to be bouncy" (Sacerdote). It is the mid-90s, not the late 90s, and demand outruns supply (Kacher). Shaulov expects "some accidents" but "the next 50 to 100 is up."

### 7. Private vs public: the labs and SpaceX

- Some of the largest companies are private: "you really have to know what's going on in the private markets" (Sacerdote). D1's private–public overlap is at a record "because of AI" (Sundheim).
- Private markets are less competitive on analysis but competitive on access: "that company has to want you to be an investor" (Sundheim).
- D1 entered OpenAI at the $125bn round only \~65–70% sure it would be a good business, betting on the skew. It backed Anthropic as "second player" on Dario's writing, "better than almost any CEO I've seen since Bezos" (Sundheim).
- Whale Rock passed at $60bn on negative gross margins, then invested at $180bn in Aug 2025 after meeting management and building a 90-page deck. It calls it "a total home run" (Sacerdote).
- SpaceX is a low-cost launch near-monopoly plus Starlink (P × Q subscribers), able to triple without long-shot bets (Sundheim, Oct 2025). By Feb 2026 Starship and Starlink had made global telecom its market.
- Counterpoint: illiquid privates are one of Davis's "three horsemen" for the next five years; "people have underestimated how valuable liquidity is." In private tech, bad bets show faster than great ones (Sundheim on Rivian vs SpaceX).
- Microsoft's \~$13.5bn bought a 27% OpenAI stake worth $135bn, and "you can't lose more than 13.5 billion" (Nadella, Oct 2025).

### 8. Valuation and sizing in a fast-moving theme

- Value on earnings 3–5 years out: Whale Rock's best entries were \~4x out-year earnings (Nvidia, Tesla) while near-term P/Es looked expensive (Sacerdote).
- Winners "may not screen as the cheapest" because "estimates are like 50 to 70% too low" (Shaulov, May 2026). "It's all been earnings"; multiples haven't caught up (Sacerdote). In secular trends, multiples rerate with revisions (Tykocinski).
- Davis is the counterweight: he models no multiple expansion and avoids even good businesses at hype valuations.
- Sizing: up to 10% at cost, run to 15%, with AI infrastructure "might be 20% of the fund" (Sacerdote, early 2025). Light Street's four core semis were \~40% of its public book (per host). "The biggest reason people blow up in Tech is they miss on revenue" (Sacerdote).
- Druckenmiller started Nvidia "not big" but "enough to get hurt on," then doubled twice. He sold at \~800 because "I couldn't stand seeing success"; it hit \~1,400 five weeks later.
- Expect pain: tech has a big selloff every 7–10 years (Sacerdote). Light Street lost 54% in 2022, then made 46%, 59% and 37% (Kacher, per host).
- Hard assets can cap the downside. In Q1 2023 Alfred valued IREN's sites at "maybe two to four X" its market cap; it rose from $1.02 (Dec 2022) to \~76 (Nov 2025). As a director, he has never sold.

### 9. Implications for the screener

- **Backward-looking quality misses S-curve winners.** Celestica and Elite Materials were low-margin "commodity" businesses before AI. Flag revenue acceleration plus rising ASPs and gross margins, and estimate revisions from a low margin base (Sacerdote, Tykocinski).
- **Add a forward-earnings lens.** Rank by P/E on earnings 2–4 years out, not trailing, since consensus may be far too low (Sacerdote, Shaulov).
- **A long record can hide an AI loser.** "Don't feel safe in times of change": Kodak was in the S&P's top third as digital overtook film (Davis). Flag seat-based pricing, reliance on annual price rises and AI revenue ≤1–2% of sales (Sacerdote).
- **But don't auto-exclude software.** Favour systems of record with strong distribution and stable retention (Sundheim, Kacher). Bravo's markers: the domain's #1, long-tenured leaders, and \~20% growth with steady margins and retention.
- **Watch capital intensity.** Flag capex above operating cash flow, debt- or equity-funded build-outs and circular financing (Maverick, Sundheim). Rising capex plus customer concentration can erode a hyperscaler's margins even as growth speeds up (Sundheim). Expect a lower multiple when defensive capex shrinks free cash flow (Stahl on Alphabet, Mar 2026), and prefer short, diversified backlogs (Nadella).
- **Don't capitalize peak hardware margins.** Hardware and materials are "more subject to commodification," and China competes in optics and analog (Tykocinski). For heavy reinvestors, "a declining return on capital may indicate a brighter not a worse future" (Dorsey).
- **Test infrastructure owners on assets and delivery.** Compare market cap with land, power and equipment, and flag repeated delays, guidance cuts and rented hosting (Alfred). In 2000 data-center capex "turned over right around the time that the stocks turned over" (Alfred, May 2026).
