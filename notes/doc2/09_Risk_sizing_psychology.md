## Risk, sizing & psychology

Across styles, the durable investors define risk as permanent loss or ignorance, not volatility. They size so that being wrong never knocks them out, and they treat their own conviction and emotions as the biggest hazard.

### 1. Define risk properly

- Risk is permanent loss of capital, not volatility (Gaonkar, Pilecki). "Volatility isn't the risk that matters most": Quantedge deliberately runs \~25% volatility.
- Risk is "not knowing what you're doing" (Hohn). Start with risk, not return.
- Elliott's worst trades came from misclassifying risk: a "late-stage" bankruptcy that wasn't, and a bond arbitrage priced on a deflation scenario that got worse (Singer).
- Negative-skew strategies (option selling, LTCM) need more than Sharpe. LTCM's data was simply too short to contain the killer outliers (Carver).
- "Risk management is not about not losing money. Risk management is about unexpectedly losing money" (Gerber).
- Volatility and beta are not risk; risk is how much money you can lose (Greenblatt).
- Concentration means exposure to one macro variable, not one sector: in 2007 Countrywide, Toll Brothers and Lehman were one housing bet (Davis).
- Kill ideas with \~70% customer concentration, single suppliers or one plant (Haley).
- Value at Risk was the GFC's flaw: holding capital for only 95–98% of outcomes lets a bank take a tail bet "an infinite number of times" (Einhorn).

### 2. Survive first, so you can exploit crises

- Losing 88% of his parents' money in 1974 taught Singer that losses wreck judgment. Intact capital and an intact mind let you buy when "everything got cheap" in 2008. "Make a buck, keep it."
- Risk control, not brilliance, is why Bridgewater outlived its 1991 competitors. Build portfolios that survive the widest range of worlds rather than betting on one (Jensen).
- Avoid regular 30% drawdowns: recovery needs +43–50%. Don't mix leverage with concentration (Orr).
- Stay near flat between big setups and keep capital for them (Paddock). Holding cash is fine (Li Lu).
- GameStop (Jan 2021): D1 covered shorts at the worst time rather than risk the firm; those shorts later underperformed massively. Size so you never have to (Sundheim).
- Debt turns equity into an option bet, and bank debt is worse than bonds because banks can force a bankruptcy (Gaines).
- Run money like an insurer: plan for the 1-in-50-year event and ask "what happens if things go down 50% can we survive it" (Watsa). Einhorn carries no leverage, so no lender's terms can force him.

### 3. Sizing rules from the interviews

| --- | --- |
| Li Lu | When you've done the work and see no downside, bet big, not 25–50bp positions |
| Dorsey | 10–15 stocks, starter position 6–7%, maximum 15%, 15% hurdle rate |
| Hohn | 10–15 stocks at \~10% each; conviction is easier in physical-asset businesses |
| Xin | Loss budget: if a stock can fall 50%, it can't be 10% of the book unless you accept losing 5% of NAV; ≤25% in any one country |
| Pilecki | \~40 names at \~3% each because idea ranking is unreliable; add when the market confirms you, never when it falls |
| Cedar | Skilled PMs' #1 mistake is starting good ideas at 1–2% instead of 4–5%, which can halve returns |
| Ying | Size by volatility, conviction, crowding and upside; fundamental investors size badly |
| Zach | Inverse-volatility weights, then tilt toward PMs whose niche is "printing," under a hard cap |
| Orr | Hundreds of small positive-EV bets; cut at least half if many go wrong at once |
| Quantedge | Information ratio ≈ skill × √(independent bets), so maximize breadth |
| Druckenmiller | Sizing is "70 to 80% of the equation". Start with \~1/3 of a position and add on confirmation; with conviction, 15–20% in a stock |
| Sundheim | Mix +50%/−50% bets with +25%/low-risk ones; \~40 small shorts instead of \~8 so you never have to cover |
| Gerber | Set leverage from each idea's reasonable worst-case loss so the risk profile stays constant |
| Haley | 8–12 longs, 15% max at cost (2019); a full position should need a recession-type shock to fall 40–50% |
| Davis | Raised his father's \~5% soft cap to 8–10% |

| Einhorn | A small number of large, unlevered positions, but "even our best idea" is capped; assume any long can lose 100% |
| Stahl | Start under 5% and let the winner become the biggest position; \~25bp for a long shot whose worst case is zero |
| Alfred | 40–50%+ in one idea to target 30–50% a year; accept 30–50% drawdowns "several times in a cycle" |

### 4. Conviction versus doubt

- "Conviction" is often bias and the reason people hold losers and mis-size. Earned conviction comes only from living through bull and bear markets (Xin).
- 100% conviction is a red flag. Go "from FOMO to TOMO" (thoughtfully missing out) and spend as much time on what consensus could get wrong (Gaonkar).
- When already very bullish or bearish experts get even more extreme, bet against them; follow the first who breaks ranks (Cedar).
- Science's balance: doubt is the brakes and can-do confidence is the accelerator, and you need both (Perlmutter).
- Li Lu and Kantesaria show the other side: when the work is done, concentrate and hold.
- "My advantage is not IQ, it's trigger pulling." The danger in concentration is stale positions, not size (Druckenmiller).
- Write a pre-mortem: the article from five years ahead explaining why each holding underperformed (Davis).
- "You always have doubt"; in a slump, "go back to your process." A hitter can go "0 for 20 and yet it's okay because they've been hitting the ball hard" (Cohen, Point72, May 2025).

### 5. Admit when you're wrong, quickly

- If an idea isn't making money within months or a couple of quarters, treat yourself as wrong. "You'd rather be right than make money" is the trap. A market moving against you is "one of the strongest tells there is" (Orr).
- "Positions can become less risky at higher prices." Falling prices often mean you're wrong. Re-underwrite and never average down by default (Pilecki).
- Great investors exit losers quickly instead of insisting the market is wrong (Rubenstein). Take the 12–20% loss instead of hoping for an activist (Dorsey).
- Sunk-cost bias: Gaonkar sold Nvidia early and then never revisited it. Missed names are a rich source of new ideas.
- Omissions are the biggest mistakes of all: Li Lu missed a 50–100x stock and Kantesaria passed on Microsoft's IPO.
- Cost basis is irrelevant: if the reason is gone, sell, and be willing to buy back higher. He'll exit a 3-year trade within 5 days (Druckenmiller).
- Set a re-underwriting threshold: earnings down 5–10% rarely change the thesis; down 20% gets a full work session (Sundheim).
- When a position goes wrong, presume "we might have missed something here." Reassess, then reduce, exit or hold; adding happens "very rarely" (Einhorn).

### 6. Emotions, temperament and self-knowledge

- Turn off the daily P&L email, or think in percentages (Carver).
- Journal every decision to learn your pain point and reflexes. Strong emotions and rigidity are the easiest tells of a bad investor (Ying).
- Greed and fear don't change. Executing rules through losing streaks is unnatural, "like boxing form" (Yokokawa).
- "People who say they're risk-neutral are actually risk-seeking" (Lebron on SBF).
- Temperament is largely fixed, and people revert to it when alone. Extreme delayed gratification is required (Kantesaria). You must be comfortable being alone (Li Lu).
- Trade smaller in cold streaks and bigger in hot ones. "There's no reward for degree of difficulty" (Pilecki).
- Constant risk aversion also prevents burnout, since adversity drains emotional energy (Singer).
- After a mistake, stop torturing yourself after \~48 hours; when you're an emotional mess, step away completely (Druckenmiller).
- Even a formula that doubled the market lagged for 3–4 years at a stretch, and clients leave by year three (Greenblatt). Stahl's firm endured "gargantuan underperformance running almost 3 years during the internet bubble" rather than buy "what we decried" (Bregman, his co-founder).
