## Careers & building a fund or firm

The career advice is consistent. Find where you are differentially good and genuinely interested, because interest drives the hours that become edge. Build deep, transferable skills, and make your work visible. For founders of funds, structure and terms matter as much as returns.

### 1. Choose where you have an edge and genuine interest

- Find what you're *differentially* good at, empirically. Use \~3 deliberately different internships, because interest is idiosyncratic: some people love finding a trade on page 34 of a prospectus (Lebron).
- Ask what you'd do if every job paid nothing, and whether you'd enjoy solving problems alone (Bianco).
- Choose what you'd stay up all night doing, not what looks best on paper. People overrate their ability to calculate expected value (Xin).
- "Choice matters more than hard work." Fish where there are fish, but don't chase the hottest sector; juniors mistake sector luck for skill (Ying). Pick the seat where you're seen as a future partner, not a resource (Xin).
- Only enter investing if you love it, Saturday mornings reading transcripts included. Seats are shrinking (Pilecki). Many brilliant problem-solvers don't enjoy trading's reward system (Lebron).
- Experiment to find what you enjoy, then do something useful (Rubenstein). Stay open to surprise: Ricks joined Lilly only because his wife studied in Indiana.

### 2. Skills that compound

- **Maths and statistics depth.** Quant firms want statisticians who know finance, not finance people. Rewriting his resume as pure statistics produced two offers in a month (Bianco).
- **First-principles thinking.** Reconstruct the core truth, then adapt (Ying). Remove all constraints and rebuild from scratch with today's tools (Arora).
- **Breadth.** Prefer a liberal education (history, politics, philosophy) over business courses (Singer). "You need many straws" to see the sky (Strannegård). Be a generalist who connects the dots (Ricks). A broad curriculum always helps (Ramakrishnan).
- **Programming and data fluency.** Versor tests "hands on the keyboard." Someone on every team should be able to audit AI output (Ying).
- **AI-native fluency.** Pitch yourself as the person who can help transform an organization (Hoffman). Fundamentals still matter, because AI is a force multiplier like a calculator (Lebron).
- **Work ethic.** "You'd better learn to love to work." It's hard to learn after your early 20s (Bianco).
- **Finance fundamentals.** Knowing options, finance and accounting let Tepper see at once that Goldman's desk model mispriced calls. You need them to program machines or to compete with them (Tepper).

### 3. Breaking in

- Publish \~10 stock write-ups or "fund letters" publicly. If you have edge, people will find you (Pilecki, Orr).
- Open a small account ($5–10K), build \~30 genuinely different longs and \~50 tiny shorts, and get 500–1,000 reps (Orr).
- Operations and investment-support roles get \~2 applicants versus \~200 for analyst roles, and a proactive operations person adds real value (Orr).
- Getting hired by showing returns on your own money "almost never happens" (Carver). No question is too stupid on day one (Lebron).
- Go where learning is fastest: AI in San Francisco, manufacturing in China, drones in Ukraine. Talk to people 5–10 years older (Lebron).
- Sundheim's anonymous Value Investors Club write-up exposed a fraud (capitalized expenses); it circulated among hedge funds and got him his job.
- Bad seats can teach the most. Tepper joined Republic Steel's treasury "on its way to bankruptcy" and did more financings in 1–2 years than in its 100-year history. "what's gonna define you is how you recover from those things" (Tepper).

### 4. Building a fund

- **Don't bend terms to raise capital.** Quantedge introduced lock-ups and soft-closed its liquid class, a move it estimates cost a \~$20B fund but bought a 3-year+ investor horizon. Employees' permanent capital gives others faith in the 50-year plan.
- **Prepare for years.** Keep a "hedge fund" document, resolve one launch item a day, and first succeed in one niche (Zach). Expect enormous effort (1,500+ meetings a year) and thick skin. Limit prospective LPs to two calls.
- **Hire PMs only to repeat what they've done for 3+ years.** Asking them to be "5–10% different" lowers success rates (Zach).
- **Lean cost structure.** No base salaries or signing bonuses, and minimal pass-through costs (Zach). Greenlight began as two people in 130 sq ft; today a 10–40-person firm needs "a couple hundred million" to launch (Einhorn).
- **Incentives.** Remove pay uncertainty, since discretionary bonuses on top of trading stress are "borderline cruelty." Avoid traders who accept blow-up risk as "worth it" (Paddock). Reward people collectively so diversifying strategies aren't punished in lean years (Quantedge).
- **Run it as a business.** Founder-investors love the craft and neglect the business, so most single-manager funds have key-man risk and no exit value (Matei).
- **Capital takes time.** Allocators cap stakes at \~25% of a fund and want 3-year records (Jha). Protecting capital doesn't stop outflows: down 19% in 2008 against \~40% for the market, Eminence still halved from \~$5B to $2.5B (Sandler). Bootstrapping a data business avoids investors and a boss (Jha).
- **Code every idea.** Hudson Bay's deal codes record how each idea was sourced, hedged and exited, plus its worst case; that scaled one trader's discipline across 27 PMs (Gerber).
- **Expand only into adjacencies, slowly.** A 2007 move into power transmission rights became Gerber's biggest mistake.
- **Outside capital needs far more infrastructure than you think.** Schonfeld expected three or four extra people and was off by several hundred (Tolkin).
- **Face investors at the bottom.** At the June 2022 trough D1 kept its LP dinners and cut portfolio risk (Sundheim).

### 5. Small versus large firms

- Small firms can compete once they cover costs (\~$100–200M): they are nimble, less likely to be cut in a drawdown, and can trade less liquid names (Jha).
- Industry structure is heading toward mega-pods plus a long tail of $100–500M niche boutiques, with generalist mid-size managers squeezed (Zach). Only "a few dozen" firms with long-short DNA remain (Sandler, Oct 2024).
- Big funds have money but bureaucracy; small funds can't afford AI talent, a "Nash equilibrium" (Chan). Outsourced data teams can give single managers platform-grade tools faster than in-house teams slowed by compliance (Matei).
- Barriers keep falling. Monthly API developers grew from \~20K to 80K+ in six months as "solopreneurs" trade through entities (Yokokawa). Cedar expects \~10x more hedge funds.

### 6. What great investment cultures look like

| Firm | Cultural signature | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bridgewater | Write down every belief, stress-test it and code it; work that isn't compounded "doesn't count"; radical transparency with feedback flowing upward | Jensen |
| Man AHL | University-like: shared research library, seminars, proposals with CIO veto; people for whom money is secondary | Carver |
| PDT Partners | Long tenure and small collaborative teams that help solve firm-wide problems | Jha |
| Third Point | "Kind but not nice," flat, zero politics; Loeb finds the flaw on page 26 | Akash |
| Quantedge | All staff wealth in the fund; collective rewards; graduates only | Quantedge |
| TCI | 7–8 investment professionals; trust; bearish team members test every bear case | Hohn |
| Pod shops | Siloed, can't "stay wrong," but crowded and costly | Orr, Zach, Carver |
| Hudson Bay | Every idea coded with its hedge and worst case; risk integrated with portfolio construction | Gerber |
| Schonfeld | Separate books with one shared P&L goal; a speak-up forum at every level | Tolkin |
| D1 | Written earnings reviews by morning; weekly mock portfolios count toward pay | Sundheim |
| Whale Rock | A "learning machine": \~2,500 management meetings a year | Sacerdote |
| Duquesne | PM buys first, analysts investigate after; specialists' enthusiasm weighed as data | Druckenmiller |
| Eminence | No screaming; two people on every stock; a dedicated short seller; the CIO decides after "you've had your day in court" | Sandler |
| Thoma Bravo | \~200 people for \~$200bn, with "very little middle management"; a 4-hour monthly operating review with every company; keeps founders | Bravo |
