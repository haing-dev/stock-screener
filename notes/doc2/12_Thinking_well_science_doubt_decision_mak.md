## Thinking well: science, doubt & decision-making

The scientists and educators supply the meta-skills behind everything above: think in probabilities, actively seek disagreement, use protocols that protect decisions from bias, and stay humble about prediction. Investors who succeed independently reinvented many of the same tools.

### 1. Think in probabilities, not certainties

- Bet 90% on this and 70% on that, rather than calling things true or false. Holding a 25% chance you're wrong makes group discussion fluid and less ego-driven (Perlmutter).
- Think in ranges, not single hands, as in poker (Jensen). Make many positive-expected-value bets and eliminate the negative ones (Orr).
- You can never *know* you have an edge, only statistical bounds (Lebron). Information ratio ≈ skill × √(number of bets) (Quantedge).
- Poker and stock-picking are "a very similar skillset": separate what you know (price, sales), what you can surmise (motives, competition) and unknowable events within a range (Einhorn).

### 2. Seek out disagreement deliberately

- Science's strength is putting work in front of people who will attack it. Society has lost this habit and retreated into bubbles, although people in different bubbles share most goals and differ mainly on factual questions (Perlmutter).
- Institutionalize dissent in your own organization:
  - a contrarian team argues against every major go decision (Ricks);
  - inherently bearish team members test every bear case (Hohn);
  - radical transparency, where the best managers draw out the most negative feedback (Jensen);
  - friendly competing teams that are rewarded for finding flaws (Perlmutter).
- Founders treated like gods end up in echo chambers (Matei). In a flat culture, "only the argument matters, not seniority" (Strannegård).
- Watch for skeptics who change their minds: "to me that's a signal." CTO Kevin Scott's conversion drove Microsoft's OpenAI bet (Nadella).

### 3. Protocols that protect decisions

| Protocol | How it works | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blind analysis | Hide the result until everyone agrees all error checks are done, then "open the envelope" | Perlmutter |
| Write first, then discuss | Collect independent written views before meeting to avoid herding; Sweden's court lets the youngest judge speak first and sees more dissent than Norway's | Perlmutter |
| Scenario planning | Pick two unrelated driving forces and test your position in all four extreme combinations | Perlmutter, Temasek |
| Pre-specified criteria | Define the results needed to advance before the experiment; hit them and go the next day | Ricks |
| Checklists | "Cheap? Good business? Trustworthy management? What did I miss? Why is this offered to me?" | Li Lu, Gaonkar |
| Decision journal | Record every decision to learn your reflexes and pain points | Ying |
| Written beliefs as code | State every belief and its reasoning, stress-test it and turn it into algorithms | Jensen (Bridgewater) |
| Pick sources blind | Choose which sources you trust before seeing their view on your case | Perlmutter |
| Invest, then investigate | Take a meaningful but modest starter, research for 2–3 weeks, then add or exit | Druckenmiller, Sundheim |
| Pre-mortem | For each holding, write why it underperformed five years from now | Davis |
| Deal codes | Tag every idea with its hedge, reasonable worst case and history; track ideas won versus lost | Gerber |
| Mock portfolio | Analysts submit weekly sizing on record, so reviews can't rely on memory | Sundheim |
| Written reasons at purchase | List five or six reasons when you buy; after a 50% fall, buy more only if they still hold | Watsa |
| Three-year mistake review | Review the last three years, not this year's winners and losers, and group mistakes into biases | Sandler |

### 4. Intuition and analysis, used against each other

- The unconscious recognizes patterns like a neural net, but it also sees patterns in noise. Vet intuitions with statistics, and feed intuition with long, focused effort; answers often arrive after sleep (Perlmutter).
- Magnus Carlsen intuits about three candidate moves, then analyzes them (via Tangen). Hohn calls intuition "thinking without thinking," built from experience.
- Aim for Kahneman's methodical "system 2" and use data to debias "system 1" (Gaonkar).

### 5. Humility about prediction

- Seven very smart people recorded 12-month predictions and were \~80% wrong (Temasek). Ullal trusts her own forecasts only 1–3 years out.
- Hype check from a Nobel biologist: "no illnesses in 10–15 years" ignores that treatments happen "in the analog world, in real people." Tech leaders' bias is to see life as software (Ramakrishnan).
- Demand control experiments: one-person longevity regimes and young-blood clinics have none (Ramakrishnan). Most hockey-stick backtests hide a flaw (Kantos).
- The world has always been uncertain. Civilization makes more of it manageable, and thriving means riding uncertainty rather than fearing it (Perlmutter).
- "History is the art of surprises." The Sino-Soviet split was known, yet few drew the conclusion, because concluding "outside of your historical frame of reference is extraordinary difficult" (Stahl).

### 6. Curiosity, humility and staying human

- Exposure to art and ideas you don't understand builds humility and empathy, which machines lack. Education should make people better at being human as machines get better at being machines (Strannegård).
- *Bildung* is "what remains after you forget what you learned." The FREE model asks you to be fact-based, reflective ("are my opinions my own?"), empathetic and entrepreneurial (Strannegård).
- Curiosity is contagious: a "learn-it-all" rather than "know-it-all" culture (Ricks). The best fundamental investors are insatiably curious, like the billionaire taking Python classes in his late 50s (Matei).
- Don't delegate all thinking to AI, which risks "making humanity stupider" (Ramakrishnan). AI can make you feel you've learned the basics before you have (Perlmutter).
- Youth matters: new generations don't inherit old mistakes, and great science mostly comes from people under 40 (Perlmutter, Ramakrishnan).

### 7. Practical life lessons from the scientists

- Health span beats lifespan. The proven levers are boring: don't overeat, sleep 7–8 hours (repair happens during sleep) and exercise. Anything that reduces stress, even a cold plunge you enjoy, is probably fine (Ramakrishnan).
- Persevere: Perlmutter's team expected 30 supernovae in 3 years, had zero after 3 and its first good one after 5, and still won a Nobel. Rival teams swapped telescope nights and shared the prize.
- Ignore the news's doom framing, which is designed to scare. "This is your moment" to work on planet-scale problems (Perlmutter). "Not everything is about living longer; it's about enjoying life while you have it" (Ramakrishnan).
