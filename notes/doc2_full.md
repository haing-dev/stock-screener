### The 12 biggest cross-cutting lessons

1. **Barriers to entry beat growth.** Airlines grew for a century with almost no cumulative profit (Hohn). Own businesses whose dominance you'd bet on for 10+ years (Kantesaria, Dorsey, Li Lu).
2. **Survive first.** Losses wreck judgment, and intact capital is what lets you exploit crises (Singer, Watsa). Funds die from redemptions and drawdowns at the wrong time, not from volatility (Quantedge, Orr, Jensen).
3. **Omissions cost more than mistakes.** The biggest regrets are the 50–100x stocks never bought or sold too early (Li Lu, Kantesaria, Gaonkar, Davis, Sundheim). In venture, missing a great company costs more than backing a poor one (Hoffman).
4. **Be contrarian *and* right.** If everyone agrees, there's no alpha (Matei, Rubenstein); the edge is an "important difference of opinion", found "maybe just a few times a year" (Einhorn). Great companies and discoveries looked wrong to smart people at first (Hoffman, Armstrong, Perlmutter).
5. **Distrust your own conviction.** "Conviction is often bias" (Xin), and 100% certainty is a red flag (Gaonkar). Most errors are overconfidence (Carver). Use blind analysis and written independent views (Perlmutter).
6. **Cut losers and add to winners.** A market moving against you is "one of the strongest tells" (Orr). Positions can become *less* risky at higher prices (Pilecki). Great investors exit quickly when wrong (Rubenstein) and let winners become their biggest positions (Stahl).
7. **Edge is relative and decays.** Edge is what the marginal participant lacks (Lebron), and it returns when rivals leave: passive investing emptied the value industry (Einhorn, Apr 2024). Shared data creates crowded trades (Jha). NLP signals get arbitraged away (Kantos), and each automation wave collapses pattern-matchable alpha (Ying).
8. **AI accelerates research and encodes judgment; it doesn't find alpha alone.** Data is too scarce and markets too unstable (Chan, Gebhardt). Judgment and disciplined execution stay human (Ying, Yokokawa), but analysts using agents can cover 3x the names (Akash).
9. **Culture and speed are management choices.** Culture is what leaders do under pressure, repeated relentlessly (Walmsley, Ricks, Vélez). Speed comes from parallel work, pre-set criteria and small "jet ski" teams (Ricks, Bloisi).
10. **Curiosity plus humility is the common trait.** It shows up in billionaire investors (Matei, Rubenstein), founders (Gentz, Forrest Li) and Nobel scientists (Perlmutter) alike.
11. **Quality compounds; cheapness doesn't.** Over long holds your return converges on the business's return on equity, "no matter what your starting price is" (Davis, Terry Smith via Munger). A cheap, low-return business is a value trap without a catalyst (Davis, Greenblatt, Bravo).
12. **Sizing decides outcomes as much as selection.** "sizing is probably 70 to 80% of the equation" (Druckenmiller, via Soros). Set size from the worst case before the trade, not after it (Gerber, Sundheim, Sandler).

## From insights to screener rules

The 63 new interviews, added in two batches of 39 and 24, mostly confirm the screener's core design: high cash returns on capital, sustained over many years, with room to reinvest, outside low-return industries. They also point to four gaps. The screen ignores price; it can't see investment that is expensed rather than capitalized; it doesn't test resilience to recessions and refinancing; and it treats healthcare as one bucket. The second batch adds checks on tax-flattered returns, owner-operators, spin-off parents, capex that buys no growth, and software and AI-infrastructure risk. Each change below is attributed, and effort assumes SEC financial-statement data unless marked.

### 1. Keep: what the new voices confirm

- **High returns persist, so a long record is informative.** High-ROE companies have clustered near \~20% since 1966, and no sector's ROIC switched ends (Terry Smith). Over long holds your return converges on the business's return on capital (Davis, Terry Smith via Munger). This supports gates G1 and G5.
- **The exclusion list matches the evidence.** Terry Smith's persistently low-ROIC sectors are utilities, telecoms, transport, energy, materials and retailing; Alta Fox's study screen also drops energy, materials and financials (Haley).
- **Cash, not reported profit.** Prefer free cash flow to earnings or EBITDA, which "can be easily manipulated" (Haley). Greenblatt and Terry Smith both measure returns in cash. This supports G7.
- **No momentum or volatility inputs.** Davis treats momentum buying as the opposite of investing, and Greenblatt calls beta and Sharpe ratios "garbage in garbage out". Price drawdowns aren't sell signals for a well-understood holding (Li Lu). With passive, quant and pod flows setting prices, short-term moves say even less (Sandler, Oct 2024).
- **Expect the obvious names and long dry spells.** Visible moats are "probably efficiently priced" (Dorsey), which is why a test run returns MSFT, V and MA. Good formulas also lag for 3–4 years at a stretch (Greenblatt).
- **Strong balance sheets are offense as well as defense.** Cash-rich companies keep investing through the AI shift, while those that "levered up to buy back stock or paid dividends" are "suffering" (Resnick, Jericho, Apr 2024). This supports the debt limits in Core and Fortress.
- **Fortress's recession limits are demanding enough.** A hard landing cuts profits "20 percent plus" (Druckenmiller, May 2023). Fortress lets operating income fall only 15% in 2020 and 25% in 2009.
- **Funded runway for clinical names.** In growth investing, lesson one is "don't run out of money" (Casdin, adapting Buffett). He over-capitalizes companies burning $100–150M every 12–24 months, which supports the clinical screen's 2-year runway rule.

### 3. Healthcare: split the bucket

- **Keep commercial pharma, medtech, tools and services in the screen.** Pharma and health care are persistently high-ROIC sectors (Terry Smith), and healthcare was over-represented among Alta Fox's 2015–20 multibaggers (Haley).
- **Keep pre-revenue biotech out.** Its edge is whether a drug works, which numbers can't show (Chen, Kantesaria); gates G3 and G4 already remove it.
- **Add three healthcare flags.** Revenue near patent expiry is \~90–98% at risk (Johnson, Flynn). Medicare/Medicaid dependence needs a larger margin of safety (Greenblatt). Growth from price rises in old, crowded drug classes is a policy target (Flynn).
- **Benchmark margins by subsector:** drugs at 80–90% gross and toward \~50% operating (Chen); diagnostics and tools at \~70–80% gross (Casdin).
- **Clinical screen: flag a sharp 3-month run-up into a known readout.** In Casdin's factor data, high-impact events 1–3 months out were negatively correlated with returns. Readout dates need the AI review.
- **AI review: payers, scheduling and the team.** Does payer coverage lag the volume (Natera; Buckley, Duquesne)? Could drug scheduling be lifted (Rosen)? Are clinical, regulatory, finance and commercial seats filled (Casdin)?

### 4. Add a valuation stage after the quality screen

The screen deliberately ignores price, but almost every investor here separates a great business from a great investment. This needs share prices, which SEC filings don't provide.

- **Earnings-yield floor:** EBIT ÷ EV at least the higher of the 10-year bond yield and 6% (Greenblatt), or a starting P/E around 15x or below with no multiple expansion assumed (Davis).
- **Reverse DCF:** flag prices that need \~20%+ growth for 10 years at >50% margins, which only \~0.1% of companies have achieved (Davis).
- **Magic-formula cross-check:** rank on EBIT/EV plus EBIT/tangible capital, excluding financials (Greenblatt).
- **Forward lens for fast growers:** P/E on earnings 2–4 years out, because S-curve winners "may not screen as the cheapest" (Sacerdote, Shaulov). This needs analyst estimates.
- **Own-history multiple:** flag quality companies whose P/E sits near the bottom of its own long range while EPS has grown. J&J at \~12x in early 2011 had been seen about five times in 60 years (Watsa).
- **Net-cash and asset floors:** show market cap ÷ net cash on every screen, not only clinical. A price below cash is the extreme margin of safety (Watsa). For hard-asset owners, compare market cap with net PP&E plus net cash (Alfred).
- **Deceleration check:** flag high multiples where the latest year's growth is under half the 3-year CAGR (Cohen, May 2025). Exempt the most durable businesses, as Costco shows (Sundheim, May 2021).

### 5. Move to the AI review (Stage 2): what numbers can't show

Speakers repeatedly named things a financial screen can't capture. These belong in the moat rubric that Claude scores from the 10-K.

- **Moat durability and switching costs:** "you can't screen for switching costs" (Dorsey); durability is "a much more ... qualitative sort of a thing" (Thiel).
- **Management and alignment:** insider ownership ≥ \~5% in small caps (Haley), long-dated pay vesting (Davis), and stated hurdle rates for buybacks and M&A (Haley, Dorsey). Is it an owner-operator or an "agent-operator" paid "often independent of the long-term value of the shares" (Stahl)?
- **AI disruption exposure:** seat-based pricing, reliance on annual price rises and AI revenue ≤1–2% of sales (Sacerdote); enabler, user, insulated or "walking dead" (Davis). Early tells: high price with low usage (Nadella), slipping net retention (Bravo) and price rises without audience growth (Marques).
- **Temporary or permanent?** The key question for any cheap, troubled name (Greenblatt). For a cyclical, also ask trough or midcycle (Einhorn on Solvay, Apr 2024).
- **Why is this mispriced?** "The most important question in the investment process" (Haley). Pair it with Davis's pre-mortem: why would this holding "dramatically underperform" over five years?
- **Read the segment note.** Is the bear case about one segment? Solvay's sell side wrote about soda ash though \~70% of revenue came from other lines (Einhorn). Is a trailing loss from a business being exited, as at Zillow (Sandler)?
- **Hidden and dormant assets:** land carried at no value ("Property, no value assigned"), unexplored acreage and non-producing royalties. A royalty is "a call option on everything" (Stahl).
- **Macro and policy exposure:** does demand rely on lower-income consumers squeezed by fuel and food inflation, or on tariff-exposed supply chains? Do very low tax rates or dominant positions face tax rises and antitrust (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022, May 2025)?
- **Financing quality:** does revenue rely on customers the company finances or owns equity in? Vendor financing in "exotic forms" needs scrutiny (Nadella). Treat private-credit debt as equity-like risk (Karniol-Tambour, May 2025).

## Investment strategies & how to think about markets

The fundamental investors converge on a simple recipe: own a few businesses with durable barriers to entry and pricing power, run by humble, aligned managers, for a very long time, and do the research of an investigative journalist. They differ mainly on concentration and on how much weight management deserves.

### 1. Quality means durable barriers to entry

- **Barriers beat growth.** "Growth without barriers is worthless" (Hohn). Competition and disruption are "the whole thing," because most of a company's NPV lies 20–30 years out (Hohn).
- **Growth × predictability.** Fast but unpredictable (young software) and predictable but slow (Nestlé) both underperform. Test: would you hold it if the market closed for 10 years? (Kantesaria)
- **Prefer a narrower moat with a growth runway** over the widest moat with nowhere to reinvest, like a saturated eBay (Dorsey).
- **Good companies stay good and bad companies stay bad,** but only if you judged quality right in the first place (Hohn).
- **Duopolies need rational competitors.** Boeing/Airbus, UPS/FedEx and potash fail the test (Kantesaria). The ideal is weak, rational, "apparent" competition (Hohn).
- **Returns converge on return on capital.** A business earning 6% on capital for 40 years returns \~6%, even if bought at a huge discount (Terry Smith, via Munger). At 4x book, a 20% ROE fully reinvested lifts a 5% yield on cost to \~10% in four years (Davis).
- **Three tests of quality.** A high return on capital in cash (cash delivered ÷ capital employed; \~25% across his portfolio), a secular runway to reinvest it, and a moat against mean reversion. High returns "landlocked" in a small niche must be handed back (Terry Smith).
- **Growth that needs no capital is worth more.** Coke reinvested \~20% of earnings to grow 12%. Moody's had \~$50M of PP&E and prepaying customers, so each dollar of its earnings was worth more (Goldstein, Greenblatt).
- **Know when ROIC misleads.** Where sales, marketing and engineering are expensed, "investment also happens on the income statement" and ROIC is "frankly meaningless". A falling ROIC can then signal a brighter future (Dorsey).
- **Industry structure drives about half the outcome.** Best is monopoly, then oligopoly, then a fragmented industry you can consolidate; worst is a sub-scale player where a few dominate (Haley). Distrust company-defined markets: monopolists inflate them, also-rans shrink them (Thiel).
- **Two simple quality screens.** Pre-tax return on capital = EBIT ÷ (net working capital + net fixed assets): 14% is "generally that's not good", good starts above 20% (Greenblatt). For pre-profit small caps, use gross profit ÷ tangible assets (Haley).

### 2. Pricing power is the tell

- Pricing 1% above inflation at a 20% margin grows profit \~5% faster than revenue, and price-led growth beats volume growth (Hohn).
- The ideal is an essential, low-cost product that can raise prices a few points above inflation for decades, but never use price to mask volume decline (Kantesaria). Classifieds raising price "5% a year forever even if there's no audience growth" now face AI disintermediation (Marques, May 2026).
- Abusing pricing power enlarges the profit pool and invites rivals: "if you take 5 in price, add at least 3 in value" (Dorsey). Managers often don't understand their own latent pricing power (Hohn).
- In inflation, own businesses whose pricing power passes on costs and protects a high ROIC (Haley, May 2022). Firms selling to the bottom 40–60% of earners struggle to pass on fuel and food inflation (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022).
- Fees that are small against what's at stake hold up: big issuers need both Moody's and S&P, so fees aren't very negotiable (Goldstein).
- Monopoly's financial signature is very high margins and cash piling up "year after year" (Thiel).
- A dominant rival's price cut can wreck an industry: Blockbuster dropping late fees cut gross profit per rental from $2.92 to about $1.69 (Gaines).
- Freight can make a "commodity" price like a franchise. Soda ash held \~$250/t in Western Europe and \~$180/t in the US over 2010–21, and Solvay got positive net pricing in inflationary 2023 (Einhorn).
- Sold-out capacity with long lead times compounds price. Carpenter Technology's prices rose 40% in two years, lifting EPS from \~$4.20 toward \~$10, "almost all of that is price". Its alloy is "maybe only 30 bips" of GE's cost (Advent, Apr 2024).

### 3. Management and capital allocation

- Dorsey moved from \~70/30 moat/management to \~30/70. He screens for humility with questions like "What's a do-over?" and "Which direct report would you least want to lose?" Red flags: "I" instead of "we," and moving HQ somewhere sunny.
- Hohn: a great manager can't fix a bad business, and arrogance is a killer. Fairfax turns away arrogant people however good they are (Watsa).
- Capital allocation is rare. Buybacks are neutral at best and M&A is mostly poor (Dorsey). Avoid serial acquirers and diworsification; the ideal company holds little cash (Kantesaria).
- Beware the "messiah" founder (Gaonkar). Founders aren't inherently better (Dorsey). Polished English made executives *seem* trustworthy, yet their stocks did worse (Xin).
- Alignment is a hard rule: "we don't invest in a single company where ... the incentives of management aren't aligned" (Davis). Stahl's owner-operators run a company "because they own more equity than anyone else".
- Ownership tests: small-cap managers should own at least \~5%, paid mostly in stock (Haley). A stock double should pay managers \~10x their annual cash pay (Goldstein). Exxon's executive pay vests 10 years after retirement (Davis).
- Ask how management chooses between projects, buybacks and M&A. A CFO who can't name a metric or hurdle is a red flag (Haley).
- Over a 5–10-year horizon "businesses are just people"; a CEO's clarity in writing (Bezos's 1997 letter) is a telltale (Sundheim).
- Boards are overrated in \~95% of cases, but a bad board can destroy a company; directors owning no stock is the bigger conflict (Davis).
- Keep the leaders who built it: "The reason they got to 2 billion in ARR is they did something right." Thoma Bravo's 2021 Medallia buyout swapped in an operator and "lost the innovative capability" (Bravo, May 2026).

### 4. Hold for a long time

- TCI's average holding period is \~8 years versus <1 year for typical institutions. Intrinsic-value compounding outweighs the multiple paid: Moody's was bought at 10x in the financial crisis and is \~$400–500 today (Hohn).
- Don't sell rare compounders: deferred tax is an interest-free loan (Li Lu). "Our favorite holding period is as long as the thesis holds" (Dorsey).
- Low turnover isn't complacency: keep re-ranking holdings against the watchlist (Kantesaria). "Do you need to change your wife every year?" (Hohn)
- Valuation sales are the classic mistake. Davis sold Costco bought at \~14x at 18–27x; it compounded on. Gotham sold Moody's up \~50%, then it rose 6–7x (Davis, Goldstein). "The biggest mistakes are selling the Costcos too early" (Sundheim).
- Fair value is a broad range; inside it, do nothing (Davis). Sell when the company stops executing, not at a price target (Haley, 2019).
- Li Lu's four reasons to sell: a realized mistake, a clearly better risk-reward, bubble-level valuation, or forced redemptions.
- Give value three to five years to be recognized, sometimes longer. Fairfax's 25% compound book-value growth included long stretches of only 3–4% (Watsa).
- Let appreciation pick the biggest positions: "The only way you achieve it is to leave it alone." Stahl held Texas Pacific Land from 1984 until his death in 2026.
- Switching compounds error. A PM right 70% of the time who sells A to buy B has 0.7 × 0.7 = 0.49 odds, "black versus red" (Stahl).

### 5. Valuation: roughly right beats precisely wrong

- Overpaying 10–15% hardly matters for a true compounder, and forward multiples mislead for special situations (Kantesaria). Ask whether a $10 stock is worth "a lot more than ten dollars", not $11 or $12 (Einhorn).
- Use multiples first and a DCF as a cross-check. DCFs undervalue firms that "beat the fade" back to the cost of capital (Dorsey).
- Think in whole-company numbers and do the maths mentally, about 5 minutes a page (Li Lu). Use free-cash-flow multiples and expense stock-based pay (Gaonkar).
- Value = incremental return on capital × the capital that can be deployed at that return (Gaonkar).
- Invert the P/E for a starting return and assume no rerating; 14–15x is "fine" (Davis).
- Reverse-engineer the price: some of the Magnificent Seven needed \~20% growth for a decade at >50% margins, achieved by \~0.1% of S&P 500 companies (Davis, Jul 2025).
- Use EV and pre-tax earnings, with EBIT = EBITDA − maintenance capex; EBITDA means nothing unless capex is \~zero (Greenblatt).
- Never discount at less than 6%, even with a 4% 10-year bond: 10x EBIT is \~6% after tax (Greenblatt, 2005).
- Normalize earnings and check that a low P/E isn't a cyclical peak (Greenblatt, Li Lu). Look through GAAP: Amazon reported losses yet produced cumulative free cash flow (Davis).
- The other side: most of the money comes from re-rating when doubts about a business model prove wrong; Priceline grew 40% a year at 9x earnings (Sundheim).

### 6. Research is investigative journalism

- Li Lu reads Value Line page by page and read every court filing on Timberland. His checklist: Is it cheap? A good business? Trustworthy management? What did I miss? Why is this being offered to me?
- Primary research methods:
  - building a dropshipping site to understand Shopify (Xin);
  - 100–120 management meetings a year (Pilecki);
  - niche trade shows and surveys of AI developers in non-tech industries (Gaonkar);
  - decade-long relationships with executives (Xin).
- Attach each thesis to an unbiased tracking data point, and spend as much time on what consensus could get wrong (Gaonkar).
- "Social arbitrage": spot cultural change, translate it into economics, find the expectation gap and time it before institutions notice (Camillo).
- **Invest, then investigate.** Take a meaningful but modest starter and give analysts 2–3 weeks; a wrong idea exits near flat (Druckenmiller). D1 starts buying before its memo is done (Sundheim).
- Alta Fox speaks with at least 15 CEOs or CFOs a week, and asks operators where they'd invest their own money (Haley).
- Qualitative work is the edge because quantitative data is crowded: talk to customers, ex-employees and ex-salespeople (Dorsey).
- At least 80% of the time the work shows a stock isn't cheap enough; it still pays when a stock later gets hit (Greenblatt).
- Study the stock, not only the business. Eminence spends 20–30% of research time on the "setup": the narrative, who holds and trades it, and what sell-side clients ask (Sandler, Eminence).

### 7. Where opportunities come from

- **Neglect.** About two-thirds of Kantesaria's entries are quality sitting in plain sight. Generalists ignore financials (Pilecki). Timberland had zero analyst coverage (Li Lu).
- **Positive change the market under-reacts to** gives you time to buy with confirmation (Pilecki).
- **Big change with a wide spread of outcomes,** where research can produce a differentiated view (Xin). The same idea from the quant side: Paddock trades paradigm shifts.
- **Crises.** In late 2008 "everything got cheap" (Singer), with good companies at 3–4x earnings (Watsa). Crises are great if you aren't forced to sell (Lebron). Crisis alpha arrives roughly every two years (Chan).
- **Misunderstood issues:** about a third of Kantesaria's entry points.
- **Situations the market can't easily value:** a new CEO or business line, complex segments, spin-offs, post-bankruptcy equity, IPOs, no comps. Combinations are best (Haley).
- **Durable franchises priced as fragile:** banks at 10–12x against utilities at \~20x in late 2024 (Davis).
- **Profitable small caps with operating leverage.** Of Alta Fox's 104 multibaggers (2015–20), 88% were EBITDA-positive five years earlier, with mid-40s gross margins and \~10% EBITDA margins, from \~10x forward EBITDA (Haley).
- **Temporary problems in cheap stocks.** Ask "temporary problem, or is it permanent?" (Greenblatt, via Pzena).
- **A shareholder-base gap.** Teva at 6x earnings was pivoting to biosimilars under a new CEO; value holders sold before growth investors arrived, and it re-rated to \~12x (Druckenmiller, 2025–26).

### 8. Activism and engagement

- Activism exists because index funds hold a plurality of equity and fewer investors act like owners. The best setup is an "open door," and \~70% of Elliott campaigns lead to meaningful action. "We're dealmakers, not crusaders" (Singer).
- Hohn has moved from hardcore activism to engagement: "the business always wins," and passive holders don't vote.
- Short-term activism can be destructive: activists would have fired Bezos in 2002, a "sugar high" (Davis). Activism matters more as passive funds stay silent (Haley).
- Friendly owners get deals. Fairfax may own 20–30% of a company but never goes hostile, and "companies want to do business with us" (Watsa). Einhorn says pushing an agenda is "very rare".
- Governments can do the activist's job. Korea's Value Up program forces governance reviews, buybacks and dividends, "mirror\[ing\] the experience that happened in Japan" (Marques, May 2026).

### 9. Indexing, hedge funds and retail

- "99.9% of active management is random noise minus fees"; most people should index (Kantesaria).
- Hedge funds sell uncorrelated, beta-stripped returns, so judging a market-neutral fund against the S&P is a category error (Tom). Retail's edge is freedom from LPs and benchmarks (Camillo).
- Pod shops: their virtue is that they can't stay wrong, but at \~4–6.5% pass-through fees and with crowded positions they create "air pockets" in sell-offs (Orr, Zach). Concentrated, long-term directional bets remain one of the few ways to get very rich (Matei). Pods cut risk when a PM is down "more than 3%," so "they're selling stocks when I'm buying them" (Mike Alfred, Alpine Fox).
- For most savers the "behavior penalty" of buying after rises and selling after falls swamps any stock-picking gain (Davis).
- For those who can't value companies, Greenblatt suggests \~30 magic-formula stocks held through long lagging stretches.
- Markets got less efficient as passives, retail, quants and multi-managers replaced mutual funds; short-term moves overshoot intrinsic value (Sundheim).

### 10. Special situations: get paid for complexity

- **Breakups stay mispriced even on the front page.** Analysts stop covering "until the smoke is cleared" and fund managers skip messy names. After Barron's laid out Sears's sum of the parts in 1993, Greenblatt's \~$5 stub reached $30 in two months (Greenblatt).
- **Value the pieces, not their quotes.** "Tear apart the company" conservatively: Sara Lee at $23 against $36 of parts was worth pursuing, against $27 it wasn't (Greenblatt). A listed stake worth \~80% of NAV can halve (Davis).
- **Read corporate-speak.** Sears's "no intention of disposing" of Allstate meant it would. Parents sell ≤20% before a tax-free spin, usually because the unit "didn't work out" (Greenblatt).
- **Use options only when you know something.** A call is stock plus a put plus a loan, so an in-the-money LEAP is a stock substitute. On Pzena's Lear thesis an $8.20 call could become $25–35 (Greenblatt, Oct 2005).
- **A distressed bond is a way to buy the equity.** Grupo TMM notes at 60 cents implied \~5x EBITDA for a railroad a strategic buyer valued at >8x, and doubled in \~18 months (Matt Mark, Jet Capital).
- **Good formulas lag for years.** Ranking on EBIT/EV and EBIT/tangible capital made 22.8% a year vs 12% for the top 1,000 stocks (1988–2004/05), yet lagged in \~6.5–8 of 18 years (Greenblatt).
- **Bonds covered by cash have a floor.** Nortel bonds at 40 cents, with \~$4B of cash against \~$4B of bonds, went to 100 cents. Fairfax bought again near 20 cents on a 40–50-cent recovery estimate (Watsa).

### 11. Short only when something is breaking, and size shorts for squeezes

- Overvaluation alone isn't a short: "we've always insisted on over valuation combined with some kind of deterioration" (Einhorn).
- Einhorn's exception was a "bubble basket" (from \~2014–15): 40–50 small shorts in profitless firms valued \~90% below price from their statements alone. He exits any name "showing real profits".
- Lookalike shorts don't hedge. In the 1998 Russian crisis Sandler's small- and mid-cap longs at Fusion fell while his large-cap lookalike shorts didn't. Eminence now shorts meme valuations, earnings misses, share losers, "melting ice cubes" and frauds.
- Volatility is asymmetric: a falling long shrinks, but on a losing short "leverage goes up" (Sundheim, May 2021). Trimming after a non-fundamental squeeze means "locking in negative alpha" (Sandler).
- Pre-size, then let shorts float. None may exceed 2% of capital. A $3–4B stock with 15% short interest in Reddit's top 50 gets at most 85bp, sized to survive a 100% squeeze (Sandler).
- AI may make quality compounders the best shorts: "the best short ideas are actually high quality compounding great businesses" (Marques, Pertento, May 2026). Their "service level deflation" is "not visible today in the near-term earnings".
- Shorting is a "negative carry business" (Alfred), so pay for it. In 2014 Eminence's analyst lists ran five longs to one short. It made its most skeptical analyst a dedicated short seller and double-weighted short alpha in pay (Sandler).

### 12. Index rules and short horizons misprice what patient owners can hold

- Float weighting buys more as insiders sell, "an algorithm for doing the opposite of what everyone knows is the sensible action." Index money also has no sell price: at "a million times earnings, the money would still come" (Stahl).
- Owner-operators earned "the bulk of the return" in the S&P 500 back to 1957, yet carry the lowest valuations: "It shouldn't exist, yet it does" (Stahl).
- Go where indexes can't. Dundee traded at \~C$1.40 against $10 of stated NAV: "It's too small to be in an index." Index capacity is "limited by the least liquid member" (Stahl).
- Price moves mean less. Passive's share of trading went from \~25% to \~50% by 2019 and "60 now"; "price action is much more meaningless than we used to think" (Sandler, Oct 2024).
- Quants bucket on trailing numbers. Zillow sat in "unprofitable growth" after exiting home flipping, but "what quants can't do is look forward" (Sandler).
- Non-AI stocks became a "source of funds" for AI and meme rallies (Sohn 2026 panel). Falling value stocks have "literally no buyers" (Rosen, Rubric); Reddit fell \~50% in an "AI loser" basket while its posts grew (Lennon, Pleasant Lake).
- The value analysts are gone. Passive replaced "the thousands of people" paid to find cheap stocks, so fine companies with double-digit cash-on-cash returns are more abundant "than at any time in my career" outside bear-market bottoms (Einhorn, Apr 2024).
- Time arbitrage is "one of the best inefficiencies in the market": most investors skip stocks that won't rise within six months (Einhorn). Calendar-year grading undervalues the long run (Stahl).
- He couldn't time the end. In Apr 2016 indexation was "in the late innings"; by Sep 2018 "I have no insight" (Stahl).

## Macro & markets

For equity investors, macro matters mainly through liquidity and the cost of money, which move valuations and decide which earnings survive. Druckenmiller reads it from inside the market rather than from economists' data; Karen Karniol-Tambour (Bridgewater) reads it from who funds spending, and Rob Citrone (Discovery Capital) from each country's policies. The stock pickers (Li Lu, Sundheim, Davis) don't forecast macro; they make sure their holdings can survive it. Two exceptions, Einhorn and Stahl, argue that debt and the end of disinflation favor gold and hard assets.

### 1. Liquidity and the Fed drive markets more than earnings

- "It's never been about politics, it's always about liquidity." What matters is "not even the level, it's the rate of change" (Druckenmiller, 2018).
- His 2018 gauges: central-bank buying falling from \~$1tn a year to zero, and M2 growth slowing from \~7.5% to 4% (Druckenmiller).
- Zero rates remove capitalism's hurdle rate, so "of course debt is going to explode." From 2010 to 2018 corporate debt rose \~60%, corporate earnings only 27% (Druckenmiller).
- "when you had 11 years of free money ... people do stupid things," and "6-month bear markets preceded by asset bubbles don't exist historically" (Druckenmiller, 2022–23).
- Greenspan's late-1990s refusal to hike was "the original sin." A 1% rate with 9% nominal growth in 2003 seeded the GFC (Druckenmiller).
- Non-bank lending makes policy harder to read. Still, when the Fed is "slamming the brakes on," as in 2022 (1% to 4%), worry about equity exposure (van Eck).
- The Fed kept "their foot on the gas" through 2021, buying $120bn of bonds a month at zero rates, then hiked 500bp in a year. Soft landings since 1950 all followed "Proactive rather than reactive" policy (Druckenmiller, May 2023).

### 2. Read the economy from inside the stock market

- "the best economists I know is the inside of the stock market." Stocks lead fundamentals by 6–12 months; housing and retail lead, capital goods lag (Druckenmiller).
- Dec 2018: the S&P was down only 13%, but autos fell 30% and homebuilders 35% while utilities, staples and pharma rose. He called it "amber" (Druckenmiller).
- Jun 2022: homebuilders were −50% and trucking −40% from their highs, trucking on record earnings. That warned of trouble 6–12 months out (Druckenmiller).
- Credit "tends to lead the economy": in late 2018 GE's CDS went from 50 to 200bp. Unemployment lags, and "most recessions were preceded by a very low unemployment rate" (Druckenmiller).
- His old signals are fading. QE switched off the 10-year, "the most important price in the world," and technical analysis is "about 20% as effective" (Druckenmiller, 2022–26).
- May 2023: trucking, "a Guiding Light for my firms" with a 6–8-month lead, was "extremely weak", and bankruptcies were "astonishingly low". He expected "a lot more bodies coming" (Druckenmiller).

### 3. As a stock picker, accept the macro and own earnings that survive it

- "The macro is what we must accept. The micro is where we can and should make a difference." With no leverage, a 50% drop can't force a sale (Li Lu, Dec 2024).
- Sundheim (D1) doesn't trade macro: it's "a binary bet," and a short-Treasuries trade "might never be right." Davis (Davis Advisors) never goes to cash; he buys "what people aren't interested in."
- Earnings that grow through a recession protect the stock: "if Staples can go up in price in a recession, why can't a company like Nvidia" (Druckenmiller, Jun 2023).
- In a bear market good earnings don't save expensive stocks. In 2018 Alibaba beat every estimate yet fell from 210 to 165, and cloud names de-rated from 9x to 7x sales (Druckenmiller).
- Avoid longs that need a macro outcome: "why in the world would you buy a group that needs rates to go up to own it" (banks, Druckenmiller, Dec 2018).
- In a hard landing profits normally fall 40–50%; he expected only 20–30% in 2023 because the recession was "so anticipated" (Druckenmiller, Jun 2023). His May 2023 definition: profits down "20 percent plus", unemployment from 3.4% to above 5%, and rising bankruptcies.

### 4. Rotate with the cycle, and know when to stand aside

- Buy an industry that is losing money and shutting capacity; beware one announcing expansions. Margins mean-revert within \~3 years (Druckenmiller).
- COVID "over-earners" (brick-and-mortar retail, trucking, then ocean shipping) gave him the best shorting period of his career (Druckenmiller, Jun 2022).
- "The present is not what moves stock prices. Change moves them." Picture the world 18–24 months out (Druckenmiller).
- When AI got "disturbingly heated" in late 2025, he rotated into biotech, down \~4 years with momentum turning. Teva re-rated from 6x to \~12x (Druckenmiller).
- Narrowing leadership is "a yellow light": no bear market has started without it (Druckenmiller, Oct 2024). In mid-2023 the S&P rested on 7 stocks (Gerber, Hudson Bay).
- "My record is as much knowing not when to play as when to play." He saw no fat pitch in Jun 2023 (Druckenmiller). Crises are where returns are made (Li Lu).
- Plan the way out before the downturn. His May 2023 list for after a hard landing was copper, US housing, biotech and AI. He held off copper, since cyclicals fall as a hard landing starts (Druckenmiller).

### 5. Inflation, rates, deficits and debt

- Two "undefeated records" once inflation tops 5%: it has never fallen unless Fed funds exceeded CPI, nor without a recession (Druckenmiller, Jun 2022). In May 2022 real rates were "at the most negative level in the last 70 years" (Einhorn).
- The 1970s template: inflation fell from \~8% to 3%, then rose again. Bonds ignored it until it reached 12%, then "responded in spades" (Druckenmiller, Oct 2024).
- The 10-year can trade to nominal GDP: 6–7% if inflation runs 4–4.5% with 2.5–3% real growth, versus \~4.5% then (Druckenmiller, Oct 2024).
- Deficits of \~7% of GDP at full employment "can't last forever." His guessed trigger is "a failed auction," in "6 months... six years" (Druckenmiller, Oct 2024). In May 2023 he called entitlements and interest a "200 foot tsunami"; the reserve currency's curse is that "markets don't check you".
- Deficits of 6% of GDP are "two feet on the gas pedal." The 40-year fall in rates is over, so long backtests carry a falling-rate bias (van Eck, Jul 2026).
- The 10-year has been below 3% in only two periods since 1871. Shadow-bank leverage breaks if long rates stay higher, starting with commercial real estate (Gerber, Jun 2023). The median regional bank had 43% of loans in commercial real estate, \~40% of that office (Druckenmiller, May 2023).

### 6. The supports behind record margins and low inflation may be reversing (2022–26)

- "everyone's spending is somebody's income," so economy-wide margins grow only when spending is funded other than by wages: borrowing, dissaving or deficits. With COVID checks, "at the same time the economy collapsed corporate profits actually surged to new highs" (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022).
- By Jun 2022 those supports were reversing: "we've already globalized every good we could globalize." After "the peak pro corporate environment", expect higher taxes, more antitrust and wage pressure (Karniol-Tambour).
- Profits alone don't make equity returns. In the 1970s commodity producers were \~30% of the market, so profits "did great", yet stocks had their "worst decade ever"; producers are \~3% now (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022).
- Disinflation rested on "two and a half Miracles": dumped Soviet resources, \~3bn new low-cost workers and multinationals entering newly opened countries. "Those are the Miracles and that's over," so inflation "can last decades" (Stahl, Oct 2024).
- There's no Volcker exit, Stahl argued: private equity is "essentially leverage Equity", and bank balance sheets are "many orders of magnitude bigger" than in 1982.
- "the fed is bluffing" (Einhorn, May 2022). With $24tn of public debt and \~$7tn rolling within a year, each 1% adds \~$70bn to the deficit, so "it has to pick the treasury." "At that point it's best to have some gold."
- Underinvestment makes old-economy profits stick: "higher prices aren't fueling new capacity instead they're fueling buybacks." Einhorn called cement, steel, chemicals, mining and energy "attractive long investments" (May 2022); by Apr 2024 soda ash's excess profits were "normalizing".
- The S&P 500 holds "as a practical matter no tangible Assets," and rebalancing toward them will be "very disruptive in an equity sense." AI capex means "we're returning... to the world of hard or tangible... assets" (Stahl, 2024–26).

### 7. Currencies, gold, bitcoin and real assets

- A peg between diverging economies is "a one-way bet" with cheap carry: the 1992 pound short cost 0.5% for six months. He passed on Brexit, a ±10% coin flip (Druckenmiller).
- In early 2026 he was short the dollar: near the top of its purchasing-power range, with foreigners "way, way overloaded" (Druckenmiller). Karniol-Tambour was short the dollar against the euro and yen in May 2025.
- Gold is "mainly a geopolitical trade" (Druckenmiller, 2026). It is van Eck's preferred hedge for US spending, but in a loss-of-confidence liquidation "nothing's going up, even gold."
- Own Bitcoin in an inflationary bull market and gold in a bear market or stagflation (Druckenmiller, 2022). Bitcoin is gold's immature "teenager" (van Eck).
- Hold 60–70% equities and \~10% real assets, with bonds the rest; real assets now diversify better (van Eck, 2026). Copper is "very tight for the next 8 years" (Druckenmiller, 2026).
- Gold is the reserve asset when trust breaks. Freezing Russia's reserves weaponized the dollar, yet gold's share of reserves was "staggeringly low": India 8%, China and Saudi Arabia 4% (Einhorn, May 2022).
- Stahl called Bitcoin "the most conservative investment there is": supply is capped at 21m, and each four-year halving doubles the cost of a coin. Gold isn't fixed: at $5,000/oz more comes out of mines and jewelry (2024–26).
- Alfred, a holder since 2017, expects $1m a coin "probably by 2033 or 2035", but "we could have a 60 or 80% draw down at some point." It fell from \~125k to \~60k by spring 2026.

### 8. China, Japan, India and the rest of the world

- "I was in love with China until about six or seven years ago." Under Xi he expects "a very undynamic economy" for 10–15 years (Druckenmiller, Jun 2023).
- A government that can "act capriciously" has "a humongous gravitational pull on valuations," so D1 left China around 2022 (Sundheim, Oct 2025).
- China's key variable is consumption: \~40% of GDP, versus 60% in India and >70% in the US. Supply-side stimulus can't fix a demand problem (Li Lu, Dec 2024).
- Japan was Druckenmiller's "biggest value trap in history," but by Jun 2023 it was embracing shareholder value. By early 2026 he held big Japan and Korea positions.
- India has a deregulation "green light" and the fastest growth in 2026, yet is underweighted in indices. Its rupee "is just not dollarized" (van Eck, Jul 2026).
- Tightening breaks emerging markets first: "that's kind of where it always starts" (Druckenmiller, 2018).
- Citrone looks at "countries like we look at companies": management and policies first. Latin America was "left for dead for the last 25 years"; his top pick, America Movil, offered a 10% FCF yield, 10% growth and 7–10% yearly buybacks (May 2025).
- Korea's discount may finally close with Japan-style Value Up reforms. On next year's consensus Samsung and SK Hynix are the world's two most profitable companies, at \~6–7x earnings (Marques, Lennon, May 2026). Lennon allowed it may be "the very... top."

### 9. The current regime (2025–26)

- Three forces drive markets: modern mercantilism, the AI build-out and record capital concentration in US equities. "We're all Brazil now" as fiscal limits bind. Go global; Europe is investable and China diversifies (Jensen, Bridgewater, Nov 2025).
- Markets are "about as risky as I've ever seen": leverage, deficits >6% of GDP, AI "over its skis" (Singer, Elliott, Feb 2025).
- A seven-person group's 12-month predictions were \~80% wrong, so build scenario planning and geopolitical capability instead. Be more circumspect on private equity; favor private credit only with cash yield (Temasek, Sep 2025).
- Post-crisis banks are well capitalized, so no crisis is imminent. Private credit's risks are hidden double leverage and zero recoveries on failed software loans (Pilecki, Apr 2026). It can carry "something much more similar to equity-like risk" (Karniol-Tambour, May 2025).
- AI spending is demand-pulled and power-constrained (Nadella, Oct 2025), so at worst expect price corrections, not contagion (Ullal, Dec 2025; Hoffman, Feb 2026). Debt-financed AI capex is "usually a bad leading indicator" (Sundheim, Oct 2025).
- The economy will get "much stronger" on fiscal stimulus, but valuations are "toward the top of the valuation range historically." "Macro has been dead for 10 or 15 years. I don't think that's the case anymore" (Druckenmiller, early 2026). The S&P is at 26x (Davis, May 2026), with semis and optical \~23% of it (Marques, May 2026).
- Cohen (Point72, May 2025) saw no recession yet but "significant slowing growth": \~45% recession odds and a 14–17% effective tariff rate. With high multiples he expected a sideways market: "Right now the market feels toppy."
- Citrone (May 2025) expected a 5–5.25% 10-year and no Fed cuts in 2025, then a 2026 boom on tax cuts and reshoring: "next year is going to surprise everybody."
- Lenders have repriced software. Refinancing leverage fell from \~7–7.5x to \~5x and spreads widened \~250bp plus fees; lenders now say "No, this is not software" (Bravo, May 2026).

### 10. Process lessons specific to macro investing

- Put big bets only in liquid markets (bonds, FX), so "you can change your mind when you're wrong" (Druckenmiller).
- Bet on asymmetry: in Dec 2018 he argued a wrong hike would cost "five to ten X" a wrong pause. Hedge the macro you don't want, as with short credit against long Treasuries (Druckenmiller).
- Being early costs as much as being wrong: his 2018 short was "three months early." Separate trade timing from thesis (Druckenmiller).
- "When I'm wrong I changed my mind." He faults the Fed's forward guidance because "you eliminate your optionality" (Druckenmiller, Oct 2024). Know your bias: "Bulls make more money than bears."
- General sizing lessons are in Risk, sizing & psychology.
- Swing only at fat pitches; otherwise "keep your gross low be open-minded." In May 2023 he ran \~60% gross and flat net, with Treasuries "off the table" at 3.5% against 5% fed funds (Druckenmiller).
- A thesis needs a time frame: "eventually is not a good time horizon for a hedge fund." Greenlight held no gold for years, until "eventually is now" (Einhorn, May 2022).

### 11. Implications for the screener

- **Rate resilience.** Check interest coverage at today's rates and debt maturing soon; termed-out corporate debt rolls over in 2025–26 (Druckenmiller). Flag high leverage with near-term refinancing (Gerber).
- **Real per-share growth.** Flag EPS growth driven by debt-funded buybacks or M&A: 2010–18 S&P EPS rose \~60% versus corporate profits +27% (Druckenmiller).
- **Recession earnings.** Score revenue and operating-income change in 2008–09 and 2020; sustainable earnings in a recession protect the stock (Druckenmiller).
- **Cyclical over-earning.** Flag margins far above long-run history (Druckenmiller), and check whether a low P/E reflects peak or one-off earnings (Li Lu). Be most wary where sales rely on transfers or consumer dissaving, as record 2022 margins did (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022).
- **Sector sensitivity.** Tag businesses that need higher rates (banks) or depend on office real estate (Druckenmiller, Gerber). High EV/Sales, high-beta names de-rate even without misses (Druckenmiller). A 1.5-point rise in the 10-year cuts the value of a 10-year earnings stream \~15% (Karniol-Tambour, Jun 2022).
- **Funding risk.** Cash-burning growth companies can suffer "a capital chain break" in a crisis (Li Lu, Dec 2024).
- **Market overlay.** Track cyclicals versus defensives, credit spreads and leadership breadth. Cut forward estimates when oil, the dollar and rates rise together (Druckenmiller).
- **Country filter.** Weight consumption share of GDP (Li Lu), discount markets without due process (Sundheim) and favor deregulating economies (van Eck). Einhorn invests only in developed markets, where insiders and local rules are easier to judge from New York.
- **Tangible assets and supply discipline.** The index holds almost no tangible assets; royalty and land owners need no reinvestment (Stahl). Favor industries returning cash instead of adding capacity (Einhorn, May 2022).

## Healthcare & biotech investing

The healthcare specialists agree that their edge is a better probability estimate than consensus, not a cheaper multiple: does the drug truly work, what is it worth if it does, and how do you hold it so a miss doesn't sink you? Generalist quality investors mostly skip clinical-stage biotech. Profitable pharma, medical and services businesses can pass quality filters, but only after checks the financial statements don't show: patent expiry, government pricing, royalties and financing needs.

| Investor | Firm and style | How they underwrite | Signature lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bihua Chen | Cormorant: \~$2.5B biotech long-short plus private funds | Causal biology, potency and the data room; \~4x peak sales | "Knowledge is different from judgment" |
| Jim Flynn | Deerfield: $16B across public equity, structured debt, venture and its own labs | Probability-weighted cash-flow models for every instrument | Fail cheap; protect the basis, keep the skew |
| Dave Johnson | Caligan: small/mid-cap life-sciences activist | Approved assets worth more than EV; pipeline as a call option; \~3:1 up/down | "we don't take a lot of scientific risk" |
| Eli Casdin | Casdin Capital: \~$3.5B public and private life sciences on long-duration capital | Scores management, market, return profile and risks; rides the falling cost of biology | "I'm comfortable missing the 30 so that I miss the 70" |
| Annie, Rod, Eli (GEF panel, Nov 2021; first names only) | Health-tech services; drug company creation; public-private life sciences | Payment reform, base rates, total system cost | "technology is not the issue when it comes to healthcare innovation" (Annie) |
| Druckenmiller | Generalist macro | Trusts a specialist team, times sector sentiment | You needn't understand the science |
| Kantesaria, Haley | Generalist quality | Exclude biotech | Unpredictable, outside "the generalist investor framework" |

### 1. Drug investing rewards specialist judgment, which is why most quality investors stay out

- Kantesaria avoids biotech as unpredictable despite 18 years in biotech VC. Alta Fox has little in biotech because it lacks "exceptional businesses with durable \[moats\] that we can understand" (Haley).
- The core question is whether a drug works, judged on incomplete information. "Every company will tell you something is causal, and most time it's not" (Chen, Cormorant).
- Credentials and networks aren't the edge. Baskets, university ties and backing a CEO's past success don't work, and a degree is "not sufficient or necessary" (Chen).
- "Science is just a language" that anyone can learn; the real skill is making good probabilistic assessments (Flynn, Deerfield).
- The generalist alternative: Druckenmiller made "a big move into biotech" (2025–26), though the science goes "right over Stan's head." He trusted a specialist team and judged how the market would embrace the change.
- Be "very skeptical" but not cynical: cynics miss huge winners through bias about who looks successful (Chen).

### 2. Value a pipeline as peak sales × a deal multiple × probability of success

- Long-term value is "actually easy": biotech trades at \~4x peak sales of its major drugs. Pharma pays 2–3x to 6–8x, depending on uniqueness, patent strength and exclusivity (Chen).
- Johnson's MorphoSys formula (Jun 2023): peak sales × \~2x precedent deal multiple × 78% probability of success (PoS) ÷ shares. That gave \~45/share against <13 paid.
- Build peak sales bottom up: monthly price × share of newly diagnosed patients × duration taken from the standard-of-care discontinuation curve (Johnson). Duration, patient count and value-justified price all lift peak sales (Chen).
- The edge is a different PoS. "If you think probability of success is 70% other people think it's only 30%," you make money if right (Chen). The sell side used 50–60% versus Caligan's 78% (Johnson).
- Deerfield uses "long-term cash flow based probabilistic models" for everything, "even long-term drug discovery." A genetic association doubles PoS, and antibodies double it again versus small molecules (Flynn).
- Respect base rates. \~90% of drug assets fail (Walmsley). Academic ideas fail \~95% of the time and half don't reproduce, so \~2.5% succeed (Flynn, 2021).
- Ex-Lilly and Novo, pharma trades at \~12x earnings because R&D ROI is \~zero: "The biggest cost is failure" (Ricks).
- Don't build a takeout into the value. Casdin's biggest mistake was overweighting M&A and sizing up: "maybe my valuation was right but the probability I should have assigned to it was much smaller."

### 3. Read clinical data as if the drug doesn't work

- Rank the hypothesis. Inherited genetics is strongest, single-driver cancers are strong, and picking the right cytokine in inflammation is where "you could really go wrong" (Chen). Genetics-linked diseases fall first; brain diseases are "big and complicated" (Flynn).
- Common traps: correlation taken as causation, too little potency in humans (resveratrol), and 99.999% protein binding. Storytelling won't find these; the data room will (Chen).
- Johnson's checks: is the Phase 2 confidence-interval lower bound (about 57%) above the standard of care (about 40%)? Is Phase 3 upsized, and did it enroll \~6 months early while a rival's ran on time? Add 3–4-year durability data and calls to trial doctors.
- There are "two kinds of risks": your drug fails, or a competitor's works better. Add-ons to standard of care must add efficacy without toxicity (Johnson).
- Read rivals' labels. Daxxify's six months was a median at double the dose, from a placebo trial rather than head-to-head (Johnson, Sep 2022).
- After a failure, ask whether the biology was wrong or the technology fell short; the latter becomes "an engineering problem." Distrust "retrospective signal finding" (Chen).

### 4. Structure exposure around binary events

- Buy a floor and get the binary cheaply. MorphoSys hit negative EV in Dec 2022: "you own this option for nothing." By Jun 2023, net cash after burn, approved products and up to $535M of milestones still covered >50% of the price (Johnson).
- Deerfield's favourite structure is senior secured debt plus warrants: one part "protects your basis," the other captures the skew (Flynn).
- Melinta shows why: when COVID hit, Deerfield credit-bid, ran it six years, doubled sales from $60M to $125M and sold for over $300M. A pure lender would have lost most of its money (Flynn).
- Venture biotech "had been bad for decades" because 90% lose, and lose a lot. Deerfield runs discovery at marginal cost and kills fast: "if you create a company around it it's going to want to live" (Flynn).
- The payoff: Nuvalent went from a $2M test-tube proof, funded alone, to \~$10B with two lung-cancer drugs filed (Flynn, Jun 2026).
- Trade the calendar, too. Stocks sit in a "catalyst desert" and wake up \~6 months before data (Johnson). Gerber's first off-floor strategy (1995) was Phase 3 FDA binaries with option overlays.
- Near-term catalysts are usually priced. In Casdin's factor data, high-impact events 1–3 months out were negatively correlated with returns: calling +10 cents was right, but "now it had to be 15 to beat."
- Look for catalysts nobody models. Viatris traded at 6–7x earnings, "100% converted to cash flow", while two drugs it bought from a forced seller, Idorsia, read out in 6–9 months. Idorsia held its trial data back for a journal, so "nobody is valuing this" (Rosen, Rubric, May 2026).
- Only Casdin described a sizing method: each idea is scored on management, market, return profile and risks, so the team knows "why something was 1%, why something else was 5%."

### 5. Financing and the biotech cycle drive short-term prices

- Biotechs are long-dated, cash-burning assets under financing pressure, so interest rates "influence biotech valuation a lot" (Chen).
- Flynn has seen three bubbles in 36 years. Each brings 1–2 years of doubling or tripling, then 1–2 years collapsing "back to the same point." In May 2025: "Things are cheap again."
- In bubbles "People accept companies that are too early, too risky, require too much cash," and they "just have to go away" (Flynn). For real winners, 2020 was a dip, not the price peak (Chen).
- After the 2022 capital drought, "capital allocation has never been more important": "focus on your winners starve your losers" (Johnson). Non-dilutive Pharmakon debt ($125M) funded Evolus to breakeven.
- Bubble-era zero-coupon converts later traded in the low 60s. Buying back at 72 earned a 22% IRR, and MorphoSys bought a quarter at 64 (Johnson).
- Business-model changes create mispricing. MorphoSys sold its 28-program royalty portfolio to Royalty Pharma for $1.4B and its European holders left. It became "a U.S biotech in a German shell" (Johnson).

### 6. Back complete teams, and fund them so they never run out of money

- Accept missing some good teams to avoid the bad ones. About 70% of the teams Casdin judges bad turn out bad, while \~30% he doubts create value anyway (Casdin, Jul 2022).
- Test management with a suggestion. "Oh, that's a good idea" is a red flag; "6 months ago we instituted a program to do that" reassures (Casdin).
- Fill every seat, not just the scientist: chemistry, trial design, regulatory, finance and commercial. "A's hire A+ people, and similarly B's hire C's and C's hire D's" (Casdin).
- Over-capitalize, then scale in from the inside rather than front-load and "fight dilution on the way down" (Casdin).
- Long-duration capital is his biggest edge: with LPs locked in he can "step in" during selloffs, which were "nine out of 10 times" unrelated to the industry (Casdin, mid-2022).
- Invest public and private together: "If you're only investing in public companies and not talking to private companies, you're only hearing half the story" (Casdin).

### 7. Pricing power is capped by patents, payers and politics

- Generics take "98% of volume within a month" of patent expiry (Flynn, 2026). A typical pharma patent cliff cuts revenue \~90% (Johnson). Pharma "replaces itself every decade" (Walmsley).
- Cliffs drive M&A. With \~$140B of pharma revenue going off patent in the late 2020s, boards must "justify not doing deals." The partner facing expiry is the natural buyer, e.g. Incyte with Jakafi in 2028 (Johnson).
- Most of pharma's top sellers were acquired from biotech, sometimes \~$10B pre-approval for a drug selling $5B a year (Chen). Pharma buys biotech \~6–12 months after selloffs (Silver, Maverick).
- Durability without patents exists. Botulinum toxins rely on trade-secret cell banks and avoid the cliff, at lower margins than small molecules (Johnson). Patents are Terry Smith's "least liked" moat because they expire.
- Government payers cap returns. Medicare/Medicaid-dependent companies see "they change the rules" if they earn too much, so demand a larger margin of safety (Greenblatt). Botox can't undercut its government price (Johnson).
- Jeuveau's aesthetics-only label escapes those rules, letting it run \~20% cheaper for patients, \~20% more profitable for practices, with loyalty rewards (Johnson, Sep 2022).
- Flynn is sanguine: "If someone comes up with something transformative, people pay for it," and drugs are only \~20% of costs. He conceded "abuses" in 2021, such as TNF prices that "inch up not down."
- Price risk is rising for me-too drugs: China has ten near-identical PD-1s at a tenth of US cost, "coming here" (Eli). Botched reform could push innovation abroad (Rod).
- Payer coverage can be the margin lever. Many Natera tests are "not getting paid anything at all" until coverage arrives, and >$1bn of R&D plus \~4x the nearest rival's publications won it. Buckley expected profits "nearly two years before consensus" (Duquesne, Apr 2024).

### 8. Where the industry is going

- Druckenmiller called obesity drugs "a razor blade business" because patients regain weight off the drug; he sold Lilly "in the high 700s" (Oct 2024). Chen says the obesity story is "only half written" (2026).
- AI in discovery is early. The first wave "did not deliver" and "the impact has not been felt yet" (Chen, 2026). It helps the earliest stage but not yet the slow, costly trials (Flynn).
- "Turn on a computer and invent drugs" is hype, though an AI fix lifted Lilly's GLP-1 output 8% (Ricks). Cures happen "in the analog world, in real people" (Ramakrishnan). Druckenmiller still calls biotech AI's best use case.
- Life-science tools are AI and reshoring winners "left for dead," with 3–5 consolidators buying $5–10B companies (Silver, Jun 2026).
- In care delivery, data is the moat, not software, which is "hard to invest in" (Flynn). Software will diagnose most people within 5–10 years (Flynn, May 2025).
- The bottleneck is payment, not technology: \~80% of care could happen at home, and hospital-owned primary care raises costs \~10% (Annie). Back products "better cheaper to the system" (Eli).
- Contrarian services trades work. Flynn bought nursing homes when "everyone hates this"; Brookdale occupancy rose from 64% to 84%. Davis owns UnitedHealth for the third time, after what "happened to" it in the year to May 2026, citing "massive mispricings."
- A genome took \~13 years and \~$3bn by 2003; now it takes a day and $500. Casdin argues cheaper biology raises success rates: "The lights are now on." He expected \~20% a year for a decade, but "buckle up because it's going to be very volatile" (Jul 2022).

### 9. What a financial-statement screen can and can't see

- Healthcare can pass quality tests. Pharma and health care are among sectors with persistently high ROIC over 1963–2004 and the latest 20 years (Smith). Healthcare was over-represented among Alta Fox's 2015–20 multibaggers (Haley).
- But Haley warns those winners may enjoy competitive dynamics that "will not persist," because regulation "greatly impacts the healthcare space."
- Clinical-stage biotech can't be screened: the edge is whether the drug works, and comps are the "less value added" part (Chen). Haley's positive-EBITDA filter removed it.
- Check commercial drug companies against pharma economics: 80–90% gross margin and up to \~50% operating margin. Operating margin depends on marketing needs and efficiency (Chen). Diagnostics and tools run \~70–80% gross margins, but "technology is not going to cover up crappy businesses" (Casdin).
- Low multiples can hide a transition. Teva was a "boring generic drug company" at 6x; a new CEO's biosimilars pivot took it from 16 to 32 and \~12x (Druckenmiller, 2025–26).

### 10. Implications for the screener

1. **Bucket pre-revenue biotech separately** rather than scoring it on quality metrics; a positive trailing EBITDA filter does this (Haley, Kantesaria, Chen).
2. **Flag patent-expiry exposure.** Treat revenue from drugs nearing expiry as \~90–98% at risk (Johnson, Flynn), and favour brands, installed base or trade secrets (Smith, Johnson).
3. **Haircut government-payer businesses.** Require a larger margin of safety where Medicare/Medicaid sets returns (Greenblatt).
4. **Red-flag price-led growth in old, crowded classes** (Flynn on TNF inhibitors) and me-too drugs in crowded mechanisms (Eli, Chen).
5. **Benchmark margins by subsector:** drugs at 80–90% gross and toward \~50% operating (Chen); diagnostics and tools at \~70–80% gross (Casdin).
6. **Check runway to the next catalyst.** Net cash must stay positive after burn (Johnson); flag bubble-vintage, cash-hungry IPOs (Flynn).
7. **Screen for a value floor:** EV below net cash plus approved products, royalties and milestones, ideally negative EV, then demand \~3:1 (Johnson).
8. **Track contractual catalysts:** royalty step-downs (+1,000bps at Evolus), regulatory milestones and discounted converts at cash-rich issuers (Johnson). Add payer coverage and drug descheduling (Buckley, Rosen), and fade sharp run-ups into known readouts (Casdin).
9. **Don't buy fallen biotechs mechanically:** \~8 of 10 post-data collapses are justified (Chen).
10. **Add a sector-cycle overlay:** after a 1–2-year collapse to pre-bubble levels "Things are cheap again" (Flynn); Druckenmiller entered after a \~4-year slump as momentum turned.

## Quantitative & systematic strategies

The quants agree that skepticism matters more than cleverness. Every signal needs an economic story, you should diversify across many small, independent edges, and your capital base and risk control decide whether you survive long enough for the edge to pay.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Market making / HFT | Seconds–days | Lebron (Jane Street), ex-IMC trader | Seeing order flow; speed; plugging every exploitable hole |
| Trend, carry and risk-premia futures | Weeks–months | Carver, Gebhardt | Many rules across 200+ markets; harvesting risk premia |
| Multi-market diversified risk premia | Months–years | Quantedge | \~300 markets lets \~25% volatility run without ruin |
| Crisis alpha | Event-driven | Ernie Chan | Option-like payoff in crashes (roughly every 2 years) |
| Merger arbitrage | Deal life | Versor | Predicting deal bumps, not just failures |
| Alternative data / NLP equity | Days–months | Jha, Kantos | Data others don't have, trained on the right labels |
| Alpha capture (structured human opinion) | Days–weeks | Cedar (Two Sigma) | Trading *changes* in expert opinion |
| Power trading | Intraday–seasonal | Paddock | Obscure market rules plus paradigm-shift views |
| Niche relative-value multi-manager | Varies | Zach | Capacity-constrained PMs the big pods can't use |
| Event and catalyst arbitrage, hedged per idea | Event-driven | Gerber (Hudson Bay) | Each idea hedged for its own beta; limited loss, independent, slight edge |
| Global multi-manager platform | Varies | Tolkin (Schonfeld) | Scale pays for technology, data, overseas reach and talent |

### 1. Skepticism is the core skill

- "Nearly every error I've seen was overconfidence": trusting backtests, assuming clean data, lookahead bias (Carver). Backtests mostly convince yourself; investors believe only live records (Gebhardt).
- The worst danger is *implicit* fitting: swapping models after seeing the full backtest. Push model choice into an explicit, evidence-weighted process. "Better to fail than to overfit" (Carver).
- Repeated tweaking against out-of-sample data turns it in-sample: a Sharpe of 1.5 in backtest becomes 0.5 live. Use the deflated Sharpe ratio and try to falsify your hypothesis (Chan).
- Most "hockey-stick" backtests hide a flaw in transaction costs or distribution assumptions (Kantos). Overfitted 20-variable backtests are "a work of fiction" (Tom).
- Historical correlation isn't predictive. Only market and sector factors matter: the 21 smaller factors explain \~6% of moves, dangerous once levered (Gerber).

### 2. Every signal needs an economic story

- Never run a black box: if you can't explain a signal simply, it will probably decay. PMs must be able to explain each day's trades (Quantedge).
- Be "empirical market scientists": write the hypothesis and causal mechanism first, then find data. "Humans are incredibly good at finding patterns where they don't exist" (Versor).
- Start from a philosophy of how prices move. "Full flexibility finds amazing systems that don't last" (Gebhardt).
- Survivors run many signals, each with economic intuition, as an evolving process (Jha).

### 3. Diversify across many small, independent edges

- Information ratio ≈ skill × √(number of independent bets). Hundreds of markets lower idiosyncratic risk enough to run high volatility (Quantedge).
- Spread across many trading rules so that no single decaying rule hurts much (Carver). Keep thin or contradictory data as a running experiment with no obligation to trade (Versor).
- A multi-manager structure exists because no single PM has 100 clever twists (Zach).
- **The Gerber statistic** is a rank-order co-movement measure that ignores moves below a threshold as noise. Papers with Markowitz found "better performance with less risk" when it replaced historical covariance (Gerber).
- Be "the house": each idea has limited loss, is independent and carries a slight edge. Hedge each idea's beta on its own, not with an overlay (Gerber).

### 4. Crowding and alpha decay

- The 2007 Quant Quake happened because everyone bought the same data and built the same factors. A forced liquidation cost PDT \~$500M in 3 days while markets looked calm (Jha).
- Signals crowd more slowly than people expect: they either fade gradually into "table stakes" or die in a sudden liquidation (Jha).
- Decay speeds: high-frequency strategies lose their edge in 1–2 months, while slow ones decay over decades and look like noise (Carver).
- Arms race: companies now pre-test earnings calls against NLP models, and "Lazy Prices" is arbitraged away (Kantos). Earnings-day moves in insurance went from 2–3% to 15–20% intraday as data spread (Ying).
- An edge's value is inversely proportional to how many people know it (Lebron).
- Momentum and value work because everyone latches onto them, and fail in dislocations (Gerber). Technical analysis is "about 20% as effective" as it was (Druckenmiller).

### 5. Regime change

- The biggest power-trading edge is a view on a paradigm shift before it's priced. Historical data is a trap when the grid's topology changes. "If you want to trade an insight from 8 years ago, we're happy to take the other side" (Paddock).
- Regime change only hurts if the regime isn't a model input: if rising rates hurt you, why weren't rates an input? (Chan)
- Even systematic traders must judge whether today's regime is in-sample for their model (Lebron). In March 2020 quant models lost 5–10% because they had never seen a pandemic (Cedar). Regimes now shift every 2–3 years, so rigidity kills (Ying).
- Today's investors grew up in a paradigm that "got way accelerated the last 15 years": globalization, the US "security umbrella" and easy money. It is ending, so its history is a biased sample (Karniol-Tambour, May 2025).

### 6. Liquidity, capacity and the capital base

- Alpha in small, illiquid markets is "a mirage" unless you can trade size. Build real transaction costs into the optimizer (Versor).
- "Funds die from redemptions at the wrong time, not drawdowns." Quantedge introduced lock-ups, a move it estimates cost \~$20B of assets but aligned investors (Quantedge).
- In a crisis your capital base decides whether you can hold on: monthly-liquidity funds rode out the Quant Quake (Jha). Forced sellers create opportunity for those who aren't (Lebron).
- Small can be an edge: firms are viable above \~$100–200M and can trade less liquid names (Jha), and niche PMs are uncorrelated with the pods (Zach).

### 7. Humans remain inputs

- Alpha capture: survey analysts often, trade *deltas* not levels, fade experts who get even more extreme, and follow the first one who breaks from the crowd (Cedar).
- Quantamental: if a question can be answered without knowing which company it is, automate it; "how is this time different" stays human. Fundamental investors size badly and quants size well (Ying).
- Top PMs are \~90–95% conventional plus a clever twist. "Clever beats working harder" (Zach).

### 8. Measuring performance honestly

- No metric is perfect. Sharpe assumes symmetric returns, and LTCM's Sharpe was >3 *including* its blow-up. Use bootstrapped geometric returns at a conservative percentile (Carver).
- You never *know* you have edge, only statistical bounds, which pushes quants toward higher frequency. Judge discretionary edge on decision quality, the way a poker coach does (Lebron).
- Ignore your daily P&L, or view it in percentages, not "I lost a car today" (Carver). Judge process over outcome: 5–10 trades make most of a year (Paddock).

## AI & data in investing

Practitioners agree that AI is a powerful research accelerator and a way to encode an investor's judgment into repeatable workflows. Few believe it can independently find durable alpha: market data is scarce and unstable, the valuable signals sit in outliers where AI is least reliable, and judgment and execution stay human.

| Where AI clearly helps | Where it struggles |
| --- | --- |
| Data gathering (\~100% automatable) and processing (\~80%) (Ying) | Final judgment and asking the right question (\~0% automatable) (Ying) |
| Earnings previews and multi-step workflows: \~35M tokens replace \~1,000 hours, coverage 20 → 60–70 names (Akash) | Discovering stable alpha: \~2 seasonal data points a year, and anything stable gets arbitraged instantly (Gebhardt) |
| Risk management and portfolio optimization with explainable models (Chan) | Predicting returns directly from scarce, regime-shifting data (Chan) |
| Diffing 10-K risk factors and generating meeting questions (Pilecki) | Market-rule nuances: LLMs hallucinate and can be argued into agreeing with you (Paddock) |
| Scaling expert calls by generating the next good question (Matei) | Outliers, the exact moment you need reliable data (Matei) |
| Research plans and code in minutes (Gebhardt, Carver) | Code correctness (\~50% of chatbot answers wrong in Chan's experience) |
| Adding news context to simple causal models (Chan) | Generic LLMs lack domain context; BloombergGPT lost to a smaller generic model (Ying) |

### 1. Finance is a small-data problem

- Structural breaks leave maybe 20 usable years, against millennia of text for LLMs (Chan).
- Seasonal energy offers \~30–45 data points in 15 years. Language is stable, markets aren't. ML is "fancy regression" that can't extrapolate (Gebhardt).
- GenAI's real promise is pre-training on loosely related data and semi-supervised learning: hand-label a few Fed transcripts instead of buying vendor sentiment scores (Chan).

### 2. Alpha lives in outliers, where AI is least trustworthy

- You make money only when you're right and others are wrong, which happens at outliers. Outliers are also where data errors and hallucinations cluster (Matei).
- LLM memory can create a personal echo chamber that feeds you stories matching your playbook, the same way founders get surrounded by yes-men (Matei).
- "If you cannot put a number on it, you don't have a differentiated opinion" (Dan Loeb, via Akash). Analysts want facts with confidence intervals, not forecasts.

### 3. The real shift: encoding judgment into agents

- Tooling went from search to RAG (2024), then knowledge graphs, then agentic harnesses (2025). Model upgrades took accuracy from \~70% to \~90%. "Work's not going away; your judgment is being encoded" (Akash).
- PMs who want analysts to follow their process exactly find agents attractive for the same reason (Matei).
- Frontier models may beat the S&P risk-adjusted, but only inside well-engineered harnesses (ex-IMC trader).
- The path forward is ticker-level knowledge graphs, derived knowledge such as management-credibility scores, then reinforcement learning on market outcomes (Ying). Reinforcement learning is also being applied to turn fair values into trades (Lebron).

### 4. Edge still requires humans, and execution

- AI shortens the trial-and-error loop, but executing rules through losing streaks is a profession most people can't master (Yokokawa).
- The winning setup is human judgment on a robust system, like AI-augmented chess players (Gebhardt).
- Demand for investing talent *grows*: AI is a quant wave for words that collapses pattern-matchable alpha and raises payoffs for differentiated views (Ying).
- Curious investors find AI "a dream come true"; lazy ones who stop at the first answer fall behind (Matei).
- AI "is not going to change the research landscape." A 10-K that took weeks by post now takes seconds, but "just because the data is available doesn't mean the people know how to interpret it" (Stahl, Mar 2026).

### 5. What it does to markets

- Context becomes currency: as everyone runs the same models, edge shifts to proprietary data and context. Markets may become *noisier* because everyone processes faster but judges differently (Cedar).
- AI will make markets more efficient overall and shrink the gaps between large and small funds, but it creates new inefficiencies around AI-driven flows (Ying).
- LLMs will eventually let quants capture social-arbitrage signals, eroding retail's edge (Tom). Barriers to launching a fund fall: expect \~10x more hedge funds (Cedar) and more solo "AI hedge funds" (Yokokawa).

### 6. How firms are adopting AI

- **Bridgewater** has done machine learning since 2012, began building an "artificial investor" in 2022, and has run an AI-first fund generating alpha since 2024. Lesson: redesign processes around the technology instead of making it imitate humans (Jensen).
- **Temasek** trains associates to work "two levels above" because AI handles the base work (Pillay).
- **Quantedge** keeps generative AI out of production models because outputs are inconsistent, but uses it for research, data cleaning and execution.
- Fundamental PMs should redesign their process from scratch and keep someone who knows Python to audit convincingly wrong AI output (Ying).
- On the corporate side, Hoffman says record every meeting and let AI draft follow-ups. Bloisi had every executive learn to code in a 4-hour course.

### 7. NLP and alternative-data craft

- Dictionary sentiment is dead. Train a classifier per document type: a reporter praising a company is positive, a CEO saying the same about their own company isn't (Kantos).
- Match data to horizon: news alpha decays in days, while earnings-call forward-looking statements drift for \~1.5 months (Kantos). Train text models directly on risk-adjusted returns, over years not quarters, to avoid fads (Jha).
- Creative data wins: highway-patrol accident data for insurers, celebrity tweets mapping wildfire damage (Ying), hailstorm searches ahead of a roofing distributor's results (Camillo).
- Data infrastructure comes first: point-in-time storage and keeping copies of vendor data to catch revisions (Akash, Jha).
- Real-time KPIs from card data, web scraping and parking-lot images are now "table stakes". Use cohorts to test a multi-year thesis: Eminence compares Dave & Buster's 10 remodelled stores with the rest of its \~200 (Sandler).

## Investing in the AI boom

The tech specialists agree that AI is a real, early S-curve whose value has gone first to compute, chips and hardware bottlenecks. Models are settling into an oligopoly, power and sites have become the binding constraint, and horizontal application software is the first casualty, though domain leaders may still win. They split on price and timing: Whale Rock, Light Street and Maplelane saw estimates still too low in mid-to-late 2026, while Davis and Druckenmiller heard late-1990s rhymes, and even Altman expects a glut.

| Who | Firm | Where they see value (date) | Main risk they flag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alex Sacerdote | Whale Rock | Model oligopoly (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google); hardware no longer commoditized; net short app software (2026) | Model progress stalls and open source drives a "race to the bottom" |
| Leon Shaulov | Maplelane | Semi equipment (Lam), memory, analog with a power angle (May 2026) | Some names went "100 miles an hour in a 60 mile an hour zone" |
| Glen Kacher | Light Street | TSMC, Nvidia, Broadcom, AMD; AWS (Sep 2026) | Data-center opposition and power |
| Tykocinski, Silver | Maverick | Upstream bottlenecks so far; next, services and applications; life-science tools (Jun 2026) | Training-to-applications "air pocket"; Chinese commoditization |
| Dan Sundheim | D1 | Frontier labs, SpaceX; AI-driven shorts, starting with software (Feb 2026) | Labs' capital intensity; a China–Taiwan clash over chips |
| Druckenmiller | Duquesne | Nvidia (2022–24); then power, uranium, copper, biotech | AI "disturbingly heated" since mid-2025 |
| Chris Davis | Davis Advisors | Enablers, users and insulated firms (May 2026) | "Overestimating phase"; picking winners at hype prices |
| Orlando Bravo | Thoma Bravo (software PE) | Each vertical's #1 software company as the channel for agents; AI \~35% of portfolio bookings (May 2026) | Disruption ("I'm extremely worried about it"); tighter software credit |
| Satya Nadella | Microsoft | Copilots and a fungible, high-utilization fleet; "agents are the new seats" (Oct 2025) | Power and "warm shells", not chips; "exotic" vendor financing |
| Sam Altman | OpenAI | Compute ahead of demand: \~$1.4T of commitments, per host (Oct 2025) | "there will come a glut for sure" as unit costs fall \~40x a year |
| Mike Alfred | Alpine Fox; IREN director | Power, land and operators that turn them into compute (Nov 2025–Jun 2026) | Weak operators' "systemic" delays; more boom-bust cycles |
| Eduardo Marques | Pertento | Korean memory through holdco and preferred discounts (May 2026) | AI hardware is cyclical, and "valuations look really full" |

### 1. Ride the S-curve, but only behind an airtight advantage

- An S-curve inflects when adoption barriers fall: smartphones went from 1% to 50% ownership in 4–5 years. The curve is "a map into the future," answering Buffett's objection to tech (Sacerdote).
- Growth turns linear at \~30–40% penetration. Yet Sacerdote calls selling Apple in 2012, at \~50% US smartphone penetration, a mistake: it kept compounding \~20%. "It's okay to miss the first 100%" if the curve is tall.
- Without a moat "you can be in the best S-curve of all time and still lose out": RIM, Palm and Nokia went to zero (Sacerdote).
- Curves can stall. US EVs hit a wall at \~10–15%, and Tesla went from "10 out of 10" to "zero out of 10" for Whale Rock (Sacerdote).
- Tech cycles run infrastructure (about 5–10 years), platform (about years 6–16), then applications (about years 11–21). As of Sep 2026 AI is "a third of the way through the first leg" (Kacher).
- AI is "an L-curve, straight up": enterprise application AI <1% penetrated, infrastructure \~10% (Sacerdote, spring 2026). The leader takes \~two-thirds of a market, #2 "20% 25% tops" (Kacher).

### 2. So far value has accrued at compute and the bottlenecks

- Infrastructure inflects first and is needed "no matter who wins above," so Whale Rock went "chips and the infrastructure first." It bought Nvidia in Jan 2023 at \~4x its 2025 earnings (Sacerdote).
- Money is made "following that migration of that bottleneck further upstream": GPUs, fabs, tools, even "obscure materials listed on a Japanese stock exchange." The sharpest "revision torque" hits low-margin starting points (Tykocinski, Jun 2026).
- AI workloads grow \~10x a year against \~30% in the x86 era, decommoditizing parts. Celestica, sole Google TPU server supplier, traded at \~8x earnings; PCBs went from 10 to 40 layers (Sacerdote).
- Semis became an oligopoly: Nvidia holds \~85% of AI accelerators, and TSMC can "win no matter who wins" (Kacher, Sep 2026). Wafer-fab equipment could go from \~$120–130bn to \~$300bn in 3–4 years (Shaulov, May 2026).
- Power is the next layer down. "Gas turbines are going to be in shortage for a very long time," yet Siemens Energy traded at half GE Vernova's EV (Sundheim, Oct 2025). Druckenmiller worked down the chain to power, uranium and copper.
- Enablers such as Applied Materials, TSMC, Texas Instruments, gas and copper "make a lot of money whether or not the returns on the capital being spent are good or not" (Davis).
- Memory was cheapest in Korea: SK Hynix at \~6x 2026 earnings vs Micron at \~10x, both "massive cash gushers" (Marques, May 2026).

### 3. Below the chips, power and sites bind now, and a glut will test the operators

- The choke point "hasn't been chips" (Alfred, Jun 2026). Microsoft has chips "sitting in inventory that I can't plug in" for lack of powered "warm shells" (Nadella, Oct 2025).
- US power use has been flat since 2007, so natural gas is "the vehicle of choice" for data centres. Stahl owned LandBridge, Permian land with water and rights of way (Oct 2024).
- Underwrite the operator: "how good are they at taking power and turning into compute." An edge resting on a rising commodity price means "long periods of time where it's not fun to be an equity shareholder" (Alfred).
- Three models compete. Colocation landlords skip the GPUs, IREN and xAI own the compute, and CoreWeave relies on "20 plus external providers." Alfred, an IREN director, bets on owning the stack.
- Delays are a tell. Operators hailed as winners reported delays and guidance cuts that the market called temporary: "No, these are systemic issues" (Alfred, Nov 2025).
- A glut will come: unit cost falls "like 40x" a year, "a very scary exponent from an infrastructure buildout standpoint." "Some people are going to get really burned" (Altman, Oct 2025).
- The defense is a fungible fleet and a short backlog: "fungibility of the fleet is everything for a cloud provider." Microsoft's $400B RPO averages \~2 years, and "circularity ultimately will be tested by demand" (Nadella).

### 4. The model layer: oligopoly or commodity, and who pays for it

- Whale Rock's April 2023 options were winner-take-all, commodity or an oligopoly of 3–4. About 50–60 startups died, leaving Anthropic, OpenAI and Google as a "three-horse race" (Sacerdote, 2026).
- Sundheim expects 4–5 lasting LLMs because "the capital required to get into this business is too great." Gross margins are "quite high," unlike a commodity industry (Feb 2026).
- His model is Netflix plus Spotify: a huge fixed training cost sold at high incremental margins, made sticky by personalization (Sundheim). Druckenmiller saw it the other way in Oct 2024: "they're all going to give you the same answer," so he stayed neutral.
- The real risk is capital intensity "to a degree that we've never seen before in the history of business." With that leverage, the labs can't afford 2–3 years of slow adoption (Sundheim). Altman tempers scaling hopes: "Unfortunately," intelligence scales roughly with the log of compute (Oct 2025).
- If models plateau, open source catches up and "it might be a race to the bottom." Chips would hold up better: "chip companies don't care who's winning tokens" (Sacerdote).
- Hyperscalers split the group. Sundheim sees "a worse business model going forward" as demand concentrates in 4–5 labs that will insource compute. Kacher sees them "playing catch-up," and Sacerdote said Google has "won AI" and is "very cheap" (May 2026).
- Inference decides who pays. Each chat burns "a lot more GPU cycles" than a search, so consumer monetization is "murky," but "enterprise monetization is much clearer" (Nadella, Oct 2025).

### 5. AI as a threat: software first, but not all software

- Whale Rock held 40–50% in software \~5 years ago. Incumbents' AI "products were not very good... Nobody could charge for them," so it was net short entering 2026 (Sacerdote).
- His headwinds: CIO budgets shifting to tokens, no more annual price rises, seats hit by hiring freezes, and a new sales motion. Salesforce has \~$40bn of sales but only \~$500–700m of AI ARR.
- Sundheim had "basically no shorts" on AI victims before 2026. Systems of record (ERP, CRM) are protected "at least for for a few years" but small productivity tools are more exposed.
- The SaaS-apocalypse view is a "huge simplification": "core systems don't tend to get swapped out," as banks' mainframes showed. Selloffs create bounceback trades (Kacher, Sep 2026).
- "It's too broad to say software": horizontal applications are in trouble, while data and infrastructure software like Datadog can win (Shaulov). The labs using Datadog is a "pretty good tell" (Sacerdote).
- Davis sorts the rest into enablers, users, insulated firms (Tyson) and the "walking dead" (Kodak, newspapers). Users have big data sets, cuttable labour costs and willing management (Capital One). "It's still early" to buy a non-tech stock for its AI plan (Kacher).
- "agents are the new seats," as an agent tier replaces the old business-logic tier. High-price, low-usage products have "a little bit of a problem," and AI adds "a real marginal cost to software this time around" (Nadella, Oct 2025).
- Domain leaders deploy "most of the agents being deployed right now," and "our retention rates are holding" (Bravo, Thoma Bravo, May 2026). In software "There never was" much of a moat, so leadership decides.
- Don't bottom-fish: "this is exactly the wrong time to buy a mediocre software company at a bargain price," even at a 7–8% day-one yield (Bravo).

### 6. Bubble or not: the debate extended, 2023–26

The disagreement table pits Ullal, Hoffman, Bloisi and Nadella against Jensen, Singer, Kantesaria and Altman. The new voices add dates and tests.

- **Druckenmiller (Jun 2023):** AI is real, unlike crypto, but "all of AI is not going to make it through." Secular runs last years: Cisco ran \~4 years in the dot-com boom.
- **Shaulov (Apr 2024):** $3–4tn of AI savings or revenue (\~5–8% Fortune 500 efficiency) justifies $500–800bn of spend, "15 to 20% Capital intensity."
- **Li Lu (Dec 2024):** Nvidia is worth more than all German listed companies combined, "despite potential bubbles in its valuation," because it is indispensable.
- **Sundheim (Oct 2025):** \~70% of breakthrough technologies brought a bubble. Watch debt-financed capex, circular financing and bad companies at crazy prices. Nvidia at "20-something times" looked like 1996–97, not 1999.
- **Druckenmiller (early 2026):** AI got "disturbingly heated" from mid-2025 and began to rhyme with 1999–2000, so he rotated into Teva and biotech.
- **Davis (May 2026):** "Similarities are bigger than the differences." Amara's law puts us in "the overestimating phase," and the internet's leaders Yahoo, AOL and Cisco ruined their holders. The S&P was at 26x earnings.
- **Alfred (Nov 2025–May 2026):** bubble talk was "a complete joke" after Nvidia's Nov 2025 results, but by May 2026 "we might be in '97 right now... We might even be in mid-'99." He watches data-center capex, which turned with the stocks in 2000.
- **Maverick (Jun 2026):** dot-com capex ran \~200% of operating cash flow; AI's ran "well under 100 percent." But a multi-trillion-dollar company just issued equity, so it has "more similarities by the day."
- **The bulls (May–Sep 2026):** Nvidia was called a bubble every year for four years; "It's going to be bouncy" (Sacerdote). It is the mid-90s, not the late 90s, and demand outruns supply (Kacher). Shaulov expects "some accidents" but "the next 50 to 100 is up."

### 7. Private vs public: the labs and SpaceX

- Some of the largest companies are private: "you really have to know what's going on in the private markets" (Sacerdote). D1's private–public overlap is at a record "because of AI" (Sundheim).
- Private markets are less competitive on analysis but competitive on access: "that company has to want you to be an investor" (Sundheim).
- D1 entered OpenAI at the $125bn round only \~65–70% sure it would be a good business, betting on the skew. It backed Anthropic as "second player" on Dario's writing, "better than almost any CEO I've seen since Bezos" (Sundheim).
- Whale Rock passed at $60bn on negative gross margins, then invested at $180bn in Aug 2025 after meeting management and building a 90-page deck. It calls it "a total home run" (Sacerdote).
- SpaceX is a low-cost launch near-monopoly plus Starlink (P × Q subscribers), able to triple without long-shot bets (Sundheim, Oct 2025). By Feb 2026 Starship and Starlink had made global telecom its market.
- Counterpoint: illiquid privates are one of Davis's "three horsemen" for the next five years; "people have underestimated how valuable liquidity is." In private tech, bad bets show faster than great ones (Sundheim on Rivian vs SpaceX).
- Microsoft's \~$13.5bn bought a 27% OpenAI stake worth $135bn, and "you can't lose more than 13.5 billion" (Nadella, Oct 2025).

### 8. Valuation and sizing in a fast-moving theme

- Value on earnings 3–5 years out: Whale Rock's best entries were \~4x out-year earnings (Nvidia, Tesla) while near-term P/Es looked expensive (Sacerdote).
- Winners "may not screen as the cheapest" because "estimates are like 50 to 70% too low" (Shaulov, May 2026). "It's all been earnings"; multiples haven't caught up (Sacerdote). In secular trends, multiples rerate with revisions (Tykocinski).
- Davis is the counterweight: he models no multiple expansion and avoids even good businesses at hype valuations.
- Sizing: up to 10% at cost, run to 15%, with AI infrastructure "might be 20% of the fund" (Sacerdote, early 2025). Light Street's four core semis were \~40% of its public book (per host). "The biggest reason people blow up in Tech is they miss on revenue" (Sacerdote).
- Druckenmiller started Nvidia "not big" but "enough to get hurt on," then doubled twice. He sold at \~800 because "I couldn't stand seeing success"; it hit \~1,400 five weeks later.
- Expect pain: tech has a big selloff every 7–10 years (Sacerdote). Light Street lost 54% in 2022, then made 46%, 59% and 37% (Kacher, per host).
- Hard assets can cap the downside. In Q1 2023 Alfred valued IREN's sites at "maybe two to four X" its market cap; it rose from $1.02 (Dec 2022) to \~76 (Nov 2025). As a director, he has never sold.

### 9. Implications for the screener

- **Backward-looking quality misses S-curve winners.** Celestica and Elite Materials were low-margin "commodity" businesses before AI. Flag revenue acceleration plus rising ASPs and gross margins, and estimate revisions from a low margin base (Sacerdote, Tykocinski).
- **Add a forward-earnings lens.** Rank by P/E on earnings 2–4 years out, not trailing, since consensus may be far too low (Sacerdote, Shaulov).
- **A long record can hide an AI loser.** "Don't feel safe in times of change": Kodak was in the S&P's top third as digital overtook film (Davis). Flag seat-based pricing, reliance on annual price rises and AI revenue ≤1–2% of sales (Sacerdote).
- **But don't auto-exclude software.** Favour systems of record with strong distribution and stable retention (Sundheim, Kacher). Bravo's markers: the domain's #1, long-tenured leaders, and \~20% growth with steady margins and retention.
- **Watch capital intensity.** Flag capex above operating cash flow, debt- or equity-funded build-outs and circular financing (Maverick, Sundheim). Rising capex plus customer concentration can erode a hyperscaler's margins even as growth speeds up (Sundheim). Expect a lower multiple when defensive capex shrinks free cash flow (Stahl on Alphabet, Mar 2026), and prefer short, diversified backlogs (Nadella).
- **Don't capitalize peak hardware margins.** Hardware and materials are "more subject to commodification," and China competes in optics and analog (Tykocinski). For heavy reinvestors, "a declining return on capital may indicate a brighter not a worse future" (Dorsey).
- **Test infrastructure owners on assets and delivery.** Compare market cap with land, power and equipment, and flag repeated delays, guidance cuts and rented hosting (Alfred). In 2000 data-center capex "turned over right around the time that the stocks turned over" (Alfred, May 2026).

## Risk, sizing & psychology

Across styles, the durable investors define risk as permanent loss or ignorance, not volatility. They size so that being wrong never knocks them out, and they treat their own conviction and emotions as the biggest hazard.

### 1. Define risk properly

- Risk is permanent loss of capital, not volatility (Gaonkar, Pilecki). "Volatility isn't the risk that matters most": Quantedge deliberately runs \~25% volatility.
- Risk is "not knowing what you're doing" (Hohn). Start with risk, not return.
- Elliott's worst trades came from misclassifying risk: a "late-stage" bankruptcy that wasn't, and a bond arbitrage priced on a deflation scenario that got worse (Singer).
- Negative-skew strategies (option selling, LTCM) need more than Sharpe. LTCM's data was simply too short to contain the killer outliers (Carver).
- "Risk management is not about not losing money. Risk management is about unexpectedly losing money" (Gerber).
- Volatility and beta are not risk; risk is how much money you can lose (Greenblatt).
- Concentration means exposure to one macro variable, not one sector: in 2007 Countrywide, Toll Brothers and Lehman were one housing bet (Davis).
- Kill ideas with \~70% customer concentration, single suppliers or one plant (Haley).
- Value at Risk was the GFC's flaw: holding capital for only 95–98% of outcomes lets a bank take a tail bet "an infinite number of times" (Einhorn).

### 2. Survive first, so you can exploit crises

- Losing 88% of his parents' money in 1974 taught Singer that losses wreck judgment. Intact capital and an intact mind let you buy when "everything got cheap" in 2008. "Make a buck, keep it."
- Risk control, not brilliance, is why Bridgewater outlived its 1991 competitors. Build portfolios that survive the widest range of worlds rather than betting on one (Jensen).
- Avoid regular 30% drawdowns: recovery needs +43–50%. Don't mix leverage with concentration (Orr).
- Stay near flat between big setups and keep capital for them (Paddock). Holding cash is fine (Li Lu).
- GameStop (Jan 2021): D1 covered shorts at the worst time rather than risk the firm; those shorts later underperformed massively. Size so you never have to (Sundheim).
- Debt turns equity into an option bet, and bank debt is worse than bonds because banks can force a bankruptcy (Gaines).
- Run money like an insurer: plan for the 1-in-50-year event and ask "what happens if things go down 50% can we survive it" (Watsa). Einhorn carries no leverage, so no lender's terms can force him.

### 3. Sizing rules from the interviews

### 4. Conviction versus doubt

- "Conviction" is often bias and the reason people hold losers and mis-size. Earned conviction comes only from living through bull and bear markets (Xin).
- 100% conviction is a red flag. Go "from FOMO to TOMO" (thoughtfully missing out) and spend as much time on what consensus could get wrong (Gaonkar).
- When already very bullish or bearish experts get even more extreme, bet against them; follow the first who breaks ranks (Cedar).
- Science's balance: doubt is the brakes and can-do confidence is the accelerator, and you need both (Perlmutter).
- Li Lu and Kantesaria show the other side: when the work is done, concentrate and hold.
- "My advantage is not IQ, it's trigger pulling." The danger in concentration is stale positions, not size (Druckenmiller).
- Write a pre-mortem: the article from five years ahead explaining why each holding underperformed (Davis).
- "You always have doubt"; in a slump, "go back to your process." A hitter can go "0 for 20 and yet it's okay because they've been hitting the ball hard" (Cohen, Point72, May 2025).

### 5. Admit when you're wrong, quickly

- If an idea isn't making money within months or a couple of quarters, treat yourself as wrong. "You'd rather be right than make money" is the trap. A market moving against you is "one of the strongest tells there is" (Orr).
- "Positions can become less risky at higher prices." Falling prices often mean you're wrong. Re-underwrite and never average down by default (Pilecki).
- Great investors exit losers quickly instead of insisting the market is wrong (Rubenstein). Take the 12–20% loss instead of hoping for an activist (Dorsey).
- Sunk-cost bias: Gaonkar sold Nvidia early and then never revisited it. Missed names are a rich source of new ideas.
- Omissions are the biggest mistakes of all: Li Lu missed a 50–100x stock and Kantesaria passed on Microsoft's IPO.
- Cost basis is irrelevant: if the reason is gone, sell, and be willing to buy back higher. He'll exit a 3-year trade within 5 days (Druckenmiller).
- Set a re-underwriting threshold: earnings down 5–10% rarely change the thesis; down 20% gets a full work session (Sundheim).
- When a position goes wrong, presume "we might have missed something here." Reassess, then reduce, exit or hold; adding happens "very rarely" (Einhorn).

### 6. Emotions, temperament and self-knowledge

- Turn off the daily P&L email, or think in percentages (Carver).
- Journal every decision to learn your pain point and reflexes. Strong emotions and rigidity are the easiest tells of a bad investor (Ying).
- Greed and fear don't change. Executing rules through losing streaks is unnatural, "like boxing form" (Yokokawa).
- "People who say they're risk-neutral are actually risk-seeking" (Lebron on SBF).
- Temperament is largely fixed, and people revert to it when alone. Extreme delayed gratification is required (Kantesaria). You must be comfortable being alone (Li Lu).
- Trade smaller in cold streaks and bigger in hot ones. "There's no reward for degree of difficulty" (Pilecki).
- Constant risk aversion also prevents burnout, since adversity drains emotional energy (Singer).
- After a mistake, stop torturing yourself after \~48 hours; when you're an emotional mess, step away completely (Druckenmiller).
- Even a formula that doubled the market lagged for 3–4 years at a stretch, and clients leave by year three (Greenblatt). Stahl's firm endured "gargantuan underperformance running almost 3 years during the internet bubble" rather than buy "what we decried" (Bregman, his co-founder).

## CEO interviews: leadership, culture, strategy & capital allocation

The sixteen CEOs and founders share a pattern. They find a large market with real customer pain, attack it with a structural cost or technology advantage, and concentrate resources. They treat culture and speed as things leaders deliberately build, and in 2025–26 they are rebuilding their companies around AI.

| Leader | Company | Signature lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emma Walmsley | GSK | Culture is the CEO's job; single-point accountability; free capital by spinning off what nobody values |
| David Ricks | Eli Lilly | Speed is a management choice: parallel work, pre-set go/no-go, "beat the last team's record" |
| Jack Dorsey | Block | Build on open protocols; don't rush into advertising; let users co-create the product |
| Nikesh Arora | Palo Alto Networks | Obsess over product; platformize a fragmented market; in crises, create certainty |
| David Vélez | Nubank | Attack oligopolies with a 20x cost edge and fanatical customer love |
| Forrest Li | Sea | Ride the tech wave; build local solutions from first principles; stay humble |
| Jayshree Ullal | Arista | Win white spaces the giant ignores; "do the right thing" even when it's costly |
| Dylan Field | Figma | When software is abundant, design and taste differentiate; hire for slope |
| Reid Hoffman | LinkedIn / Greylock | Deploy, iterate, learn; don't drive every risk to zero first |
| Robert Gentz | Zalando | A clear flywheel aligns leadership; "dare to fail" |
| Brian Armstrong | Coinbase | Compete on trust; be right before consensus; "action produces information" |
| Alain Lam | Xiaomi | Honest prices; 10x resources on one product; own the core tech |
| Fabricio Bloisi | Prosus | Ambidextrous organizations with "jet skis"; AI is under-hyped |
| Yu Kai | Horizon Robotics | "A terrible business can be a great business"; be the platform |
| Satya Nadella | Microsoft | "one company with one platform logic"; keep the fleet fungible; "agents are the new seats" |
| Sam Altman | OpenAI | "Platform plus killer app"; bet compute ahead of demand; work with people for a decade or more |

### 1. Start from customer pain and product obsession

- Find a big market protected by an oligopoly and full of pain. Vélez took 5 months to open a Brazilian bank account and got a card at 1,200% interest; Brazil's top five most-hated companies were banks. Xiaomi saw low-quality, high-price phones. Shopee's founder couldn't accept that next-day e-commerce didn't exist in Southeast Asia.
- Customer love pays in the long run: Nubank's NPS reached 90–94, and it refunded customers after a bug they never noticed (Vélez). Sea and Zalando track satisfaction daily.
- "Tech companies die when they stop focusing on product" (Larry Page, via Arora). Products take 4–7 years to build.
- Trust is the moat in regulated businesses (Armstrong). Arista resolves support issues in 25 minutes on average, ICU-style (Ullal).

### 2. Speed is a management choice

- Lilly roughly halved development times by running steps in parallel (accepting financial risk), pre-specifying the results needed to advance, and setting each team's record as the next target (Ricks). "Companies seen as slow should look first at their leadership."
- Xiaomi launched a car in under 3 years from a standing start. Chinese automakers' model cycles are \~2 years versus \~5 in Europe (Lam, Yu Kai).
- "Jet skis": 5–10-person teams test 20–30 versions before any big budget. Big companies fund a $1B idea and lose a year (Bloisi).
- A clear flywheel lets leadership align and scale fast: Zalando went from €5M to €1.2B in four years (Gentz).

### 3. Focus and concentration of resources

- Xiaomi put \~10x the usual R&D into one first car instead of many models, and focuses on three things a quarter (Lam).
- "Put all your wood behind one arrowhead": the world values only a few things you do (Ricks).
- GSK spun off a consumer division that no analyst asked about, freeing the balance sheet for R&D (Walmsley).
- Horizon has done one thing (computing for robots) for 11 years, and Arista entered only white spaces that Cisco ignored (Yu Kai, Ullal).
- Microsoft is "not a conglomerate" but "one company with one platform logic." "One capital outlay" serves Azure, M365 and Xbox cloud gaming, and the blended return must match its margins (Nadella, Oct 2025).

### 4. Moats seen from the operator's side

- "Heavy investment, slow return" can be the moat: auto-grade chips take 5–8 years to reach production, which keeps VC-funded entrants out (Yu Kai).
- Complexity is a moat: Zalando's pan-European logistics became a B2B product, and Shopee built its own address system (Gentz, Forrest Li).
- Platformization of a fragmented market (Arora); custody depth that makes products sticky (Armstrong); software architecture that self-heals (Ullal).
- Field's warning: far less software has moats now, so design, craft and point of view decide winners.

### 5. Culture is what leaders do

- Culture is "not an HR issue" but the CEO's job. Say it relentlessly, since a third of staff may be new; hire, pay, promote and fire for it; measure it with anonymous team feedback (Walmsley).
- Vélez has personally run culture onboarding for every new hire every month since 2013. Bloisi holds brutally transparent "strategy share" sessions covering what's going badly.
- Values that recur:
  - customer obsession;
  - ownership ("owners, not renters");
  - frugality (Zalando's €60 book debate);
  - humility (Sea's foundational value);
  - "do the right thing" (Arista replaced customers' gear at its own cost).
- Being too nice is a failure mode. Lilly added structured conflict, with a contrarian team arguing against every Phase 3 advance (Ricks). "Kindness means being extremely direct" (Field).
- "Dare to fail": without calculated risk you are playing "poker without blinds" (Gentz).

### 6. People: hiring, firing and scaling yourself

- "Companies grow exponentially; humans grow linearly." Vélez's biggest mistakes were changing the team too slowly. Hire missionaries, not mercenaries.
- Hire for slope, the rate of improvement, and ignore degrees (Field). Hire entrepreneurs, empower them and fire when it doesn't work (Bloisi). "Hire people better than you" (Walmsley).
- Grow your own talent with graduate rotations: Sea and Quantedge do this where no experienced pool exists.
- Leaders must scale themselves:
  - Move from solving every problem to clarifying goals and staffing (Ricks's Canada lesson).
  - "Show your work" so people can reason without you (Field).
  - Step in only on ambiguous or long-term calls and on culture (Gentz).
  - When people escalate, ask what they've already tried with their peers (Ricks).
- In a crisis, "your job isn't to talk about winning but to create certainty" (Arora). Leaders take the hardest, unpopular decisions personally (Forrest Li).
- Build founding teams from people you've known "for like a decade plus," mixed with "some new energy and volatility" (Altman, May 2023).

### 7. Adopting AI inside the company

- Force initial adoption: Armstrong required every engineer to try AI coding tools within a week, and >50% of Coinbase's code is now AI-written. Bloisi taught every executive to code; 20,000 Prosus staff build agents.
- AI handles \~80% of Sea's customer inquiries at human-level satisfaction. One AI fix added 8% to Lilly's GLP-1 output. Xiaomi simulated 100+ material formulas.
- Use AI to grow and learn, not just to cut costs; cutting too early slows adoption (Field). If you aren't finding it useful in substantive work, "you're not trying hard enough" (Hoffman).
- Agentic commerce is coming: agents will transact with stablecoins and order on users' behalf (Armstrong, Bloisi). Seat-based software and phone apps face disintermediation (Arora).
- Every Microsoft employee gets M365 Copilot and GitHub Copilot, and "any planning, any execution starts with AI." A network lead who couldn't hire built agents instead (Nadella, Oct 2025).

### 8. Strategy, markets and geopolitics

- Contrarian timing: "if it's consensus, you're too late" (Armstrong). Every successful idea looked dumb to smart people at first (Hoffman).
- Expand into new markets as a "call option," not betting the house (Vélez). Build ecosystems on high-frequency use cases (Bloisi).
- Europe is a recurring frustration:
  - forced divestments (Bloisi);
  - a \~2-year drug-access lag (Ricks);
  - fragmentation and too many stock exchanges (Gentz).
  - Their prescription: "get on the pitch" (Hoffman) and build a positive "European dream" (Gentz).
- Going public brings discipline and trust (Field, Armstrong, Gentz). Share prices reflect external perception, so stay true to fundamentals and be wary of euphoria at peaks (Forrest Li).
- Prefer partnership to zero-sum framing with China and Europe (Yu Kai). Anything innovative eventually intersects with government, so build a policy muscle (Armstrong).

## Careers & building a fund or firm

The career advice is consistent. Find where you are differentially good and genuinely interested, because interest drives the hours that become edge. Build deep, transferable skills, and make your work visible. For founders of funds, structure and terms matter as much as returns.

### 1. Choose where you have an edge and genuine interest

- Find what you're *differentially* good at, empirically. Use \~3 deliberately different internships, because interest is idiosyncratic: some people love finding a trade on page 34 of a prospectus (Lebron).
- Ask what you'd do if every job paid nothing, and whether you'd enjoy solving problems alone (Bianco).
- Choose what you'd stay up all night doing, not what looks best on paper. People overrate their ability to calculate expected value (Xin).
- "Choice matters more than hard work." Fish where there are fish, but don't chase the hottest sector; juniors mistake sector luck for skill (Ying). Pick the seat where you're seen as a future partner, not a resource (Xin).
- Only enter investing if you love it, Saturday mornings reading transcripts included. Seats are shrinking (Pilecki). Many brilliant problem-solvers don't enjoy trading's reward system (Lebron).
- Experiment to find what you enjoy, then do something useful (Rubenstein). Stay open to surprise: Ricks joined Lilly only because his wife studied in Indiana.

### 2. Skills that compound

- **Maths and statistics depth.** Quant firms want statisticians who know finance, not finance people. Rewriting his resume as pure statistics produced two offers in a month (Bianco).
- **First-principles thinking.** Reconstruct the core truth, then adapt (Ying). Remove all constraints and rebuild from scratch with today's tools (Arora).
- **Breadth.** Prefer a liberal education (history, politics, philosophy) over business courses (Singer). "You need many straws" to see the sky (Strannegård). Be a generalist who connects the dots (Ricks). A broad curriculum always helps (Ramakrishnan).
- **Programming and data fluency.** Versor tests "hands on the keyboard." Someone on every team should be able to audit AI output (Ying).
- **AI-native fluency.** Pitch yourself as the person who can help transform an organization (Hoffman). Fundamentals still matter, because AI is a force multiplier like a calculator (Lebron).
- **Work ethic.** "You'd better learn to love to work." It's hard to learn after your early 20s (Bianco).
- **Finance fundamentals.** Knowing options, finance and accounting let Tepper see at once that Goldman's desk model mispriced calls. You need them to program machines or to compete with them (Tepper).

### 3. Breaking in

- Publish \~10 stock write-ups or "fund letters" publicly. If you have edge, people will find you (Pilecki, Orr).
- Open a small account ($5–10K), build \~30 genuinely different longs and \~50 tiny shorts, and get 500–1,000 reps (Orr).
- Operations and investment-support roles get \~2 applicants versus \~200 for analyst roles, and a proactive operations person adds real value (Orr).
- Getting hired by showing returns on your own money "almost never happens" (Carver). No question is too stupid on day one (Lebron).
- Go where learning is fastest: AI in San Francisco, manufacturing in China, drones in Ukraine. Talk to people 5–10 years older (Lebron).
- Sundheim's anonymous Value Investors Club write-up exposed a fraud (capitalized expenses); it circulated among hedge funds and got him his job.
- Bad seats can teach the most. Tepper joined Republic Steel's treasury "on its way to bankruptcy" and did more financings in 1–2 years than in its 100-year history. "what's gonna define you is how you recover from those things" (Tepper).

### 4. Building a fund

- **Don't bend terms to raise capital.** Quantedge introduced lock-ups and soft-closed its liquid class, a move it estimates cost a \~$20B fund but bought a 3-year+ investor horizon. Employees' permanent capital gives others faith in the 50-year plan.
- **Prepare for years.** Keep a "hedge fund" document, resolve one launch item a day, and first succeed in one niche (Zach). Expect enormous effort (1,500+ meetings a year) and thick skin. Limit prospective LPs to two calls.
- **Hire PMs only to repeat what they've done for 3+ years.** Asking them to be "5–10% different" lowers success rates (Zach).
- **Lean cost structure.** No base salaries or signing bonuses, and minimal pass-through costs (Zach). Greenlight began as two people in 130 sq ft; today a 10–40-person firm needs "a couple hundred million" to launch (Einhorn).
- **Incentives.** Remove pay uncertainty, since discretionary bonuses on top of trading stress are "borderline cruelty." Avoid traders who accept blow-up risk as "worth it" (Paddock). Reward people collectively so diversifying strategies aren't punished in lean years (Quantedge).
- **Run it as a business.** Founder-investors love the craft and neglect the business, so most single-manager funds have key-man risk and no exit value (Matei).
- **Capital takes time.** Allocators cap stakes at \~25% of a fund and want 3-year records (Jha). Protecting capital doesn't stop outflows: down 19% in 2008 against \~40% for the market, Eminence still halved from \~$5B to $2.5B (Sandler). Bootstrapping a data business avoids investors and a boss (Jha).
- **Code every idea.** Hudson Bay's deal codes record how each idea was sourced, hedged and exited, plus its worst case; that scaled one trader's discipline across 27 PMs (Gerber).
- **Expand only into adjacencies, slowly.** A 2007 move into power transmission rights became Gerber's biggest mistake.
- **Outside capital needs far more infrastructure than you think.** Schonfeld expected three or four extra people and was off by several hundred (Tolkin).
- **Face investors at the bottom.** At the June 2022 trough D1 kept its LP dinners and cut portfolio risk (Sundheim).

### 5. Small versus large firms

- Small firms can compete once they cover costs (\~$100–200M): they are nimble, less likely to be cut in a drawdown, and can trade less liquid names (Jha).
- Industry structure is heading toward mega-pods plus a long tail of $100–500M niche boutiques, with generalist mid-size managers squeezed (Zach). Only "a few dozen" firms with long-short DNA remain (Sandler, Oct 2024).
- Big funds have money but bureaucracy; small funds can't afford AI talent, a "Nash equilibrium" (Chan). Outsourced data teams can give single managers platform-grade tools faster than in-house teams slowed by compliance (Matei).
- Barriers keep falling. Monthly API developers grew from \~20K to 80K+ in six months as "solopreneurs" trade through entities (Yokokawa). Cedar expects \~10x more hedge funds.

## Thinking well: science, doubt & decision-making

The scientists and educators supply the meta-skills behind everything above: think in probabilities, actively seek disagreement, use protocols that protect decisions from bias, and stay humble about prediction. Investors who succeed independently reinvented many of the same tools.

### 1. Think in probabilities, not certainties

- Bet 90% on this and 70% on that, rather than calling things true or false. Holding a 25% chance you're wrong makes group discussion fluid and less ego-driven (Perlmutter).
- Think in ranges, not single hands, as in poker (Jensen). Make many positive-expected-value bets and eliminate the negative ones (Orr).
- You can never *know* you have an edge, only statistical bounds (Lebron). Information ratio ≈ skill × √(number of bets) (Quantedge).
- Poker and stock-picking are "a very similar skillset": separate what you know (price, sales), what you can surmise (motives, competition) and unknowable events within a range (Einhorn).

### 2. Seek out disagreement deliberately

- Science's strength is putting work in front of people who will attack it. Society has lost this habit and retreated into bubbles, although people in different bubbles share most goals and differ mainly on factual questions (Perlmutter).
- Institutionalize dissent in your own organization:
  - a contrarian team argues against every major go decision (Ricks);
  - inherently bearish team members test every bear case (Hohn);
  - radical transparency, where the best managers draw out the most negative feedback (Jensen);
  - friendly competing teams that are rewarded for finding flaws (Perlmutter).
- Founders treated like gods end up in echo chambers (Matei). In a flat culture, "only the argument matters, not seniority" (Strannegård).
- Watch for skeptics who change their minds: "to me that's a signal." CTO Kevin Scott's conversion drove Microsoft's OpenAI bet (Nadella).

### 3. Protocols that protect decisions

| Protocol | How it works | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blind analysis | Hide the result until everyone agrees all error checks are done, then "open the envelope" | Perlmutter |
| Write first, then discuss | Collect independent written views before meeting to avoid herding; Sweden's court lets the youngest judge speak first and sees more dissent than Norway's | Perlmutter |
| Scenario planning | Pick two unrelated driving forces and test your position in all four extreme combinations | Perlmutter, Temasek |
| Pre-specified criteria | Define the results needed to advance before the experiment; hit them and go the next day | Ricks |
| Checklists | "Cheap? Good business? Trustworthy management? What did I miss? Why is this offered to me?" | Li Lu, Gaonkar |
| Decision journal | Record every decision to learn your reflexes and pain points | Ying |
| Written beliefs as code | State every belief and its reasoning, stress-test it and turn it into algorithms | Jensen (Bridgewater) |
| Pick sources blind | Choose which sources you trust before seeing their view on your case | Perlmutter |
| Invest, then investigate | Take a meaningful but modest starter, research for 2–3 weeks, then add or exit | Druckenmiller, Sundheim |
| Pre-mortem | For each holding, write why it underperformed five years from now | Davis |
| Deal codes | Tag every idea with its hedge, reasonable worst case and history; track ideas won versus lost | Gerber |
| Mock portfolio | Analysts submit weekly sizing on record, so reviews can't rely on memory | Sundheim |
| Written reasons at purchase | List five or six reasons when you buy; after a 50% fall, buy more only if they still hold | Watsa |
| Three-year mistake review | Review the last three years, not this year's winners and losers, and group mistakes into biases | Sandler |

### 4. Intuition and analysis, used against each other

- The unconscious recognizes patterns like a neural net, but it also sees patterns in noise. Vet intuitions with statistics, and feed intuition with long, focused effort; answers often arrive after sleep (Perlmutter).
- Magnus Carlsen intuits about three candidate moves, then analyzes them (via Tangen). Hohn calls intuition "thinking without thinking," built from experience.
- Aim for Kahneman's methodical "system 2" and use data to debias "system 1" (Gaonkar).

### 5. Humility about prediction

- Seven very smart people recorded 12-month predictions and were \~80% wrong (Temasek). Ullal trusts her own forecasts only 1–3 years out.
- Hype check from a Nobel biologist: "no illnesses in 10–15 years" ignores that treatments happen "in the analog world, in real people." Tech leaders' bias is to see life as software (Ramakrishnan).
- Demand control experiments: one-person longevity regimes and young-blood clinics have none (Ramakrishnan). Most hockey-stick backtests hide a flaw (Kantos).
- The world has always been uncertain. Civilization makes more of it manageable, and thriving means riding uncertainty rather than fearing it (Perlmutter).
- "History is the art of surprises." The Sino-Soviet split was known, yet few drew the conclusion, because concluding "outside of your historical frame of reference is extraordinary difficult" (Stahl).

### 6. Curiosity, humility and staying human

- Exposure to art and ideas you don't understand builds humility and empathy, which machines lack. Education should make people better at being human as machines get better at being machines (Strannegård).
- *Bildung* is "what remains after you forget what you learned." The FREE model asks you to be fact-based, reflective ("are my opinions my own?"), empathetic and entrepreneurial (Strannegård).
- Curiosity is contagious: a "learn-it-all" rather than "know-it-all" culture (Ricks). The best fundamental investors are insatiably curious, like the billionaire taking Python classes in his late 50s (Matei).
- Don't delegate all thinking to AI, which risks "making humanity stupider" (Ramakrishnan). AI can make you feel you've learned the basics before you have (Perlmutter).
- Youth matters: new generations don't inherit old mistakes, and great science mostly comes from people under 40 (Perlmutter, Ramakrishnan).

### 7. Practical life lessons from the scientists

- Health span beats lifespan. The proven levers are boring: don't overeat, sleep 7–8 hours (repair happens during sleep) and exercise. Anything that reduces stress, even a cold plunge you enjoy, is probably fine (Ramakrishnan).
- Persevere: Perlmutter's team expected 30 supernovae in 3 years, had zero after 3 and its first good one after 5, and still won a Nobel. Rival teams swapped telescope nights and shared the prize.
- Ignore the news's doom framing, which is designed to scare. "This is your moment" to work on planet-scale problems (Perlmutter). "Not everything is about living longer; it's about enjoying life while you have it" (Ramakrishnan).
