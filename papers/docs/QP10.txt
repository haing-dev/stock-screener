EXPECTED VOLATILITY, UNEXPECTED VOLATILITY, AND THE
CROSS-SECTION OF STOCK RETURNS

Choong Tze Chua, Jeremy Goh, and Zhe Zhang

Singapore Management University

Abstract

The existing literature finds conflicting results on the cross-sectional relation
between expected returns and idiosyncratic volatility. We contend that at the
firm level, the sample correlation between unexpected returns and expected id-
iosyncratic volatility can cloud the true relation between the expected return
and expected idiosyncratic volatility. We show strong evidence that unexpected
idiosyncratic volatility is positively related to unexpected returns. Using unex-
pected idiosyncratic volatility to control for unexpected returns, we find expected
idiosyncratic volatility to be significantly and positively related to expected re-
turns. This result holds after controlling for various firm characteristics, and it is
robust across different sample periods.

JEL Classification: G10, G11, G12

I. Introduction

Standard asset pricing theory predicts that only systematic risks should be priced.
Merton (1987), however, argues that expected idiosyncratic volatility may explain
expected stock returns if investors are underdiversified. The recent empirical ev-
idence on the relation between idiosyncratic volatility and return is mixed. 1 We
propose a new method of examining this contentious issue. First, we document that

We contend that the conflicting results in the extant literature may be due to
the lack of consistency in the choice of variables being used or problems inherent in
some commonly used variables. First, some authors who study the relation between
idiosyncratic volatility and returns employ total idiosyncratic volatility, whereas
others use expected idiosyncratic volatility. Fu (2009) explicitly addresses this dis-
parity and argues that because the objective is to examine whether idiosyncratic risk
is priced in expected returns, the theoretically relevant idiosyncratic volatility mea-
sure should be expected idiosyncratic volatility, determined ex ante. Additionally,
French, Schwert, and Stambaugh (1987) make a compelling justification, albeit
at the aggregate market level, that the use of total volatility may obscure the true
relation between market returns and market volatility. They suggest decomposing
total market volatility into expected and unexpected components. We therefore treat
the relation between expected idiosyncratic volatility and expected returns as one
of the central questions to be answered, as opposed to the relation between total
idiosyncratic volatility and expected returns.
Second, practically all authors who investigate the relation between id-
iosyncratic volatility and expected returns use realized returns as a proxy for ex-
pected returns. However, total realized returns are poor (and potentially biased)
proxies for expected returns in finite sample. At the monthly horizon, the effects
of unexpected returns often dominate those of expected returns, and the sam-
ple correlation between expected idiosyncratic volatility and unexpected returns
can cloud the true relation between expected returns and expected idiosyncratic
volatility.
Because there is no easy way to decompose total stock returns into expected
and unexpected components, we propose an alternative approach by constructing a
measure that is highly correlated with unexpected returns and use that as a control
variable. Specifically, we decompose idiosyncratic volatility into expected idiosyn-
cratic volatility (EIV ) and unexpected idiosyncratic volatility (UIV ) and use UIV
to control for variation in unexpected returns. Our approach is similar to that of
French, Schwert, and Stambaugh (1987), who decompose market volatility into
expected and unexpected components and relate both to the market return. As
discussed later, we document a strong correlation between UIV and unexpected
returns, which allows the true relation between expected returns and idiosyncratic
volatility to be better observed because the finite sample bias on the coefficient of
EIV is mitigated, and the standard error is reduced. The Appendix contains a more
detailed discussion of the related econometric mechanism.

In our decomposition approach, we first construct a measure of idiosyn-
cratic volatility for each stock at each month, as the within-month sum of squared
residuals from the daily regression of the Fama–French (1993) three-factor model.
This is in line with Ang et al. (2006), Wei and Zhang (2006), and Zhang (2004).
Then, using each stock’s time series of idiosyncratic volatility, we model the evolu-
tion of the idiosyncratic volatility as an autoregressive process (AR2). 2 The monthly
predicted idiosyncratic volatility is labeled EIV, and the residual is labeled UIV . We
then perform cross-sectional regressions of returns on EIV alone as well as on both
EIV and UIV . We find that EIV alone cannot explain stock returns. The point esti-
mate is virtually zero and statistically insignificant at the 10% level. However, when
used jointly with UIV, both EIV and UIV are positively and significantly related to
contemporaneous stock returns. The average coefficients for EIV and UIV are 0.17
and 0.828, respectively, both statistically significant at the 5% level. The results are
robust after controlling for size, book-to-market, past returns, stock exchange, and
various liquidity measures.
These results suggest that EIV and UIV jointly matter for the cross-section
of stock returns. Note that the UIV variable used in this study is the unexpected
volatility at any month t that is not in investors’ information set at month t−1;
hence, by construction it should not be related to the expected stock returns. We
deduce that the positive and significant coefficient for UIV is driven by its relation
with unexpected returns. Controlling for UIV, a positive and significant coefficient
for EIV suggests that EIV is positively related to expected stock returns. Our results
therefore show that using total returns as a proxy for expected returns clouds the
true relation between expected returns and EIV . By separating the roles of EIV and
UIV, our method provides a better approach to test this relation.
The main result of our article is consistent with Merton’s (1987) model
as well as findings from Fu (2009) and Spiegel and Wang (2005) that document
a robust positive relation between expected returns and expected volatility. Unlike
Fu and Spiegel and Wang, who apply the EGARCH model on monthly returns to
estimate expected volatility, we focus on decomposing the idiosyncratic volatility
(estimated from daily returns) into EIV and UIV . By controlling for the positive
return effect of UIV, our approach allows us to observe the true relation between
expected returns and volatility with more clarity. Our result is different from that
of Ang et al. (2006, 2009), who find a negative relation between realized returns
and lagged realized volatility. Their findings are novel and interesting, but because

To further investigate our results on the relation between UIV and stock
returns, we perform several robustness checks. Each month we construct five EIV -
stratified, UIV -sorted portfolios and record the average monthly portfolio returns,
the capital asset pricing model (CAPM) alpha, Fama–French three-factor alpha,
and Fama–French three-factor plus momentum-factor alpha. For both equal- and
value-weighted portfolios, the returns and alphas of the high-UIV portfolio are
always significantly higher than the corresponding returns and alphas of the low-
UIV portfolio.
Our main argument centers on the fact that large monthly unexpected stock
returns call into question the use of total returns as a proxy for expected returns.
The solution is to find a proxy that we can use as a control variable that is likely
to be highly correlated with unexpected returns and uncorrelated with expected
returns. There are both theoretical and empirical reasons why UIV will perform
well as a control variable for unexpected returns. Merton (1974) posits that we can
view common stocks as call options on the assets of the firm. A positive shock
to the idiosyncratic volatility (UIV ) of the stock implies that the volatility of the
firm’s assets has increased and, consequently, that the call option (the common
stock) should increase in value as well, hence, the increase in the contemporaneous
stock price and the unexpected return (henceforth termed “the option effect”). The
positive empirical relation between UIV and unexpected returns observed here is
therefore consistent with the option effect of Merton. In addition, recent empirical
studies such as French, Schwert, and Stambaugh (1987), Jiang and Lee (2006),
and Ang et al. (2006) find that innovations in volatilities, albeit aggregated over
all stocks, are useful in the study of stock returns. We complement the literature
by showing that individual stock’s innovation in idiosyncratic volatility is also
important in studying the underlying stock’s returns.
As mentioned earlier, our choice of using UIV to control for unexpected
returns is theoretically motivated by Merton’s (1974) idea of viewing equity as a call
option on the firm’s assets. The option effect also implies that the observed relation
should be stronger for firms with higher financial leverage because the equity of
these firms are more option-like. To further substantiate the validity of Merton’s
theory, we sort firms into five quintiles based on their leverage. Our results show
that the relation between UIV and returns is positive for firms in all five quintiles
and the relation gets monotonically stronger as leverage increases. 3 We further

II. Data and the Construction of Volatility Measures

Data

We obtain daily and monthly stock returns from the Center for Research in Security
Prices (CRSP). The sample period is from January 1963 to December 2003. All
common stocks (share codes 10 and 11) traded at NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ are
included. In later sections we also use data on firm characteristics such as size, book-
to-market ratio, and financial leverage. These characteristics are constructed from
the Compustat annual file and we merge them with the return data. Specifically,
we define the book-to-market ratio every month as the ratio between the previous
year’s ending book value of common equity (item 60) and the current month’s
market capitalization. Financial leverage is defined as the book value of total debt
(item 181) divided by the sum of the book value of debt, other equity, and the
market value of common equity (previous year-end total assets (item 6) – previous
year-end common equity + current month market capitalization). We exclude firms
with negative book-to-market ratio. Following the literature, we exclude financial
firms (Standard Industrial Classification [SIC] codes 6000–6999) and utility firms
(SIC codes 4900–4999) whenever we need leverage information. We also exclude
firms whose leverage ratio is outside the range of [0,1]. We obtain the daily and
monthly risk-free rate and Fama–French three-factor returns from 1963 to 2003
from Kenneth French’s Web site. 5

should have little relation with expected returns. This implies that the positive and
significant coefficient for UIV must be largely driven by its relation with unexpected
returns. Therefore, UIV serves as a control variable by controlling for the effects of
unexpected returns. Consequently, the coefficient for EIV now reflects the positive

The significant coefficient for UIV is neither a statistical artifact nor a direct
product of the construction of the study. A high UIV implies that the realized IV for
the month is unexpectedly high. This does not automatically mean that the return
for the month should be unexpectedly high as well. In contrast, an unexpectedly low
(negative) return will also generate a high UIV statistic. Therefore, a priori, there is
no reason to believe that high UIV s should be contemporaneously associated with
high returns. 8

Control Variables and Size Quintiles

It is well documented that size and the book-to-market ratio have explanatory power
for the cross-sectional returns (see Fama and French 1992). To check the robustness
of our results, we use these two characteristics as additional explanatory variables.
We also add previous one-year return (returns from previous 12 to 2 months ago)
to control for the momentum effect (see Jegadeesh and Titman 1993). It is possible
that market microstructure issues for small and illiquid stocks may drive the results.
Thus, we conduct two additional robustness tests. In one test, for every month we
sort all stocks in our sample into five size quintiles based on the previous month’s
market capitalization and exclude the smallest quintile from the sample. In another
test, we exclude NASDAQ stocks entirely from our sample. We then repeat the
cross-sectional regression analysis. The results for both tests together with the full-
sample results are reported in Table 3. For the full sample, the average coefficient
estimates for EIV and UIV are 0.43 and 1.01, respectively, after controlling for other
firm characteristics. Both estimates are statistically significant at the 1% level. The
results for the two subsamples are similar. The coefficients for both EIV and UIV
remain statistically significant after controlling for firm-specific characteristics and
are stable across different samples. Firm size is negatively related to returns after
controlling for the volatility effects, suggesting that the size effect may be partially

Further Robustness Checks: The Effects of Liquidity

So far, we show a positive relation between expected (unexpected) return and EIV
(UIV ). One possibility is that our results are partially driven by the liquidity effect.
The literature documents negative correlation between stock return and liquid-
ity measures at both the market and individual stock levels (see, e.g., Amihud
and Mendelson 1986; Brennan and Subrahmanyam 1996; Amihud 2002; Nguyen
et al. 2007). It may be possible that firms with high idiosyncratic volatility are less
liquid (e.g., high bid–ask spread may lead to spurious high volatility) and hence
command a higher premium. The relation between cotemporaneous return and UIV

IV. Unexpected Idiosyncratic Volatility

This study is among the first to examine the role of firm-level (unaggregated)
UIV in explaining cross-sectional variation in stock returns. Because the previous
section documents, empirically, a positive and highly significant relation between
UIV and stock returns, the results merit closer examination. We do this through two
investigations. First, we construct UIV -sorted portfolios and observe how returns
and alphas vary across the different portfolios. Second, we test the option effect by
observing how the relation between UIV and returns change as the leverage of the
firm changes.

Linkage between UIV and Stock Returns: Testing the Option Effect

Merton (1974) views equity as a call option on the firm’s assets; hence, an (unex-
pected) increase in the volatility should result in a higher value for the call option.
The more highly leveraged the firm is, the more option-like the equity will be. The
option effect further predicts that the positive relation between UIV and returns
should be stronger for high-leverage vis-à-vis low-leverage firms. Our results in
both this and the previous section corroborate the prediction that higher UIV is
associated with higher returns.
To test the proposition that high-leverage firms experience a stronger re-
lation between UIV and returns than low-leverage firms, we first divide the firms

demeaned UIV by the time-series standard deviation of the UIV for that firm. This
results not only in equal variances across the five quintiles but also in equal uncon-
ditional variances for the UIV variable for all firms in all months. The standardized

It is worth noting that the option effect of UIV on contemporaneous stock
price does not lead to a negative relation between expected volatility and future
expected stock returns. In the Merton (1974) framework, the expected return on
a firm’s equity depends on both the risk premium on the underlying asset (total
firm value) and the sensitivity of the equity value to the change in the value of
the underlying total asset (equity beta). If the premium on the total assets of the
firm remains constant, it can be shown that an increase in current volatility of
the firm leads to lower equity beta and, in turn, to lower expected future return on
firm equity (see Johnson 2004). Our empirical evidence, however, suggests that
the assumption of a constant premium on a firm’s total assets may not be valid.
The significant positive coefficient for EIV suggests that idiosyncratic volatility
has a positive effect on the premium of the firm’s total assets, and this effect
dominates the negative effect of volatility on equity beta. As a result, we empirically
observe a positive relation between expected stock (equity) returns and expected
volatility.

V. Conclusion

We reexamine whether idiosyncratic volatility is related to the cross-section of stock
returns. To better examine the relation between the expected return and expected
volatility, we decompose volatility into expected and unexpected volatility, and use
unexpected volatility to control for the noise caused by unexpected returns.
We find strong evidence of a positive relation between UIV and contem-
poraneous returns. Employing unexpected volatility as a control variable, we find
a significant positive relation between stock returns and EIV . The interpretation
is that both EIV and UIV are important in explaining stock returns. Specifically,
EIV is positively related to expected stock returns and UIV is positively related
to unexpected stock returns. Excluding UIV from the analysis leads to incorrect
conclusions about the role of expected idiosyncratic volatilities. We include several
robustness checks to ensure that our results are not due to characteristics such as
size, book-to-market, past returns, liquidity, or choice of sample periods.
Our study is related to several recent articles on the relation between id-
iosyncratic volatility and the cross-section of stock returns. Ang et al. (2006, 2009)
document a negative relation between monthly realized returns and one-month
lagged realized idiosyncratic volatility. Because Ang et al. do not test the relation
between the expected return and expected volatility (Ang et al. 2009), their finding
is not necessarily comparable to ours. However, our results are consistent with Fu
(2009) and Spiegel and Wang (2005) in that we, too, find a robust positive relation
between expected return and expected volatility. Whereas Fu and Spiegel and Wang
emphasize the importance of using of the EGARCH model on monthly stock re-
turns in estimating the monthly volatility, we argue that the difference comes from
the decomposition of the idiosyncratic volatility (estimated from daily returns) into
EIV and UIV, and then using UIV to control for unexpected returns. In addition, we
provide strong empirical evidence that UIV is positively related to the unexpected
return, in the way predicted by Merton’s (1974) theoretical model.
To further investigate the importance and role of UIV in explaining cross-
sectional stock returns, we perform two additional tests. First, we construct five
value-weighted portfolios that are EIV stratified and UIV sorted. We show that
for both types of portfolios, the average returns, as well as the Jensen’s alphas
(over a wide range of models), increase monotonically as we move from a low-UIV
portfolio to a high-UIV portfolio. Second, we investigate the validity of the option
effect. We group firms into five leverage quintiles. We then standardize UIV and
show that the magnitude of the regression coefficients for the standardized UIV
increases monotonically as leverage increases. This leads us to conclude that UIV
is indeed important, and this importance stems from the consequences of the option
effect.

We focus on the relation between expected returns (ER) and expected idiosyncratic
volatility (EIV ). Ideally, this can be discovered using the following regression:

ER i,m = ξ 0 + ξ 1 EIV i,m + υ i,m

(A1)

for each stock i and month m.
Unfortunately, we cannot observe, nor is there a reasonably accurate model
to derive, ER i,m . The extant literature therefore uses realized returns (R), which
comprise ER and unexpected returns (UR), in the following regression to obtain a
proxy estimate for ξ 1 :

R i,m = ξ o + ξ 1 EIV i,m + υ i,m

ER i,m + UR i,m = ξ o + ξ 1 EIV i,m + υ i,m .

(A2)

However, because the noise in UR i,m dominates ER i,m (these are monthly
returns for individual stocks), the standard error for the estimate of ξ 1 is very
large. Furthermore, if there is any sample correlation between UR i,m and EIV i,m , ξ 1
would be a biased estimator of ξ 1 . Specifically, if the correlation in that sample is
negative, the estimate of ξ 1 would be biased downward. This poses two problems for
investigators. First, the potential bias in the estimate may result in wrong conclusions
being made. Second, the large standard error for the estimate of ξ 1 means that even
if there is a true relation, the invariably low t-statistic may mask the statistical
significance of that fact.
Because of the lack of tools available to remove undesirable effects of
UR i,m in the left-hand side of the regression, we propose that a control variable be
inserted into the right-hand side of the regression. Suppose we can find a variable
that is perfectly correlated with UR i,m (call this variable X i,m ). We can then have
the following regression:

ER i,m + UR i,m = ξ o + ξ 1 EIV i,m + ξ 2 X i,m + υ i,m .

(A3)

Because X i,m and UR i,m are perfectly correlated, the term ξ 2 X i,m will
capture the effects of UR i,m and allow the underlying relation between ER i,m and

As we discuss in the introduction, we motivate our choice of control variable
for unexpected returns from Merton (1974). One can view equity as a call option on
the firm’s assets. If the volatility of the firm’s asset value unexpectedly increases, the
option value (equity price) will increase and, hence, so will the contemporaneous
return. Given that the nature of the shock is unexpected, we expect a positive relation
between UIV and contemporaneous unexpected returns. This makes UIV a natural
candidate for controlling unexpected returns. As defined in Section II, UIV is the
unexpected innovation in volatility from firm-level time-series estimation. It need
not necessarily be orthogonal to EIV in the cross-section. Bear in mind that the
overriding concern is finding a variable that is highly correlated with UR i,m . It is to
be expected that this control variable has a nonzero sample correlation with EIV i,m
because UR i,m itself has a nonzero sample correlation with EIV i,m . Furthermore,
the nonzero correlation between UIV and EIV i,m moves the estimate ξ 1 closer to
ξ 1 compared with ξ 1 .
